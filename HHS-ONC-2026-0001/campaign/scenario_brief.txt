This campaign will oppose the use of AI in healthcare in the broadest sense using wide-ranging arguments involving depersonalization, safety, effectiveness, impact on employment, loss of trust, rearrangement of power and control, economic, and other arguments. HHS and ONC should do everything in their power to slow or stop the use of AI in clinical care and insurance decisions, even administrative functions that are performed by humans who are trained in the human context that AI will ignore. 

A serious case against AI in healthcare starts with the fact that medicine is not just an information problem. It is a human relationship built on judgment, context, accountability, and trust. Patients are often scared, confused, in pain, or unable to explain themselves clearly, and clinicians are not simply matching patterns but interpreting stories, body language, family dynamics, and ambiguity. AI can create the illusion that care is becoming more objective and efficient when in fact it may push medicine toward a colder, more transactional model in which the patient becomes a data point and the clinician becomes an overseer of machine recommendations. That shift could erode one of the most important parts of healthcare: the sense that another human being is truly responsible for your care and answerable for mistakes.

There is also a powerful safety and effectiveness argument against relying on AI in clinical settings. Healthcare is full of edge cases, incomplete records, biased historical data, rare conditions, and rapidly changing circumstances. A system that performs well on average can still fail catastrophically for the wrong patient at the wrong moment. AI tools may inherit racial, socioeconomic, and institutional biases from the data used to train them, and those biases can be harder to detect precisely because the systems often appear authoritative. Even when an AI system is technically accurate, it may not be clinically useful, and even when it is useful, it may be used outside the setting in which it was validated. In practice, this means healthcare organizations may deploy systems that are impressive in demonstrations but brittle in real life, adding hidden risk while making people feel falsely reassured.

The social and economic objections are just as strong. AI may be sold as a way to reduce clinician burnout, but it can just as easily become a mechanism for surveillance, standardization, and cost cutting. Hospitals, insurers, and vendors may use AI not to improve care but to squeeze more productivity out of workers, justify staffing cuts, automate denials, or shift decision-making upward into opaque systems that frontline professionals cannot challenge. That threatens employment in some roles, devalues expertise in others, and may leave healthcare workers feeling that their judgment is being replaced by tools they do not control. Patients, meanwhile, may reasonably worry that “AI-powered care” is really a euphemism for cheaper care, thinner staffing, and less human attention, especially for the elderly, the poor, the disabled, and other vulnerable groups who already struggle to get equitable treatment.

Finally, there is a political and moral argument against normalizing AI in healthcare: once these systems are embedded, they become hard to question, hard to audit, and hard to reverse. Vendors gain influence, institutions become dependent, and responsibility becomes diffuse. When something goes wrong, the clinician can blame the tool, the hospital can blame the vendor, and the vendor can blame the data or the user. That diffusion of responsibility is dangerous in a field where errors can maim, bankrupt, or kill. A society can decide that some domains should remain stubbornly human, slower, and more accountable, even at the cost of some efficiency. From that perspective, opposition to AI in healthcare is not anti-technology at all; it is a defense of dignity, professional responsibility, democratic oversight, and the belief that medicine should be governed first by care rather than computation.
