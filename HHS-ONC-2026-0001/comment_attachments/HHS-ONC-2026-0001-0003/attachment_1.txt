Executive Summary
The RFI correctly identifies that clinical AI adoption is constrained less by model capability than
by whether care settings can reliably supply the longitudinal, high-fidelity context needed for
safe inference. This comment proposes a structural solution: patient-designated longitudinal
routing by default.
Core Proposal: Enable a one-time patient designation of a certified longitudinal endpoint to
which full-fidelity USCDI data is automatically delivered as it is created, across care settings.
This is not a consumer portal strategy. It is a convergence layer that makes complete longitudinal
records reliably available back to care delivery systems (with consent) where AI actually runs.
Why This Matters for AI: Clinical AI fails when deployed into informationally impoverished
workflows. Patient-designated routing changes the default so that longitudinal completeness
becomes the expected input condition for AI, rather than a special-case integration achievement.
Key Recommendations:
1. Regulation: Define and certify patient-designated longitudinal endpoints with strict
security, consent, provenance, and audit requirements.
2. Reimbursement: Pilot payment models tied to verified delivery rather than capability
attestations.
3. R&D: Fund evaluations that connect longitudinal completeness improvements to clinical
outcomes, workflow impact, and AI safety/bias monitoring.
This is a narrow structural change with outsized impact: it improves care coordination
immediately, reduces preventable harm, and creates the data conditions necessary for safe,
effective, and equitable clinical AI.
1. Introduction: Why I'm Commenting / Relevant Experience

I am submitting this comment because the RFI correctly identifies a core problem: clinical AI
adoption is constrained less by model capability than by whether the care setting can reliably
supply the longitudinal, high-fidelity context needed for safe and clinically useful inference.
My perspective comes from building and operating clinical data infrastructure in real healthcare
delivery environments, including clinical data warehouses, integration pipelines, and
point-of-care systems used by clinicians under real time constraints. In those settings, the central
failure mode is not "no standards exist," but "the record is incomplete when and where clinical
decisions are made." AI embedded into workflows inherits that incompleteness, which directly
degrades safety, quality, and equity.
2. Background: The Problem the RFI is Trying to Solve
2.1 Fragmentation, not standards maturity, is the binding constraint
Despite FHIR/USCDI maturity, national exchange efforts, and the shift toward API-based
access, longitudinal aggregation remains the exception rather than the norm. The operational
reality is that records are frequently split across hospitals, specialist groups, labs, urgent care,
imaging centers, behavioral health providers, and post-acute settings. Clinicians often have an
incomplete view of diagnoses, medications, prior workups, allergies, procedures, problem lists,
and results performed elsewhere.
For clinical AI, this is not a "nice-to-have." Longitudinal completeness is the substrate for:
accurate clinical context and risk stratification; reduction of false positives/false negatives that
arise from missing history; bias mitigation (because missingness is not random and often
correlates with socioeconomic access patterns); and trustworthy post-deployment monitoring
(drift, subgroup performance, safety event correlation).
2.2 Why fragmentation persists: routing control is mis-assigned
The underlying structural issue is that routing decisions are typically controlled by institutions
and vendors, not by patients. Even when standards and exchange exist, the default routing
behavior tends to produce episodic exchange, partial aggregation, and brittle "islands" of data
that do not converge into a reliable longitudinal record accessible at the point of care.
Stated differently: interoperability capability does not guarantee longitudinal completeness,
because the system's routing defaults do not reliably cause data to converge.
3. Core Proposal: Patient-Designated Longitudinal Routing by Default
3.1 One structural change
HHS can unlock meaningful clinical AI adoption by enabling patient-designated routing by
default: a one-time patient designation of a certified longitudinal endpoint to which full-fidelity
USCDI data is automatically delivered as it is created, across care settings.
Key properties:

4. One-time designation (like choosing a primary pharmacy or selecting a health plan PCP)
5. Automatic delivery of standardized clinical payloads (USCDI-aligned) as events occur
6. Persistent routing via a record-locator mechanism so the designation "follows" the
patient
7. No patient management burden (no requirement that patients log in, curate, or actively
manage data)
8. Return-to-care enablement: treating systems can retrieve a current longitudinal view
with patient consent, supporting clinician workflow and embedded AI
3.2 What this is not
This is not "a consumer portal strategy" and not a burden-shift to patients. The endpoint is not
primarily for human browsing or personal recordkeeping. It is a convergence layer intended to
make a complete, current longitudinal record reliably available back to care delivery systems
(with consent) where AI actually runs.
3.3 Why this solves the AI adoption bottleneck
Clinical AI fails in practice when deployed into workflows that are informationally
impoverished. Patient-designated routing changes the default so that longitudinal completeness
becomes the expected input condition for AI, rather than a special-case integration
achievement.
This is the smallest structural shift that: improves patient safety and care coordination
immediately (even without AI); creates the precondition for AI systems to be reliable and
monitorable; and aligns incentives in a way enforcement alone has not achieved.
4. Analysis: Impacts, Unintended Consequences, and Why HHS Should Prefer
This Approach
4.1 Benefits to clinical care (even before AI)
A persistent longitudinal record reduces: duplicated labs and imaging ordered because prior
results are unavailable; medication reconciliation errors (a major source of preventable harm);
delays in diagnosis due to missing prior workup and specialist notes; avoidable
admissions/readmissions driven by incomplete outpatient history; and clinician time lost hunting
for records across portals and faxes. These improvements are direct clinical quality benefits that
also improve AI readiness.
4.2 Why incentives matter more than capability attestations
Interoperability has often been framed as "build capability" + "enforce compliance." That
approach does not reliably create longitudinal completeness because it does not make
completeness an operational default that organizations are paid to produce.

If reimbursement is conditioned on verified delivery to a patient-designated endpoint (not mere
"capability to deliver"), interoperability shifts from an enforcement problem to an operational
norm. This is a durable behavioral change lever.
4.3 Distributional impacts (equity)
Fragmentation and missingness are not evenly distributed. People who receive care across
multiple systems (often due to access patterns, insurance churn, transportation constraints, or
regional provider availability) are disproportionately harmed by non-convergent records. These
same populations are at higher risk of AI underperformance when missingness becomes a hidden
input variable correlated with disadvantage.
Patient-designated routing improves equity by making longitudinal completeness a default
expectation across sites rather than a privilege of being "in the right health system."
4.4 Semantic interoperability is a real risk—and a necessary forcing function
Implementing default longitudinal routing will reveal known weaknesses in semantic
consistency: coding variation, normalization drift, incomplete reconciliation of problem lists, and
differing local interpretations. These issues exist today; episodic exchange often masks them.
This should be treated as a forcing function rather than a reason to avoid reform. AI systems
amplify semantic inconsistency risks; therefore, operationalizing longitudinal aggregation is the
mechanism that makes semantic quality measurable, improvable, and tied to downstream
usability.
4.5 Privacy, security, and trust: the "central objection" and how to mitigate it
A convergence endpoint increases the perceived "blast radius" of a breach if designed poorly.
That concern is legitimate and should be addressed explicitly in the program design.
Mitigations that can be required through certification and policy guardrails:
• Consent + revocation: simple, durable patient consent workflows; immediate revocation
capability
• Strong provenance + auditability: comprehensive audit logs; patient-accessible "who
accessed what and when"
• Minimum necessary retrieval: retrieval into treating systems should be scoped;
discourage uncontrolled bulk retrieval except when clinically justified
• Segmentation & sensitive domains: phased inclusion for highly sensitive categories;
require clear labeling and provenance for sensitive information
• Security baseline: modern authentication/authorization, encryption in transit/at rest,
continuous monitoring, and incident response controls commensurate with handling
longitudinal clinical data
A default routing policy must be paired with a certification program that makes "safe
aggregation" non-negotiable.

5. Recommended HHS Actions Using the RFI Levers
(Explicitly aligned to the RFI's emphasis on regulation, reimbursement, and R&D.)
5.1 Regulation: Define and certify "longitudinal endpoints" and routing behavior
HHS/ASTP/ONC should define certification criteria for longitudinal endpoints and for EHR
behaviors required to support patient designation and delivery.
Certification should require longitudinal endpoints to:
9. Accept complete USCDI-aligned payloads (full-fidelity, not lossy summaries)
10. Support standardized query and return to treating systems with patient consent
11. Provide robust provenance, versioning, and audit logs
12. Enforce strong security controls and clear operational responsibilities
13. Implement consent and revocation mechanisms appropriate for clinical workflows
14. Support resilience and availability expectations appropriate for point-of-care retrieval
Certification should require sending systems to:
15. Support a patient-designated routing instruction
16. Automatically deliver standardized payloads to that endpoint (subject to consent and
policy)
17. Support reliable event-based update flows (not just occasional exports)
18. Provide delivery verification (receipt + integrity checks)
This does not require redefining USCDI or replacing FHIR; it changes the default routing
behavior and makes longitudinal convergence reliable.
5.2 Persistent routing: record locator mechanism
HHS should establish or enable a persistent routing pointer (record-locator-like) so a patient's
designation is discoverable across care settings. That pointer should be patient-controlled,
privacy-preserving, and designed to avoid enabling unauthorized tracking.
Conceptually: the routing pointer says "where the longitudinal record lives," not "what is in it."
Access to the content remains consent-governed and auditable.
5.3 Reimbursement: tie payment to verified delivery and usability (critical)
Regulation alone will not produce durable change. HHS should pilot reimbursement models
where the measure is successful delivery (and, over time, usability) rather than capability
attestation.
Potential models:
• Infrastructure payments for certified longitudinal endpoints with pricing guardrails

• Quality / value-based measures tied to verified delivery performance (completeness +
latency)
• Incentives for treating systems that demonstrate retrieval use in workflows (without
penalizing patients)
CMS can directly influence Medicare/Medicaid. Commercial alignment can be approached via
demonstrations that show ROI and reduced waste through fewer duplicates and improved
coordination.
5.4 R&D and pilots: fund implementation science, not just model research
HHS should sponsor pilots that evaluate:
• Completeness: USCDI coverage over time vs expected sources
• Latency: time from data creation to endpoint availability
• Clinical outcomes proxies: duplicate test reduction, medication discrepancy reduction,
preventable adverse events
• Operational burden: net clinician time saved vs added workflows
• AI safety and bias: subgroup performance changes when longitudinal completeness
improves
• Total cost of ownership: endpoint ops, integration costs, and security compliance
Study design suggestion: stepped-wedge rollout across sites or regions to enable causal
inference without requiring all participants to switch simultaneously.
6. Addressing Likely Counterarguments
Counterargument A: "This is too hard / too expensive"
The current model externalizes costs into duplicated testing, preventable errors, and
administrative burden (fax, portal chasing, manual reconciliation). Patient-designated routing
consolidates infrastructure investment into a measurable, reusable utility. By tying
reimbursement to verified delivery and using certification to reduce bespoke integrations, the
system becomes cheaper over time than the current patchwork of one-off interfaces.
Counterargument B: "Patients won't participate"
Patients already make durable healthcare designations (insurance plans, pharmacies, PCPs,
consent forms). The program can be designed so designation occurs at registration, enrollment,
or during standard administrative encounters, with minimal friction. Critically: patients do not
need to "use an app" for this to work.
Counterargument C: "Privacy risk is unacceptable"
The risk is unacceptable only if the endpoint lacks rigorous controls. This is precisely why
certification, auditability, consent, and security baselines must be central requirements. A poorly
secured fragmented environment is not inherently safer than a well-governed convergence
model; it is simply less visible and less accountable.

7. Specific Requests to HHS (Recap as Actionable Items)
19. Define and certify patient-designated longitudinal endpoints with strict security, consent,
provenance, and audit requirements
20. Require support in certified health IT for patient designation and automated delivery
of standardized USCDI-aligned data to the designated endpoint
21. Establish a persistent routing pointer (record-locator-like) so designations follow
patients across care settings
22. Pilot reimbursement models tied to verified delivery (and later, usability) rather than
capability attestations
23. Fund evaluations that connect longitudinal completeness improvements to clinical
outcomes, workflow impact, and AI safety/bias monitoring
8. Conclusion
Clinical AI cannot reliably succeed without longitudinal completeness available in real
workflows. The most direct way to achieve that completeness is to transfer routing control—by
default—from institutions to patients through a one-time designation of a certified longitudinal
endpoint, paired with persistent routing and strong consent/audit safeguards.
This is a narrow structural change with outsized impact: it improves care coordination
immediately, reduces preventable harm, and creates the data conditions necessary for safe,
effective, and equitable clinical AI.
