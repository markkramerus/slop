Dear Mr. Posnack: In response to HHS’s request for information (RFI) concerning suggestions
for Accelerating the Adoption and use of AI as part of clinical care,1 I write to suggest that HHS
further emphasize the ethical aspects of AI in its work addressing AI in clinical care, including
involving HHS and other federal bioethics staff and advisory committees, collaborating with
non-federal ethics and bioethics centers, departments and institutes, and potentially supporting
restoration of a Presidential-Level commission on bioethics to discuss AI and other topics.
As one author defines it, “AI ethics is a set of values, principles, and techniques that employ
widely accepted standards of right and wrong to guide moral conduct in the development and use
of AI technologies.”2 Ethical issues with respect to AI in clinical care may arise with respect to
data ownership, privacy, data sharing and potential (re)identification of individual patients. Some
commenters have expressed concerns about ethical implications associated with: potential use of
biased tools and algorithms; autonomous AI systems; governance and oversight of AI systems;
access to the benefits of AI (both for US-based and international/lower-middle income nation
populations, researchers and students); data and AI tool/system ownership; how use of AI will be
acknowledged (e.g., as in authorship and obtaining patient informed consent); billing and
reimbursement for use of AI tools/services; impacts of AI on jobs and provider-patient
relationships; long-term evaluation of AI impacts and potential dual uses/misuses of AI systems.

pediatrics) may require additional scrutiny.5 Yet, while not without challenges, AI also may do

The National Artificial Intelligence Research Resource (NAIRR), now under the auspices of the
National Science Foundation, emphasized ethical issues in its 2022 interim report and 2023 final
report which called for the creation of an ethics advisory board to guide AI research.7 NAIRR
commented in its interim report that “[t]o earn and maintain public trust, research in areas that
may impact privacy, civil rights, or civil liberties will need to be reviewed, approved, and
performed in a way that meets the expectations of civil society and protects subjects' rights.” In
its final report, NAIRR recommended developing an ethics advisory board to provide advice to
NAIRR and NSF on “issues of ethics, fairness, bias, accessibility, and AI risks and blindspots.”
To support an ethical component to its AI programs, HHS could include NIH’s bioethics staff
and others who have expertise and use its advisory committees to discuss and obtain input about
the ethical and moral implications of AI topics.8 HHS also can collaborate with academic and
nonprofit ethics and bioethics centers, institutes and departments.9 In an ideal world, private
companies, academic institutions, faith-based entities and other nonprofits will join HHS in
implementing their own ethical programs and advisory boards focused on AI and clinical care
issues. Toward this end, HHS could consider supporting restoration of the Presidential Bioethics
Commission that historically have helped research and set policy on bioethics topics.10
Historically, these commissions, which existed prior to 2017 under several Administrations, have
researched and made recommendations on such topics as access to care, cloning, whole genome
sequencing, neuroscience, synthetic biology, newborn screening, clinical research,
pandemics/emergency preparedness topics and aging/caregiving. With adequate staff and
funding, a new Presidential bioethics commission would well-positioned to examine and make
recommendations relating to the ethical aspects of AI and its use(s) in clinical care as discussed
in this RFI.
