Comment on HHS-ONC-2026-0001
Governing Clinical Artificial Intelligence as a Population-Level
Submitted by: Dr. Akshaya Bhagavathula, PharmD, PhD, FACE
Position: Associate Professor of Epidemiology, North Dakota State University
Expertise: Digital Epidemiology, Real-World Evidence, and Population level AI Governance
Clinical artificial intelligence (AI) is no longer a discrete technology to be evaluated solely at
deployment, but a population-level intervention whose safety, effectiveness, and equity can only
be governed through continuous, post-deployment evaluation and accountability. I appreciate
the opportunity to comment on HHS-ONC-2026-0001 and submit this response as an
epidemiologist with expertise in artificial intelligence, causal inference, and population health,
informed by direct experience evaluating AI systems after they have been deployed in clinical
and public-sector environments. My intent is not to argue for or against particular technologies,
but to address a foundational regulatory issue that underlies many of the concerns raised in this
Request for Information: the growing mismatch between how clinical AI is currently regulated
and how it actually behaves once implemented at scale. This comment responds directly to the
Department’s focus on improving the evaluation, governance, and patient-centered use of AI in
health and public health, with particular attention to how AI systems should be assessed beyond
pre-deployment testing, how regulatory oversight can remain effective as AI scales and adapts in
real-world settings, and how patient-centered innovation can be operationalized as a concrete
governance standard rather than an aspirational principle. The observations and
recommendations that follow are intended to support HHS in advancing governance approaches
that are scientifically grounded, practically implementable, and aligned with the realities of AI
deployment in clinical and public-sector systems.
AI is no longer experimental within health care. It is embedded within clinical decision support,
utilization management, documentation, triage, and surveillance systems. As a result, the
dominant risks associated with AI are no longer confined to algorithm design or pre-deployment
performance. They emerge after deployment, through interaction with clinicians, workflows,
institutions, and populations. This shift has profound implications for how AI should be
evaluated and governed.
At present, clinical AI is largely regulated as a discrete product. In practice, it functions as a
population-level intervention.
Once introduced into a health system, an AI system does not simply generate outputs. It reshapes
how decisions are made, how attention is allocated, how workflows evolve, and how care is
delivered across groups of patients. These effects accumulate over time and extend beyond the
individuals directly targeted by the algorithm. From a regulatory standpoint, this distinction is
critical, because the most consequential harms and benefits of AI are not immediate or isolated.
They are systemic.

The operational reality of deployed AI can be represented as a causal sequence rather than a static
endpoint:
Current regulatory frameworks largely evaluate AI at the point of deployment, near the top of
this sequence. However, the majority of risk emerges downstream, where no systematic oversight
currently exists.
Pre-deployment evaluation, while necessary, is structurally insufficient. Validation studies
answer whether an algorithm can perform under specified conditions, but they do not answer
whether the system remains safe, effective, or equitable once it reshapes real-world clinical
behavior. Questions such as who is most frequently exposed to AI-mediated decisions, how
reliance on AI changes clinician judgment, how performance drifts over time, or how outcomes
differ across subpopulations cannot be resolved before deployment. They require observation in
practice.
This limitation is not theoretical. In one evaluation I conducted, an AI model demonstrated strong
pre-deployment performance across conventional metrics. After implementation, clinicians
increasingly relied on the model for triage decisions. High-risk patients were identified more
efficiently, but moderate-risk patients experienced longer delays. No alerts were triggered. No
technical failures were logged. The model behaved exactly as designed. The harm emerged
through interaction with workflow and human adaptation, not through algorithmic error.

Because no post-deployment monitoring was required, the effect remained invisible until
outcome disparities became apparent months later.
From an epidemiologic perspective, this pattern is familiar. Deployed AI exhibits properties that
closely mirror population-level exposures. It has intensity, reflecting how frequently it influences
decisions. It has latency, with effects that manifest downstream. It demonstrates effect
modification, producing different impacts across demographic, clinical, and social groups. It
generates spillover effects that influence patients and clinicians not directly targeted. Its risk
profile changes over time as data, workflows, and institutional practices evolve.
Yet unlike other population-level interventions, AI is not subject to routine post-deployment
surveillance.
This gap represents a regulatory science failure rather than a technological one. HHS has long
recognized that interventions with diffuse, delayed, or heterogeneous effects cannot be governed
through one-time approval alone. Pharmacovigilance, device post-market surveillance, and
public health monitoring systems all exist because pre-market evidence is insufficient to ensure
safety at scale. Clinical AI currently sits outside this paradigm.
To address this gap, I recommend that HHS adopt Post-Deployment Algorithmic Surveillance
(PDAS) as a cross-agency framework for governing clinical AI. PDAS reflects a simple principle:
no AI system influencing care should be presumed safe or effective based solely on pre-
deployment evidence.

Under PDAS, evaluation shifts from a static, product-based model to a dynamic, population-
based model. This shift can be illustrated as follows:
The first element of PDAS is exposure characterization. Without knowing who is exposed to AI-
mediated decisions, how often, and in what contexts, no causal assessment is possible. Exposure
measurement is foundational, yet it is rarely required.
The second element is continuous population-level performance monitoring. AI systems must be
evaluated longitudinally, not episodically. Calibration, discrimination, and error patterns should
be tracked over time, with mandatory stratification by key subgroups. Performance drift should
be treated as a safety signal, not a technical footnote. In another deployment I reviewed, a model’s
performance gradually degraded due to changes in coding practices within the electronic health
record. Because no routine monitoring was in place, disparities widened quietly over a year. The
model did not fail. Oversight did.
The third element is outcome-linked evaluation. After deployment, accuracy alone is not an
adequate metric of success. The relevant question becomes whether AI use improves downstream
clinical outcomes, access, or utilization patterns. Surrogate metrics that are acceptable during
development become insufficient once systems influence care at scale.
The fourth element is structured reporting of algorithmic harms. Many AI-related harms do not
resemble traditional adverse events. They include delayed care, silent misclassification, workflow
distortions, and disparity amplification. These events rarely trigger complaints or error reports,
yet they accumulate into population-level harm. PDAS requires mechanisms to capture these
signals systematically.
The fifth element is predefined regulatory response pathways. Surveillance without action is
performative. PDAS requires clarity regarding when recalibration, restriction, suspension, or
withdrawal is warranted, and how those actions are documented and enforced.

Patient-centered innovation must be understood within this same framework. Patient-
centeredness is often treated as an ethical aspiration or design preference. From a safety
perspective, this framing is inadequate. An AI system that influences care but cannot be
explained, contested, or reviewed is not merely non-patient-centered; it is unsafe. I have
encountered cases in which AI-driven utilization decisions delayed care, clinicians could not
explain the rationale, patients had no appeal pathway, and accountability was diffuse. Technical
accuracy was irrelevant. The failure was one of governance. Patient contestability and human
accountability must be treated as regulatory requirements, not optional features.
Equity considerations further reinforce the need for post-deployment surveillance. Disparities
associated with AI are frequently attributed to biased training data, but many inequities emerge
only after implementation. Uneven adoption, differential trust, resource constraints, and context-
specific failures all contribute to disparate impact over time. Equity cannot be ensured through
design alone. It must be measured continuously. PDAS should therefore include equity-specific
monitoring requirements for AI deployed in Medicaid, maternal health, rural care, and public
health settings.
Adaptive and continuously learning AI systems introduce additional complexity. Static approval
models are incoherent when systems are designed to evolve. Regulation must shift toward
process-based accountability, with predefined learning boundaries, rollback mechanisms,
transparent change logs, and independent auditability. Without these safeguards, adaptation
becomes experimentation embedded within routine care.
Finally, none of this is feasible without interoperability. Surveillance, auditability, contestability,
and equity monitoring all depend on the ability to access and integrate data across systems.

Vendor opacity and lock-in are not merely market concerns. They are governance failures. HHS’s
existing interoperability initiatives provide a necessary foundation, but AI oversight must be
explicitly built upon them.
In closing, artificial intelligence will continue to reshape health care regardless of regulatory
posture; the question before U.S. Department of Health and Human Services is whether that
transformation is governed with the same rigor applied to other population-level interventions.
Population science already provides the tools needed to meet this challenge. Post-Deployment
Algorithmic Surveillance (PDAS) advances the Department’s stated goals by establishing a
practical framework for continuous evaluation, accountable governance, and patient-centered
oversight of AI systems once they are embedded in care. By shifting evaluation from static
performance metrics to population-level outcomes, enabling timely regulatory response to
emerging risks, and ensuring that patients remain visible and protected within AI-mediated
systems, PDAS allows innovation to proceed without normalizing unmeasured harm. In this
way, PDAS supports the responsible adoption of AI while maintaining public trust, safeguarding
equity, and strengthening the evidence base for AI in health and public health.
