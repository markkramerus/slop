Key Recommendations for HHS
Accelerating AI in Clinical Care: A "Three-Lever" Approach
To transition AI from "bench to bedside," Massive Bio recommends HHS prioritize the following high-impact changes:
1. Modernize Regulation & Governance (The Safety Lever)
• Update FDA Frameworks: Finalize the "Total Product Lifecycle" approach for Software as a Medical Device (SaMD),
allowing AI algorithms to learn and improve over time without requiring new submissions for every update.
• Clarify Liability via Safe Harbors: Establish clear guidance that clinicians remain the final authority, but provide legal
"safe harbors" for providers who follow recommendations from HHS-certified or validated AI tools.
• Voluntary AI Certification: Launch an ONC-administered voluntary certification program for clinical AI tools to validate
safety, bias mitigation, and interoperability, giving providers the confidence to adopt them.
2. Align Reimbursement & Incentives (The Motivation Lever)
• Create CPT Codes for AI Services: Introduce billing codes for "AI-assisted decision support" and care coordination to
reimburse the time and complexity of using AI tools that improve outcomes.

Question 1. Biggest Barriers to Private-Sector AI Innovation and Adoption in Clinical Care
Q1: “What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption and use in clinical care?”
Key Barriers: Despite rapid advances in AI, private-sector innovators face significant hurdles bringing AI tools from
bench to bedside. Major barriers include:
• Fragmented Data and Interoperability Challenges: Quality healthcare AI depends on large volumes of diverse data, but
health data today is siloed across EHR systems, labs, imaging archives, and devices. Data fragmentation – and the lack of
seamless interoperability – is a top barrier to AI innovation. Many AI developers struggle to obtain diverse,
representative datasets due to information blocking or inconsistent standards. For example, oncology data lives in
disparate systems (EMRs, genomic testing labs, clinical trial databases) that historically do not communicate. This
fragmentation hinders training robust AI models and limits an AI tool’s ability to ingest real-time patient data during
clinical use. Massive Bio has confronted this challenge directly: our AI platform integrates data from community clinics,
academic centers, and patient-reported sources using common standards (FHIR, mCODE)[10]. However, not all
developers have the resources or permissions to aggregate such data. HHS’s focus on “data liquidity” and patient data
trust is well placed – improving interoperability is essential to overcome this foundational barrier[18].
• Regulatory Uncertainty and Risk Aversion: Innovators face ambiguity about how existing health regulations apply to AI,
especially software not clearly classified as medical devices. Uncertainty around FDA oversight, HIPAA, and liability for
AI-driven decisions creates a chilling effect. Startups fear investing in a tool that might later be deemed a regulated
device requiring lengthy approval, while providers fear using AI without clear legal guidelines. For instance, if an AI
clinical decision support system suggests a treatment and an error occurs, it’s unclear who is liable – the clinician, the
hospital, or the software maker?[19] This uncertainty leads to risk-averse institutional cultures, where hospital
compliance teams often err on the side of caution and delay adoption of new AI tools. Clear guidance is needed to
assure innovators and users that they can deploy AI safely within existing legal frameworks (more on this in Q3). Until
then, many promising AI solutions remain stuck in pilot phases. HHS’s RFI aptly notes that digital health regulatory
frameworks must evolve to account for AI-driven tools while maintaining safety[17] – the private sector needs this
evolution to proceed confidently with innovation.
• Lack of Reimbursement and Misaligned Incentives: The current payment landscape does not reward – and can even
penalize – the use of AI in clinical practice. Providers have little financial incentive to adopt AI tools that, for example,
prevent hospitalizations or automate tasks, because fee-for-service reimbursement is tied to volume of services. An AI
that improves efficiency or avoids an expensive therapy might reduce revenue under traditional models. Meanwhile,
there are few billing codes or payment models that directly reimburse AI-driven care improvements (e.g. no codes for
“AI-assisted diagnosis” or “AI care coordination”). The absence of reimbursement pathways is repeatedly cited by
providers as a barrier to investing in AI[20]. This is particularly relevant in oncology: many AI tools could help identify the
most cost-effective therapy or an appropriate clinical trial (saving costs of ineffective treatments), but providers face
upfront costs to integrate these tools without immediate reimbursement. Additionally, prior authorization processes
can unintentionally discourage innovation – if an AI recommends a novel therapy, insurers may not approve it without
extensive documentation, delaying care. Massive Bio’s approach of offering clinical trial options before prior
authorization barriers arise is one way to sidestep this, ensuring patients can access alternative cutting-edge treatments
without waiting for insurance approvals. However, broader payment reform is needed so that AI that reduces costs and
improves outcomes is financially rewarded, not discouraged. We discuss specific reimbursement levers in Q2.
• Clinical Integration and Workflow Challenges: Integrating AI into clinical workflows remains difficult. Busy healthcare
providers are often overwhelmed with technology (EHR alerts, documentation burdens), and a new AI tool can feel like
added complexity unless it is seamlessly embedded. If AI outputs are not presented at the right time and in an intuitive
way, clinicians may ignore them. Training requirements can also be a barrier – clinicians need to trust and understand
the AI, which takes education. In smaller practices or rural hospitals, there may be limited IT support to implement
advanced AI systems[21]. This creates an adoption gap: large health systems with resources charge ahead, while smaller
providers lag behind, exacerbating disparities[21]. Massive Bio has observed this gap in oncology; community oncologists
often lack the decision support tools available at major cancer centers. Our solution has been to provide AI-driven trial
matching as a turn-key service accessible via a simple web interface or phone call, which lowers the barrier for small
practices to participate. Nonetheless, widespread clinical adoption will require HHS to support easier integration

• Trust, Transparency, and Bias Concerns: Finally, a pervasive barrier is the concern among both providers and patients

and inclusive[15][26]. Building trust through such measures is crucial. HHS can assist by establishing or endorsing
standards for AI transparency and bias mitigation, giving providers and the public greater confidence to embrace AI
tools.
In summary, the biggest barriers to private-sector AI innovation and adoption in health care are not technological – they are
systemic. Data silos, unclear regulations, misaligned payments, workflow integration challenges, and trust issues all conspire to
impede progress. These barriers are interrelated and require a coordinated strategy across HHS agencies (ONC, CMS, FDA, OCR,
etc.) to address. We are encouraged that HHS recognizes many of these challenges in this RFI. By systematically lowering these
barriers – as outlined in the following responses – HHS can unleash a wave of private-sector innovation, allowing AI to fulfill its
promise of improving clinical care.
Question 2. Regulatory, Payment Policy, and Programmatic Changes to Incentivize AI in Clinical Care
Q2: “What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to incentivize the effective use of
AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or
use AI in clinical care? Please provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.”
Massive Bio believes HHS can accelerate AI adoption by acting decisively on three fronts – regulation, reimbursement, and
program design – the very levers highlighted in the RFI[3][17]. We recommend the following priority changes:
A. Evolve Regulatory Frameworks to Foster Innovation with Safety:
1. Modernize FDA’s Digital Health Oversight: HHS should work with the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to
update guidance on AI/ML-based software in clinical care. Current FDA regulations (21 CFR Part 820 and related
guidances) were not designed with continuously learning algorithms in mind. We urge HHS to finalize and implement
the FDA’s proposed framework for AI/ML SaMD (Software as a Medical Device) that allows for a “total product
lifecycle” approach. This would let developers make certain algorithm updates under pre-agreed protocols (e.g. a
“Predetermined Change Control Plan”) without requiring new submissions each time. Such an approach maintains
safety and effectiveness while allowing AI tools to improve over time. Additionally, clarification is needed on what
constitutes a regulated “medical device” versus clinical decision support exempt from regulation under 21st Century
Cures Act Section 3060. HHS should issue guidance (or support FDA in doing so) that clearly delineates the boundary
– for example, predictive analytics that drive therapy decisions might be regulated, whereas administrative AI or
transparent decision support might not. Clear rules will reduce uncertainty and spur innovation, as companies will
know whether to pursue FDA clearance or not. We also suggest HHS consider regulatory sandboxes or pilot
programs in partnership with FDA where novel AI solutions (especially those addressing high-need areas like cancer)
can be tested in a controlled setting with temporary regulatory flexibility. This can generate evidence to inform
permanent regulatory changes.
1. Address Liability and Safe Harbors: To encourage providers to adopt AI, HHS should explore creating safe harbors or
guidance around liability when using approved AI tools. Fear of malpractice exposure is a deterrent to clinical AI use. For
example, if a physician follows an AI recommendation that is consistent with clinical guidelines or an HHS-endorsed tool
and an adverse event occurs, that physician should not be held to a higher liability standard than if they had followed
standard practice. HHS, possibly via the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) or in collaboration with
professional societies, could develop “best practice” guidelines for AI-assisted care. Providers adhering to those could be
granted certain protections (similar in concept to safe harbors for following evidence-based guidelines). Clarifying
liability can reduce the “novel legal issues” noted in the RFI[19]. Likewise, HHS could encourage states to update medical
malpractice laws or insurer policies to recognize the role of AI. Additionally, we recommend HHS consider working with

2. Strengthen Data Privacy & Sharing Policies for AI: Privacy regulations should be updated to facilitate responsible AI

that. We recommend HHS develop standard patient consent frameworks for AI (potentially building on the model of
research biobanks) so that private companies can create opt-in patient data repositories for AI training under HHS
oversight. Furthermore, HHS should promote the adoption of Privacy Enhancing Technologies (like federated learning
and differential privacy) by issuing guidance or even amending the HIPAA Privacy Rule to recognize these techniques as
safeguards. For example, allowing health systems to participate in federated model training (where data doesn’t leave
their servers) without it being considered a HIPAA disclosure could accelerate cross-organization data collaborations. In
summary, privacy rules should not be static barriers but rather enablers of “safe, secure, and allowed” data use for
AI[18]. By updating regulations to explicitly accommodate modern AI data practices, HHS can both protect individuals
and advance innovation.
B. Align Reimbursement and Payment Policy with AI-Driven Care:
1. Create Reimbursement Pathways for AI Services: CMS should establish clear reimbursement mechanisms for clinical uses of AI
that add value. We recommend developing new CPT codes or modifiers for AI-assisted procedures and decision support. For
example, if an oncologist uses an AI platform to identify a therapy or clinical trial for a patient, there could be a billable code for
the time and complexity involved in that AI-informed care coordination. Similarly, radiologists who utilize AI to read scans or
pathologists using AI for slide analysis should have that reflected in billing (e.g., an add-on code indicating “AI augmentation
used”). These codes could initially be introduced as Category III (tracking) codes to collect data, then progress to payment once
value is demonstrated. Additionally, CMS’s technology add-on payments (NTAP) for new innovations might be extended to AI: if a
hospital adopts an AI system that demonstrably reduces complications or readmissions, an add-on payment could offset the cost.
The underlying principle is to reward outcomes and efficiency – if AI reduces total cost of care or improves quality, providers
should share in the savings. In oncology, for instance, if an AI tool helps avoid ineffective chemotherapy by matching a patient to a
targeted therapy trial at no cost, the payer saves money and the patient potentially has better outcomes. CMS could capture this
by allowing some form of shared savings or payment for the care coordination that led to trial enrollment. Without such
reimbursement, providers may hesitate to take the extra steps to use AI for trial matching, even though it benefits the system
overall. We applaud HHS for explicitly asking how reimbursement structures can be “simplified and better aligned to
support…efficient, deflationary technologies”[17]. One specific policy to revisit is Medicare’s reimbursement for telehealth and
remote monitoring: as AI becomes embedded in these services (e.g., AI chatbots for patient follow-up, AI-enhanced remote
patient monitoring for symptoms), CMS should ensure coverage and payment parity. The recent expansion of telehealth codes
could be expanded further to cover “AI-assisted remote care” so long as there is oversight by a clinician. Likewise, CMS might
consider demo programs where it reimburses AI-driven preventive care interventions that reduce hospitalizations (for example,
paying a monthly fee for an AI predictive service that helps manage high-risk patients, similar to chronic care management fees).
1. Incorporate AI into Value-Based Care Models and Quality Programs: HHS should leverage alternative payment models
(APMs) to drive AI adoption. Accountable Care Organizations (ACOs), bundled payment programs, and specialty models
like the Oncology Care Model (OCM) are natural vehicles for AI since they reward outcomes and cost reduction. We
recommend that CMS explicitly encourage or even require the use of advanced analytics/AI in these models. For
example, in a future Oncology Value-Based model, CMS could include quality measures or bonus points for utilizing
evidence-based AI decision support that improves care (like matching stage IV cancer patients to trials or genomic-
guided therapies). CMS Innovation Center (CMMI) could pilot an “AI Accelerator” within ACOs – perhaps giving
participating ACOs technical assistance grants to implement AI tools for care coordination, and then measuring the
impact on cost and quality. If successful, that could be scaled. Furthermore, HHS can align Medicare Advantage and
Medicaid policies to support AI: for instance, allowing plans to count investments in AI tools as quality improvement
activities in their Medical Loss Ratio (MLR) calculations, thereby incentivizing insurers to invest in provider-facing AI. On
the quality measurement front, HHS could work with organizations like NCQA and The Joint Commission to incorporate
AI-enabled care processes into accreditation and scoring. A concrete near-term step: include use of clinical AI in Merit-
Based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) Improvement Activities. Clinicians could earn credit for implementing an AI tool
that, say, identifies care gaps or reduces medication errors. Aligning quality incentives will nudge providers toward AI as
a means to achieve targets.

2. Reduce Administrative Barriers and Prior Auth Burdens via AI: We suggest HHS revise policies that inadvertently
discourage AI by imposing unnecessary administrative hurdles. A prime example is prior authorization. HHS can
modernize prior auth regulations to recognize AI-based determinations. For instance, if an AI algorithm (certified for
accuracy) finds that a patient meets evidence-based criteria for a certain test or referral, payers could be required to
automatically approve it or use a “gold card” approach for providers using such tools. This would flip prior auth into a
more automated, AI-driven process that reduces delays. CMS could pilot this in Medicare Advantage: encourage MA
plans to integrate AI for initial screening of auth requests, granting immediate approval when criteria are met (perhaps
tying this to the recently proposed rules on electronic prior auth to ensure timely decisions). By doing so, patients get
needed care faster and the burden on providers is lessened[27]. From Massive Bio’s perspective, offering patients
clinical trial alternatives is one way to avoid the prior auth maze altogether – if HHS were to formally support policies
that “clinical trial enrollment can be considered a prior auth exception” (for instance, pausing prior auth on standard
treatment when a patient opts for an eligible clinical trial), it would be a strong incentive for providers to utilize AI
matching services proactively. Essentially, reward pathways that solve the prior auth challenge either by automation or
by providing alternative options (like trials) that render prior auth moot.
C. Programmatic and Policy Design Changes:
1. Expand Public-Private Partnerships and Challenge Programs: HHS should use its convening power to create partnerships that
accelerate AI implementation. One idea is an AI Clinical Care Challenge series – prize competitions (as authorized by the America
COMPETES Act) around specific needs (e.g., “AI for Cancer Detection Challenge” or “AI for Reducing Hospital Readmissions”).
These could be run by HHS’s IDEA Lab or ONC, with winners receiving funding or pilot opportunities at HHS hospitals (like the VA or
IHS)[28]. Such competitions signal HHS’s priorities and incentivize private innovators to focus on high-impact areas. Additionally,
HHS could establish Centers of Excellence for AI in Clinical Practice via grant funding (perhaps through AHRQ or HRSA for
community health centers). These centers (academic or community consortiums) would develop, test, and disseminate best
practices for AI adoption – effectively demonstration sites that others can learn from. For example, Massive Bio is a founding
member of the federally sponsored CancerX public-private partnership[29][30]; continued support and funding for such
consortiums will drive multi-stakeholder solutions to common barriers. We recommend HHS also collaborate with the Department
of Energy and NSF on the Genesis Mission initiative – while DOE’s Genesis Mission focuses on AI for scientific research[31][32], a
parallel HHS “Genesis Project” could focus on AI for health challenges, ensuring healthcare is a key domain in national AI strategy.
By aligning with broader national AI missions, HHS can tap into additional resources and talent for healthcare AI.
1. Revise ONC Certification and CMS Conditions of Participation: ONC’s EHR certification program has been effective in
standardizing certain functionalities. HHS could consider an ONC-administered AI Certification program – voluntary at
first – where AI clinical tools are assessed for basic safety, interoperability, and bias mitigation. If a tool meets the
criteria, it gets an HHS “stamp of approval,” giving providers confidence to adopt it. This kind of certification could later
tie to CMS conditions (e.g., hospitals using AI for clinical decisions must use certified tools to qualify for Medicare
reimbursement, ensuring quality). Similarly, CMS could update hospital Conditions of Participation to require an AI
governance plan for hospitals deploying AI (ensuring they address bias, monitor outcomes, etc.). While adding
requirements may seem like a burden, done thoughtfully it can actually encourage hospitals to embrace AI with proper
oversight rather than avoid it.
2. Revisit CFR provisions hindering data sharing: HHS might identify specific regulations in 42 CFR (Medicare/Medicaid) or
45 CFR (Public Welfare, including HIPAA rules) that unnecessarily restrict innovative uses of AI. For example, 42 CFR Part
2 (confidentiality of substance use records) is one area that, while important, complicates integrating behavioral health
data into AI models; aligning Part 2 with HIPAA as recently proposed will help comprehensive AI care. Another example:
45 CFR 164 (HIPAA Privacy Rule) – defining de-identification standards that allow for the sharing of key data like genomic
information (which currently can be tricky under the “identifier” removal rules). Clarifying that codified genomic variants
or certain derived data can be shared if individuals cannot be readily identified would help AI in precision medicine. We
encourage HHS to take a holistic look at its regulations to find and fix such friction points.
In summary, HHS should act boldly to update regulations, realign incentives, and design programs that collectively turbocharge
AI adoption in care. By prioritizing the changes above, HHS will send a strong signal that America is committed to operationalizing
AI for health. These steps will incentivize companies like Massive Bio to continue innovating in areas like oncology, knowing that a
supportive policy environment is in place. Importantly, none of these changes sacrifice patient safety or privacy – rather, they
modernize protections and payments to fit AI, ensuring that the effective use of AI is rewarded and scaled. Massive Bio is
especially enthusiastic about these changes because they would directly augment our ability to develop and deploy AI for clinical
trials and precision oncology. For example, clearer FDA guidance would streamline enhancement of our trial-matching algorithms;
new reimbursement for AI-driven care coordination would allow more clinics to adopt our platform; and partnership programs
would enable us to demonstrate our tech in more public settings. We believe these policy moves are not only justified – they are
urgently needed to keep pace with technology and to realize AI’s potential to “propel the American health care system
forward.”[3]

Question 3. Novel Legal and Implementation Issues for Non-Medical-Device AI – and HHS’s Role
Q3: “For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel legal and implementation issues that
challenge existing governance and accountability structures (e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security).
What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?”
AI tools that are not regulated as medical devices (e.g., clinical support software, administrative AI systems, etc.)
indeed present new challenges for our health care legal and governance frameworks. Massive Bio engages
with several such AI applications (for example, our AI-driven patient triage and scheduling tools are not FDA-
regulated devices but are used in care workflows), so we have first-hand insight into these issues. Key novel
issues and recommended HHS roles include:
• Liability and Accountability for AI-Influenced Decisions: When an AI system influences clinical decisions, it blurs
traditional lines of accountability. If an algorithm (say, an AI risk score or triage recommendation) contributes to a clinical
decision that leads to harm, there is ambiguity over who is legally responsible – the clinician who accepted the AI’s
advice? The hospital that deployed the tool? The software developer? This lack of clarity is a “novel” issue because our
malpractice system assumes human decision-makers. HHS should convene stakeholders (providers, developers, insurers,
patient advocates) to develop consensus guidelines on AI liability. While legislation may ultimately be needed, in the
near term HHS can issue guidance that clarifies expectations. For instance, HHS could endorse the approach that
clinicians remain the final authority and thus primarily liable, but that following validated AI recommendations could be
considered in determining the standard of care. Simultaneously, HHS could work with professional liability insurers to
encourage coverage models that don’t penalize providers for using AI that improves care. By framing when it is
appropriate to rely on AI (e.g., if tool meets certain validation criteria), HHS can give providers confidence to use AI
without fear of unjust legal exposure. Additionally, HHS might explore model legislation for states that addresses AI
liability – for example, laws that provide a rebuttable presumption that if a clinician followed an HHS-certified AI
recommendation, they met the standard of care. This would address liability concerns in a balanced way.
• Indemnification and Vendor Responsibility: Hospitals and health systems often seek indemnification from AI vendors
for any harm caused by their software. However, vendors are wary of open-ended liability, especially since the end-users
ultimately make clinical decisions. This dynamic can slow adoption – negotiations over indemnity can be lengthy or some
vendors refuse to enter the healthcare market. HHS could help by developing model contract language or best practices
for AI procurement in healthcare, perhaps through ONC or AHRQ. By standardizing how responsibility is shared (e.g.,
vendor liable for software faults, provider liable for misuse), HHS can ease the burden on individual organizations to sort
this out. Another approach is HHS fostering the creation of an insurance pool or fund for AI-related incidents –
analogous to the National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program, which removed direct manufacturer liability as a
barrier to vaccine availability. A similar concept could be considered for certain AI: an industry-wide fund that
compensates patients in rare cases of AI error causing harm, thereby limiting the need for each vendor to carry massive
liability insurance. HHS’s role would be in studying feasibility and potentially advocating for enabling legislation if
needed.
• Privacy and Data Use Ethics: Unlike traditional medical devices, many AI systems feed on vast datasets – including
patient information – raising concerns about privacy beyond HIPAA’s original scope. Two novel concerns are: (1) AI
models trained on patient data might inadvertently “remember” sensitive details (creating risk of re-identification or
unintended disclosure), and (2) companies may seek to use patient data for secondary AI development in ways patients
don’t expect. To address these, HHS should update privacy regulations and guidance as discussed in Q2. Specifically,
HHS/OCR should clarify how de-identification rules apply to complex data like genomic or free-text data used in AI. It
may be necessary to develop new guidelines or safe harbors for AI model training, ensuring that if certain best practices
are followed (like data aggregation, removal of direct identifiers, bias testing to protect minority groups), the use is
permissible. HHS might also support development of technical standards for AI model security – for instance,
encouraging the use of encryption, access controls, and audit trails for AI systems that handle patient data. The idea of AI
systems themselves needing to be HIPAA-compliant is novel. Perhaps HHS could assert that significant AI models should
undergo a Privacy Impact Assessment or be certified for how they handle data. In essence, HHS should extend its existing
privacy and security frameworks to explicitly cover the AI lifecycle (data collection → model training → deployment →
data outputs). This might include guidance on issues like using cloud-based AI services (which introduces third-party
concerns) or leveraging patient data to create synthetic data for AI (potentially a privacy-preserving practice HHS could
endorse).
• Bias, Fairness, and Health Equity Concerns: Not specifically legal in the traditional sense, but a governance issue: AI tools
can inadvertently propagate bias against protected classes (race, gender, etc.), leading to disparities in care. While anti-
discrimination laws (like Civil Rights Act Title VI for entities receiving federal funds) could be interpreted to cover biased
AI, this is uncharted territory. HHS should take an active role in setting expectations that AI in clinical settings must be

fair and unbiased. We suggest HHS, through the Office of Civil Rights and Office of Minority Health, issue guidance or
even regulations requiring that any AI used in clinical care undergo bias testing and mitigation. This could involve
routine audits to see if outcomes differ by race or other factors. If significant bias is found and not corrected, that could

• Transparency and Explainability Requirements: A novel implementation issue is how to govern “black box” algorithms.

accountability. HHS should consider policies that push for algorithmic transparency appropriate to context. For non-

is involved in their care and, upon request, given a basic explanation of how it works (similar to how patients have a right

cancer trial, it should provide the oncologist with the key reasons (patient has X mutation, Y clinical criteria matched) in
human-readable form. This is something Massive Bio has implemented (transparent rationales)[33], and it greatly aids
clinician acceptance. HHS might not need a new law for this; even a guidance or adding a requirement in Medicare
Advantage plan oversight (since MA plans are increasingly using AI for utilization management) that beneficiaries are
informed of AI use would set a precedent.
In all the above areas, HHS’s role is to provide guidance, standards, and a forum for resolution of these novel issues. The
Department can use its influence to shape soft law (guidelines, best practices) that preempt the need for constantly reacting in
courts after problems occur. We specifically recommend HHS establish a Healthcare AI Governance Council – bringing together
FDA, ONC, CMS, OCR, OIG, and external experts – to continuously monitor these emerging issues and update policies. This council
could issue annual reports or recommendations to keep regulations nimble. The council could tackle questions like: Should there
be an “AI Incident Reporting System” (analogous to FDA’s adverse event reporting, but for AI errors or near-misses)? How do we
harmonize state laws (e.g., some states may start passing their own rules on AI liability or data use – HHS could offer a model to
encourage consistency)?
Massive Bio’s perspective is that sensible guardrails will ultimately increase adoption of AI, by ensuring trust and accountability.
HHS should neither micromanage nor take a hands-off approach, but rather serve as an enabler and coordinator on these
governance challenges. By addressing liability, privacy, bias, and transparency in a proactive way, HHS will help remove these
as points of friction or fear. For example, once providers know HHS’s stance on AI-related liability and see a clear framework,
they will be more willing to integrate AI (as one physician told us, “I just want to know I won’t be hung out to dry if I use the
AI recommendation in good faith”). Similarly, by ensuring AI adheres to privacy and equity standards, HHS will build public
trust, which is foundational for widespread AI use. In summary, HHS should play an active role by updating governance
structures to accommodate AI, thereby protecting patients and providers while unleashing the technology’s benefits.
Question 4. Promising Evaluation Methods for Non-Device AI – and How HHS Can Support Them
Q4: “For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and post-deployment), metrics,
robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support
these processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most impactful (e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or
prize competitions)?”
Evaluating AI tools that are not subject to FDA pre-market review is a critical challenge. Without a formal
regulatory approval process, we need other methods to ensure these AI systems are safe, effective, and fit
seamlessly into clinical workflows. Fortunately, the industry and research community have been developing
evaluation approaches. Massive Bio has practical experience in this area – we continuously evaluate our AI
matching algorithms both before deployment (in silico and pilot testing) and after deployment (real-world
performance monitoring) to ensure they function as intended for patients and clinicians. Below, we outline
promising evaluation methods and suggest how HHS can bolster them:
Promising Pre-Deployment Evaluation Methods:
• Retrospective Validation with Gold-Standard Datasets: Before an AI is used in live clinical care, one robust method is to
test it on retrospective data where outcomes or “ground truth” decisions are known. For example, an AI that
recommends cancer clinical trials can be validated on past patient cases: feed in historical patient data and see if the AI
would have identified the trials that those patients actually enrolled in (or that experts would have chosen). Similarly,

diagnostic AIs can be tested against existing datasets of images or cases with known diagnoses. These validations
measure accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, etc. A promising trend is the creation of open clinical datasets and challenges
– for instance, the NIH has released large imaging datasets for AI algorithm benchmarking. Community competitions
(such as MICCAI challenges in imaging) have driven algorithm improvements. HHS can encourage this by supporting the
curation and sharing of de-identified clinical datasets as evaluation benchmarks. Metrics like area-under-curve (AUC) for
accuracy, or positive predictive value, are common, but evaluation should go beyond just accuracy: robustness across
subgroups (e.g., how does performance vary by age, ethnicity, hospital site?) is key. Pre-deployment robustness testing
might involve intentionally stress-testing the AI with edge cases or noisy data to see if it still performs. For example, at
Massive Bio, we test our trial matching AI on data with missing fields or atypical patient profiles to ensure the system can
handle real-world messiness.
• Simulation and Clinical Vignettes: Another pre-deployment approach is simulation studies. In a controlled setting,
clinicians can be presented with cases (vignettes) and the AI’s recommendation, and researchers measure how the
clinicians respond, whether the AI’s input improved decision quality or speed. Think of it as a “clinical Turing test” – does
the clinician aided by AI do better than without? This human-centered evaluation assesses usability and usefulness, not
just technical accuracy. For instance, we have conducted user-acceptance testing with oncologists for our AI interface:
we simulate a patient consult with and without the AI suggesting trials, and gather feedback. Key metrics from such
studies include time saved, reduction in cognitive workload (maybe via NASA-TLX surveys), and user trust scores.
These metrics capture workflow integration and human factors, which are as important as pure accuracy. If an AI is
accurate but too cumbersome or confusing, clinicians won’t use it. Measuring Net Promoter Score or satisfaction of
clinicians in pilot usage can predict whether the AI will be adopted at scale.
• Prospective Pilot Studies (Pre-deployment in limited setting): Especially for high-impact AI, conducting a prospective
pilot in a limited real-world setting before wider rollout is valuable. This is akin to a phase 4 trial but done proactively.
For example, test an AI in one unit or one hospital for a period, with oversight, gather outcome data and iterate. While
technically this is “deployment,” it’s part of evaluation before scaling up. Promising methods include A/B testing or
stepped-wedge trial designs where some clinicians or sites use the AI and others serve as control, then outcomes (clinical
outcomes or process measures) are compared.
Promising Post-Deployment Evaluation and Monitoring:
• Continuous Performance Monitoring and Feedback Loops: Once an AI is live, it should not be a static “fire-and-forget”
tool. A strong method is implementing ongoing monitoring of the AI’s outputs versus actual outcomes. For instance, if
our AI predicts a patient is eligible for a trial, we track whether that patient actually enrolls and benefits; if not, why not?
This helps catch any drift in the AI’s effectiveness. In hospital settings, AI systems often have dashboards tracking their
performance (e.g., how many sepsis alerts were true positives vs false). Monitoring metrics could include real-world
accuracy, error rates, override rates (how often clinicians disagree with or ignore the AI), and outcome impact (did
using the AI correlate with better patient outcomes or lower costs?). For example, Cleveland Clinic monitored its AI
sepsis alerts and found a ten-fold reduction in false positives and a 46% increase in sepsis cases identified after
implementing a particular AI, which validated the tool’s performance in practice[34]. We also look at disparities post-
deployment: are there any biases emerging (e.g., is the AI systematically less effective for certain subpopulations)?
Continual auditing for fairness is a key part of post-deployment evaluation.
• Robustness Testing in Production: Real-world can throw curveballs that weren’t in the training data. A good practice is
periodic robustness testing of AI in production by injecting test cases. For example, an institution might occasionally run
a set of test patient profiles through their live clinical AI (without affecting actual care decisions) to see if outputs remain
consistent and robust. Another aspect is monitoring for data drift: if the patient population or medical practice changes
over time, the AI’s model might need recalibration. Automated drift detection algorithms can alert if the input data
distribution shifts significantly or if outcome metrics deteriorate. This triggers model retraining or revision. At Massive
Bio, we retrain our matching algorithms regularly as new trials and guidelines emerge; we also use Radiant Core, our AI
control plane, to supervise agents and flag anomalies in the workflow in real time[13]. This kind of built-in monitoring
(agents overseeing agents) is an emerging best practice in AI operations (AI Ops).
• User Feedback and Governance Committees: Human-centered post-deployment evaluation includes formalizing user
feedback channels. Many hospitals have set up AI or Clinical Decision Support oversight committees that review AI
performance and clinician feedback, somewhat like a Pharmacy & Therapeutics committee for algorithms. They track if
clinicians are reporting any issues, confusion, or near-misses involving the AI. Metrics like “number of incident reports
related to AI per 1,000 uses” can be useful. HHS could encourage healthcare organizations to adopt such governance
structures, ensuring that AI tools are continually evaluated in context and not just set loose.

Workflow and Human-Centered Evaluation Methods:
Beyond technical performance, evaluating how AI fits into clinical workflow and impacts humans (providers and
patients) is vital:
• Usability Testing: Before and after deployment, performing usability tests (with methods like heuristic evaluation or
direct observation) ensures the AI’s user interface and integration are optimal. For instance, does the alert come at the
right time in the workflow? Is the information too much, too little? A great AI that is hard to use will fail. Metrics include
error rates in using the interface, time to complete tasks, and user satisfaction ratings.
• Impact on Provider Workload and Burnout: One promising evaluation angle is measuring how AI affects provider
workload. Early examples show AI scribes reduced physician after-hours documentation time, correlating with a 40%
drop in burnout in a pilot at Mass General Brigham[35]. On the flip side, poorly designed AI can increase frustration.
Therefore, evaluation should include surveys or analysis of provider workload before vs after AI. HHS might fund studies
on AI’s impact on provider well-being as part of evaluation. If AI can truly reduce burden (a key HHS goal[2]), it should be
quantified.
• Patient-Centered Evaluation: If the AI directly or indirectly touches patients, we should evaluate patient experience and
outcomes. Do patients trust the AI’s involvement? Are they more satisfied because decisions are faster or more
personalized? For example, in Massive Bio’s case, when patients use our AI-powered trial matching through ACS ACTS,
we collect feedback on whether it improved their understanding and options. Metrics like patient satisfaction scores,
uptake rates of AI-suggested options, and outcomes (like time to treatment or adherence rates) are important. In cancer
care, we could evaluate if AI-driven trial matching increases the percentage of patients who get onto a trial or a targeted
therapy, and if that in turn improves survival or quality of life (longer-term metrics).
Should HHS support these evaluation processes? Absolutely, and with urgency. Given the rapid proliferation of AI, we cannot rely
on ad-hoc evaluation if we want public trust and effective adoption. HHS can play a pivotal role by facilitating systematic
evaluation in the following ways:
• Funding and Grants: HHS (through AHRQ, NIH, ONC, etc.) should fund research and development of AI evaluation
frameworks and tools. For pre-deployment evaluation, grants could support creation of open reference datasets and
bias testing suites that vendors can use to self-evaluate. For post-deployment, AHRQ could fund projects that develop
best practices for continuous monitoring or that measure real-world impact of AI on outcomes. For example, funding a
multi-center trial to test how an AI referral system affects cancer patient outcomes across different communities would
provide valuable evidence (and inform CMS reimbursement decisions). Specific grant programs (like SBIR for small
businesses or ONC’s Leading Edge Acceleration Projects - LEAP) could solicit proposals on innovative AI evaluation
methods, such as automated validation tools or explainability techniques.
• Contracts and Cooperative Agreements: HHS could use contracts to engage entities to perform evaluations on a broader
scale. For instance, ONC could contract an academic center or standards organization to develop an “AI in Clinical Care
Accreditation” program or voluntary certification (akin to how ONC contracted for development of EHR test procedures).
Cooperative agreements could establish centers that partner with HHS to evaluate AI implementations in real healthcare
settings (similar to patient safety organizations, but for AI). One idea is a National AI Testing Collaborative: HHS
sponsors a cooperative network of health systems that agree to pilot and evaluate AI solutions to generate public data
on what works and what doesn’t. This network can publish results that everyone can learn from, rather than each
hospital reinventing the wheel in private.
• Prize Competitions and Challenges: As the RFI suggests, prize competitions are a flexible tool. HHS could run an “AI
Evaluation Challenge” – inviting proposals for novel metrics or methods to test AI’s safety and efficacy, with prize
funding to the best ideas[28]. Another possibility: an “AI Stress Test Challenge” where participants must design a battery
of tests to rigorously probe an AI system for edge-case failures. These competitions can spur creativity and bring in
multidisciplinary talent (e.g., tech, human factors, ethics experts) to tackle evaluation.
• Standards and Frameworks Endorsement: HHS should collaborate with standards-developing organizations (like IEEE,
ISO, or NIST). NIST, for example, has released an AI Risk Management Framework recently; HHS can adopt and tailor this
to healthcare, encouraging providers and vendors to use it as a guideline for evaluating and managing AI risks. By
referencing such frameworks in HHS communications or even incorporating them into ONC’s certification criteria or
CMS’s expectations for ACOs/hospitals, it nudges the whole industry to systematically evaluate AI. ONC could, for
instance, include criteria for “baseline algorithm validation and ongoing monitoring” in its voluntary health IT
certification for AI tools, which would essentially operationalize evaluation standards.

Question 5. Supporting Private-Sector AI through Accreditation, Certification, and Credentialing – Role of HHS
Q5: “How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, industry-driven testing, and
credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?”
HHS can play a catalytic role in empowering the private sector to self-organize and self-regulate in ways that
promote safe and effective AI. The key is for HHS to endorse and reinforce industry-led efforts by providing
guidance, recognition, and where appropriate, incentives or requirements tied to accreditation and
certification. Here are concrete ways HHS can support these private-sector activities:
• Establish an “AI in Healthcare” Voluntary Certification Program: Just as ONC certifies electronic health record systems,
HHS (via ONC or a designated body) could set up a voluntary certification for AI tools used in clinical care. The private
sector can drive the criteria development – for example, working through a consortium like the Coalition for Health AI or
IEEE – but HHS’s support would give it weight. Certification criteria might include core requirements such as:
demonstrated accuracy above a threshold, adherence to privacy/security standards, bias testing results, interoperability
capabilities (e.g., can ingest/produce data via FHIR), and usability evidence. HHS can support this by convening
stakeholders to define the standards and by officially recognizing certified products (perhaps maintaining a public list on
HealthIT.gov of “HHS-recognized Certified Clinical AI Tools”). Although voluntary, this signals to healthcare providers
which products have met quality bars, accelerating adoption. Moreover, HHS could tie incentives to certification: for
instance, CMS could provide a slight increase in reimbursement or a bonus under MIPS for providers who utilize certified
AI tools (analogous to how using certified EHR technology was required for incentive payments). Massive Bio sees value
in this – as a company, we would pursue such certification to differentiate our AI platform as trustworthy and effective.
• Support Development of Accreditation Standards for AI Use by Healthcare Organizations: The private sector, through
bodies like The Joint Commission or URAC, could develop accreditation criteria around AI deployment in hospitals and
clinics. These might cover governance (does the organization have an AI oversight committee?), training (are clinicians
trained in AI tools?), monitoring (does the hospital monitor AI outcomes and safety incidents?), and patient
consent/communication about AI. HHS can encourage this by collaborating with these accrediting organizations. For
example, HHS could fund a project with The Joint Commission to develop new standards or provide pilot testing
opportunities. Once developed, HHS could endorse these accreditation standards as best practice. Eventually, CMS
might even incorporate some into Conditions of Participation, but even before that, simply having an accreditation
available sets a bar for health systems to strive for. An analogy is how cybersecurity accreditation (like HITRUST) became
a de facto requirement in healthcare IT; similarly, an AI governance accreditation could become a norm. HHS’s role is to
validate and promote that these private standards serve public goals (safety, efficacy, equity).
• Facilitate Industry-Driven Testing and Benchmarking Consortia: Private companies often hesitate to share data or
methods for competitive reasons, but HHS can provide a neutral ground for collaboration on testing. For instance, HHS
could sponsor an AI Clinical Validation Lab where multiple AI vendors’ tools are evaluated on a common dataset or
against common clinical scenarios. This lab could be run by a trusted third party (maybe NIST or a university) under
contract with HHS. Participation could be voluntary but if HHS provides reports or “seals of quality” to top performers, it
incentivizes companies to participate and improve. The industry-driven aspect means the metrics and test cases are
decided in consultation with vendors and providers so they’re meaningful. We’ve seen some industry coalitions (like the
xVIEW challenges in medical imaging) – HHS can amplify these by lending support and possibly aligning federal needs
with them (e.g., linking a challenge to a federal prize or incorporating results into procurement preferences).
• Credentialing and Training of Healthcare Workforce in AI: HHS can encourage the private sector in credentialing
clinicians for AI competencies. For example, professional boards (like the American Board of Radiology or Oncology

Question 6. Successes and Shortcomings of AI in Clinical Care; High-Potential Novel AI Tools
Q6: “Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost expectations and where have they
fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights
on quality, and help reduce costs?”
Successes – AI Tools that Met or Exceeded Expectations: In several domains, AI has already delivered notable
successes, improving performance or reducing costs beyond what was expected:
• Medical Imaging and Diagnostics: AI for image analysis has shone as an early success. For example, in radiology, FDA-
cleared AI algorithms for detecting diabetic retinopathy or identifying pulmonary nodules on CT scans have
demonstrated accuracy on par with expert physicians. Many hospitals report that AI-driven diagnostic tools are speeding
up workflow and catching findings that might be missed. A concrete success story is in sepsis detection: Cleveland Clinic
deployed an AI-based sepsis early warning system (Bayesian Health’s platform) and observed dramatic improvements –
a 10-fold reduction in false alarm rates and a 46% increase in actual sepsis cases identified in time[34]. The AI alerted
clinicians to sepsis in many cases even before antibiotics were given, allowing earlier life-saving intervention[34]. This
exceeded expectations by both improving clinical outcomes (sepsis detection and treatment timing) and reducing alert
fatigue (fewer false positives than prior systems). Another diagnostic success is in pathology: AI tools that analyze digital
pathology slides have improved detection of metastatic cancer cells and can do in minutes what might take a pathologist
much longer. These tools augment the specialists, leading to faster results and potentially cost savings through
efficiency.
• Operational Efficiency and Cost Savings: AI has made significant strides in hospital operations. Predictive algorithms for
patient flow and staffing have helped some hospitals optimize bed management and reduce length of stay. For instance,
AI-driven scheduling tools have cut wait times in operating rooms and imaging departments by predicting no-shows or
delays. On a system level, industry analyses project major cost savings; one estimate suggests AI could reduce hospital
operating costs by 10–20% in the long term, translating to up to $300–$900 billion in annual savings by 2050 if scaled
nationally[36]. While much of that is projection, some organizations are already seeing substantial returns on specific AI
deployments (e.g., reducing unnecessary ER visits through AI triage, saving millions annually for large health systems).
We have even early examples in revenue cycle management: AI for automated medical coding and billing has sped up
reimbursements and lowered administrative costs for many practices, meeting expectations for ROI in that area.
• Administrative and Documentation Burden Reduction: AI “scribes” and natural language processing tools have been
quite successful in reducing clinician burden. Pilot programs with ambient listening devices that transcribe and structure
clinical notes have shown that doctors spend less time typing and more time with patients. As noted, Mass General
Brigham reported about a 40% relative drop in physician burnout during an AI scribe pilot, as it significantly cut after-
hours documentation[35]. While the financial ROI for scribes is still being assessed, the improved provider satisfaction
and reclaimed time have exceeded expectations in those trials – a critical outcome given the burnout crisis.
• Clinical Decision Support and Personalized Medicine: In specialized areas like oncology, AI-driven decision support has
started to exceed expectations by handling complexity beyond human limits. Massive Bio’s experience provides an
example: our AI-driven trial matching “pre-screening hubs” have dramatically shortened the time to identify clinical
trial options for cancer patients and boosted trial participation rates, as recognized by the White House Cancer
Moonshot[12]. These hubs scan comprehensive genomic profiles and patient data to pinpoint trials in real-time,
achieving in minutes what used to take expert teams many hours. The result is more patients getting matched to trials
(over 50,000 patients per year projected) and faster enrollment – outcomes that exceed prior benchmarks and
expectations for expanding trial access[11][12]. This has also shown cost benefits: every patient who goes on a trial often
gets expensive therapy covered by the trial sponsor, potentially saving payers tens of thousands of dollars per patient
compared to standard care.
• Population Health and Preventive Care: AI risk prediction models have helped focus interventions on the right patients.
For example, some ACOs use AI to predict which patients are at risk of hospitalization in the next 30 days and then
proactively deploy care managers to those patients. In some cases, these models have exceeded expectations by
identifying high-risk individuals that traditional methods missed, leading to measurable reductions in readmissions or ER
visits. Insurers, too, have used AI to flag potential medication non-adherence or care gaps and have improved outcomes
with relatively low-cost outreach, saving larger costs down the line.
Shortcomings – Where AI Tools Have Fallen Short:
• Generalizability and Real-World Performance Gaps: A number of AI tools that performed well in research settings failed
to meet expectations in the real world. One famous example is IBM Watson for Oncology – heavily touted as an AI to

revolutionize cancer treatment, it ultimately fell short of clinical expectations and was not shown to improve patient
outcomes meaningfully. This highlighted that domain-specific nuance and integration into workflow were
underestimated. In some cases, AI algorithms have shown degraded accuracy when deployed on different patient
populations or different hospitals than they were trained on, exposing a lack of generalizability. For instance, an AI
trained on one hospital’s imaging data might not perform as well on another’s due to different equipment or patient
demographics. These disappointments have made clear that rigorous external validation is needed; some early AI
products did not get that, leading to over-promising and under-delivering.
• Bias and Health Equity Issues: A notable shortcoming was uncovered in an algorithm widely used to prioritize patients
for care management in some health systems: it systematically underestimated the health needs of Black patients
compared to white patients because it used healthcare spending as a proxy for illness, and historically less money was
spent on Black patients[22]. This bias meant the tool fell short morally and practically by missing high-need patients. It
was an eye-opener that some AI can inadvertently perpetuate disparities – a failure to meet the expectation that AI
would be objective and fair. HHS has rightly flagged this as an area needing attention. Patients and providers expect AI to
at least do no harm in terms of equity, and when those expectations are violated, it undermines trust.
• Lack of Clear Financial ROI in Some Cases: While AI can save costs, in some deployments the ROI has been elusive or
slower than expected. For example, early adopters of AI for clinical documentation found that while doctors were
happier, the financial ROI wasn’t immediate because these tools can be expensive to implement and maintain[37]. Some
hospital executives expected quick cost savings from AI, but realized that benefits sometimes accrue in intangible ways
(quality, patient satisfaction) or long-term (averted costs in the future) rather than immediate budget relief. As noted in
a FierceHealthcare evaluation, AI scribes improved experience but had “limited financial ROI” initially[37]. This shortfall
in meeting cost expectations is often due to integration costs, training, and the need to adjust workflows. Similarly, some
predictive models didn’t yield savings because organizations struggled to act on the predictions (the “last mile”
problem). This taught us that AI alone doesn’t save money; it must be paired with effective process changes, and that
piece sometimes fell short.
• Workflow Integration and Alert Fatigue: Some AI tools under-delivered because they weren’t well integrated or
produced too many alerts. For example, early AI-based clinical decision support for EHRs sometimes generated excessive
suggestions or warnings that annoyed clinicians (e.g., constantly reminding about care gaps or suggesting irrelevant
clinical trials). This created alert fatigue and low utilization, falling short of the hoped-for impact on quality. The issue
wasn’t that the AI was “wrong” – rather it wasn’t nuanced or user-friendly enough, betraying expectations that AI would
simplify workflows when in fact it sometimes complicated them. The lesson learned is the importance of human-
centered design, which some early solutions lacked.
In summary, AI has excelled in well-defined tasks like image analysis, prediction of specific events like sepsis, and automating
routine processes – often exceeding expectations in speed and accuracy. It’s faltered when faced with issues of generalization,
bias, user adoption, and translating predictions into practice. Many shortcomings have been due to context, not core algorithmic
ability: integration, trust, data quality, etc.
High-Potential Novel AI Tools for the Future:
Looking forward, we see several categories of novel AI tools that could have transformative impacts on outcomes,
quality insights, and cost reduction:
• AI for Precision Medicine & Complex Decision Support*: AI that can synthesize multimodal data* (genomics,

in complex diseases like cancer, cardiac disease, or neurological conditions. The next generation of decision support
might use deep learning to identify hidden patterns – for instance, predicting which cancer patients will respond to
immunotherapy by integrating genomic markers with pathology images and clinical history. Such an AI could spare
patients ineffective treatments (improving outcomes and avoiding costs of toxicity). It could also suggest novel uses
for existing drugs (drug repurposing) or optimal trial opportunities. The potential to give new insights on quality comes
from uncovering subgroups of patients who have vastly different outcomes with different approaches – something
traditional analysis often misses. Massive Bio’s work hints at this potential: by analyzing real-world data across
thousands of patients, our AI has started to identify patterns (like particular tumor mutations combined with patient
demographics) that correlate with better trial responses. In the future, a clinician might get an AI-generated “patient
similarity” analysis: “patients like this one, with X and Y characteristics, tended to live 6 months longer on treatment A vs
treatment B.” This kind of guidance could revolutionize quality of care by truly individualizing it.
• Generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs) in Clinical Care: The emergence of powerful LLMs (akin to ChatGPT)

early signs of complication and arrange a home nurse visit immediately – heading off an ED visit.

Question 7. Influential Roles in AI Adoption and Primary Hurdles in Health Care Organizations
Q7: “Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations have the most influence on the
adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?”
In our experience working with a variety of healthcare organizations (from major academic medical centers to
community oncology clinics), certain key decision-makers and bodies heavily influence whether AI tools are
embraced or not. Additionally, there are common administrative hurdles that organizations face when
implementing AI. Identifying these roles and hurdles is important because it helps target interventions (who
to educate, what processes to streamline) to speed up AI adoption.
Key Roles and Decision Makers Influencing AI Adoption:
• Executive Leadership (C-Suite): Hospital and health system executives – particularly the CEO, Chief Medical Officer
(CMO), Chief Information Officer (CIO), and Chief Technology/Innovation Officer – play a decisive role. If the C-suite
believes in a strong digital/AI strategy, the institution will pursue AI projects and allocate resources. Conversely, if top
leadership is skeptical or risk-averse, AI initiatives often stall. The Chief Financial Officer (CFO) is influential from a
budget perspective – their view on ROI can make or break an AI investment. Many successful AI adopters have had CEOs
who champion technology and CIOs/CTOs who have developed an AI roadmap. We’ve observed that large integrated
systems with visionary leadership (some even creating roles like Chief Data Scientist or VP of AI) are far ahead in
adoption rates, whereas organizations without that leadership focus lag behind[21].
• Clinical Champions (Physician/Nurse Leaders): Within clinical departments, respected physicians or nurse leaders who
advocate for an AI tool can significantly sway their peers and department’s willingness to adopt. For example, a Chair of
Radiology who is pro-AI can push their department to adopt AI in imaging faster, working through kinks and getting buy-
in. Many hospitals have a Chief Medical Information Officer (CMIO) or similar role – often a physician – who bridges IT
and clinical practice; these individuals are critical in evaluating AI tools and persuading other clinicians of their value. If
the CMIO or a departmental chief says “this AI will help us and I’m on board,” that endorsement carries weight.
Conversely, if frontline clinicians are not involved or are opposed, no amount of executive push will succeed – they can
refuse to use the tool or undermine it. Thus, identifying and empowering clinical champions is key. In our Massive Bio
implementations at cancer centers, we always identify an oncologist or research nurse leader who becomes the internal
champion for our trial-matching AI; their influence helps integrate the tool into the care flow.
• Governance Committees (e.g., IT Governance, Value Analysis, Ethics Boards): Many health organizations have formal
committees that evaluate new technologies or practices. These can include a Value Analysis Committee (which reviews
new clinical products for efficacy and cost), an IT Governance Board, and sometimes specialized AI or Digital Health
Committees in more advanced systems. These bodies typically include a mix of administrators, clinicians, IT staff, and
compliance officers. They influence adoption by scrutinizing proposals for new AI tools – looking at evidence, cost,
alignment with strategic goals – and then approving or denying them for purchase or pilot. If such a body is progressive
and educated about AI, it can facilitate adoption by giving the green light and funding. But if it’s overly conservative or
unfamiliar with evaluating AI, it can be a bottleneck. We’ve seen cases where a promising AI solution gets stuck for a
year in committee deliberations over data security or uncertain clinical benefits. Strengthening these governance
processes (with clear criteria for evaluating AI, perhaps training members on AI basics) can help.
• IT Department / Chief Information Security Officer (CISO): The IT team, including security and infrastructure personnel,
heavily influence whether an AI solution can be implemented. They evaluate integration effort, cybersecurity risks, and
compatibility. A supportive CIO/CTO will allocate resources to integrate an AI system with the EHR or data warehouse; a
cautious one might cite concerns and delay integration. The CISO’s approval is often needed if an AI involves cloud or
external data sharing. We often see great ideas slowed by months due to data security reviews and legal contracting for
data use. So while these roles might not “decide” on the business need, they have veto power if risk is deemed too high
or if the project demands IT work they can’t support.
• Payers and External Decision Makers: Within provider organizations, the above are primary. But we should mention that
external actors like insurance payers or regulators can indirectly influence adoption. For example, if a payer starts
reimbursing an AI-based service, the hospital CFO is more likely to approve that project. If a regulator or accreditation
body starts requiring AI-related competencies, the CMO will push to implement AI to stay compliant. Thus, while not
inside the organization, these external forces change the internal calculus of decision makers.

• Budgeting and ROI Justification: One of the biggest hurdles is simply getting budget approval. Hospital budgets are tight,

• Procurement and Contracting Processes: Hospitals have complex procurement processes that can be a hurdle. Any new

which makes procurement committees nervous. We’ve seen several month lags in just negotiating data use agreements.
Streamlining these processes (perhaps with template AI contracts, as recommended earlier) is needed.
• Integration with Existing Systems (Technical Hurdle): Administratively, coordinating between the AI vendor and internal
IT for integration can be a hurdle. Schedules, priorities, and technical resources must align. If an AI can’t plug easily into
the EHR or workflow, it may end up as another separate system clinicians have to log into – which is a barrier to
adoption (clinicians resist multiple logins/screens). So from an admin perspective, the hurdle is marshaling the needed IT
changes or interfaces, which might require approvals from EHR vendors or internal change control processes. This is
time-consuming and sometimes costly (EHR vendors might charge for integration work). Until interoperability improves,
each new tool has this hurdle. Interoperability improvements (discussed in Q8) would directly tackle this.
• Training and Change Management: Adopting AI isn’t just a software install; it’s a process change. Administratively,
planning and executing training for staff is a hurdle. Hospitals need to schedule training sessions, develop new policies
(e.g., how to override AI recommendations, documentation of AI usage), and monitor compliance. Change management
is hard in healthcare – people are used to certain routines. Some administrators might be hesitant to introduce yet
another change given how burdened clinicians already are. Without strong training and support, an AI tool could flop, so
leadership needs to invest in that. But dedicating time for training takes providers away from clinics, which has a cost.
Some might not see it as a priority unless mandated.
• Data Governance and Privacy Concerns: Many organizations have internal rules about data sharing (like, they may not
allow PHI to be sent to cloud services, etc.). Getting approvals through privacy boards or compliance committees can be
a hurdle especially if the AI is cloud-based or requires big data transfers. There may be concern about HIPAA compliance,
and administrators in compliance/legal roles might press “pause” until thoroughly convinced. While these are legitimate
concerns, sometimes the caution is disproportionate to actual risk and slows beneficial projects.
• Measurement and Proof of Concept Requirements: Some institutions require extensive pilot studies internally before
full adoption – an administrative decision to mitigate risk. This means additional time to do an internal trial, measure
outcomes, present results to committees, etc. It’s a hurdle because it delays scaling. On the flip side, places that skip this
and jump to adoption might face backlash if something goes wrong. So it’s a careful balance.
• Cultural Resistance and Workforce Acceptance: Though not a formal administrative process, the culture is an underlying
factor. If an organization’s culture is not innovation-friendly or if staff fear AI (job displacement fears, “not invented
here” syndrome), adoption faces an uphill climb. Administrators must manage these cultural factors – e.g.,
communicating that AI is there to assist, not replace, and incorporating frontline feedback into selection and
implementation. Where culture has been addressed (through inclusive decision-making and transparency), adoption is
smoother; where it hasn’t, AI tools get silently sidelined by users.
To illustrate, consider a smaller community hospital: The CEO and CFO might be unsure about an expensive AI for ICU monitoring.
The ICU director is interested but the value committee says “show us evidence from similar hospitals.” IT says integration with
their older EHR will be tough. Meanwhile, nurses worry the AI alarms might add workload. All these need addressing by the right
leaders: the ICU director championing, the CIO ensuring integration, the CEO aligning it with strategy (like to be a high reliability
org), and the CFO perhaps agreeing to a pilot with vendor risk-sharing. If any of these pieces break down, adoption stalls.

Question 8. Interoperability Opportunities to Fuel AI Development and Widen Adoption
Q8: “Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and accelerate the development of AI
for clinical care? Please consider specific data types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.”
Interoperability – the seamless exchange of health data – is the oxygen that AI needs to breathe and flourish in clinical
care. Enhanced interoperability would have far-reaching benefits: it would enlarge the available data for AI model
training (fueling research), make it easier to deploy AI in different settings (widening market opportunities), and
enable new types of AI-driven workflows that are currently impractical (accelerating adoption). Below we outline
key areas where improved interoperability is most needed and the specific data types/standards involved:
• Integration of Electronic Health Records (EHRs) with AI Tools: A top priority is making clinical data in EHRs easily
accessible to AI developers and systems via standardized APIs. The advent of FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability
Resources) is a huge step forward. Enhanced use of FHIR (and full compliance with the 21st Century Cures Act’s
interoperability and information blocking rules) will widen opportunities by allowing third-party AI apps to plug into EHRs
securely. For example, if a hospital’s EHR exposes a FHIR API for patient demographics, problems, medications, labs, etc.,
an AI tool can fetch those in real-time to analyze and provide decision support (e.g., suggesting a clinical trial or
detecting a care gap) without custom integration. Standard FHIR profiles for specific needs (like scheduling, imaging
results, pathology reports) could let AI modules operate across many hospitals with minimal tweaks – this opens the
market for smaller innovators to serve multiple sites. ONC’s recently finalized regulations on API standards need to be
fully implemented and extended. Specifically, bulk FHIR data access (using FHIR Bulk Data / Flat FHIR) is crucial for
research and population-level AI. It allows pulling de-identified datasets of EHR records across thousands of patients for
model training or quality improvement analysis. If every EHR can export bulk data in a standard way (with proper
permissions), researchers won’t have to spend 80% of time cleaning and normalizing data – they can focus on the AI
algorithms. HHS should continue pushing bulk data standards and perhaps provide synthetic or test FHIR datasets for AI
benchmarking (like a realistic synthetic health record database on which AI can be evaluated fairly).
• Standardization of Key Clinical Data Types (e.g., Genomics, Imaging) for Interoperability: Certain high-value data types
are currently not as interoperable as they could be:
• Genomic Data: Precision medicine and AI go hand in hand, but genomic data (like DNA sequencing results, variant
interpretations) often reside in PDFs or proprietary lab portals. Adopting standards like HL7 FHIR Genomics or the
GA4GH (Global Alliance for Genomics and Health) schemas, and integrating those into EHRs, would greatly help. For
instance, the mCODE (minimal Common Oncology Data Elements) standard, built on FHIR, defines how to represent
oncology-specific data, including genomics, in a structured way[10]. If genomic results (mutations, biomarkers) are
interoperable and readily pulled into AI systems, it fuels advanced oncology AI that can identify trials or therapies
matching a patient’s molecular profile. Massive Bio’s platform uses native FHIR/mCODE integration to connect EHRs and
labs[10], which was essential for scaling our AI across different clinics. Widespread adoption of such standards will allow
any oncology AI tool to work in any oncology practice system, widening the market dramatically (currently, AI companies
have to custom-map data from each provider’s system).
• Imaging and PACS: Radiology and pathology images are typically stored in PACS systems using DICOM standards, which
is good, but making those images accessible to AI algorithms outside the local network is challenging. Enhanced

interoperability via cloud image exchanges or APIs that can provide images (with appropriate patient consent) to AI
model providers would accelerate development of imaging AI – researchers could access diverse imaging data, and
hospitals could plug in external AI services more easily. Initiatives like DICOMweb (RESTful APIs for DICOM) should be

• Unstructured Clinical Notes: A lot of important info lives in doctors’ free-text notes. Natural language processing (NLP)
AI thrives on this, but currently exchanging notes reliably is tough (CCDA documents carry some notes, but often in blob

notes in structured formats. Also, using NLP-specific standards like CTAKES annotations or FHIR Communication
resources for note content could help. Imagine if a researcher could query a network for “de-identified notes of patients
who had symptom X” through a standard service – it would enable training conversational AI or symptom-checker
models at scale.
• Patient-Generated Health Data and Devices: Data from wearables, home devices, patient-reported outcomes etc., is
increasingly valuable for AI (e.g., in chronic disease management). Interoperability here is often overlooked. Standards
like IEEE 11073 (for device data) and frameworks to integrate patient-generated data into EHRs (projects like CARIN
Alliance for consumer data) should be advanced. If these data streams can flow into the clinical record in standardized
ways, AI can analyze continuous monitoring data to predict issues. For example, interoperability standards enabling a
patient’s smart watch ECG or glucometer readings to be pulled into an AI arrhythmia or hypoglycemia prediction model
across providers would widen telehealth and remote monitoring AI use.
• Interoperability to Link Clinical and Research Data: A major opportunity is connecting clinical care data with clinical trial
and research data. For instance, clinical trial registries (like ClinicalTrials.gov) are a rich source of eligibility criteria and
trial availability, but historically it’s been separate from EHR data. If criteria could be standardized (there are efforts to
use FHIR for trial eligibility criteria) and patient data can be matched to them systematically, AI can automate trial
matching at scale. Massive Bio does this by parsing ClinicalTrials.gov and matching with patient EHR data; the more
those formats are standardized, the more efficient and universal this becomes[40][7]. Enhanced interoperability
between healthcare and research also means if a patient consents, their EHR data could flow into research databases
(e.g., for learning health networks). HHS could encourage adoption of standards like OMOP common data model to allow
easier pooling of multi-center data for AI training. Also, consider linking claims data with clinical data – standards to
integrate Medicare claims or payer data with EHR data on the fly could allow AI to factor in cost and utilization patterns,
which might reveal insights about quality and efficiency (like identifying high-cost patient trajectories that could be
improved).
• Benchmarking Tools and Shared Datasets: The question also mentions benchmarking tools. Interoperability can enable
shared benchmark datasets for AI. For example, if multiple health systems can contribute de-identified data to a central
repository because they all speak the same data standards language, you can create a national “benchmark dataset” for,
say, diabetic patient outcomes, on which AI models from different vendors can be benchmarked. A current challenge is
that each AI vendor might test on their own proprietary dataset – not apples-to-apples. HHS could coordinate creation of
reference datasets (with patient privacy preserved) that are continuously updated via interoperable feeds from diverse
sources. These could be used to benchmark algorithm performance fairly. Perhaps the NIH or AHRQ could host such
repositories (some exist, like MIMIC for ICU data, but expanding to other domains).
• Enhanced Interoperability in Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) Data: Often overlooked, but SDOH (transportation,
housing, social support data) is critical for AI to truly understand patient context and improve outcomes. We need
standards (HL7 Gravity Project is defining some) to record and share SDOH in health records. If an AI can access
standardized SDOH data (like food insecurity status, or transportation needs), it can better tailor interventions and
identify at-risk patients. For instance, Massive Bio’s ACS ACTS program not only matches trials but also identifies social

Question 9. Patient and Caregiver Challenges for AI to Address – and Their Concerns about AI Adoption
Q9: “What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the adoption and use of AI in
clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?”
From the patient and caregiver perspective, AI is not about algorithms – it’s about tangible improvements in their
healthcare experience and outcomes. Massive Bio engages directly with patients (particularly cancer patients
and their families) through our services, and we have gleaned insights into what they hope AI can solve and
what worries them about AI.
Challenges Patients/Caregivers Want AI to Address:
• Access to the Right Care and Information: One of the biggest patient pain points is navigating the healthcare system and
finding the best treatment options. Patients want AI to help cut through the complexity. For example, cancer patients
often ask, “Is there a clinical trial or a new therapy that could help me?” Many feel overwhelmed trying to research on
their own[45]. They would love AI-driven tools that proactively present them with personalized options. Our ACS ACTS
partnership is an example: patients fill a simple form and our AI matching provides tailored trial options, which patients
find empowering[46][45]. Patients desire this kind of personalized guidance at critical decision points – which hospital is
best for my condition, which treatment path likely fits me, etc. In general, they hope AI can democratize expertise, so
that even if they aren’t at a top academic center, they still get cutting-edge recommendations. Caregivers, likewise, want
tools that help them help their loved ones – e.g., an AI that could summarize a patient’s condition and list questions to
ask the doctor, or predict care needs.

• Timely and Accurate Diagnoses: Patients often express frustration about missed or delayed diagnoses. They would like
AI to assist in catching problems earlier. For instance, in radiology or pathology, if AI can detect a tumor or abnormality
that a busy clinician might overlook, patients see value – it could mean an earlier cancer diagnosis or avoiding a missed
finding. Particularly in fields like rare diseases, patients want AI to shorten the diagnostic odyssey by recognizing patterns
in symptoms that might point to a condition sooner than a human would.
• Better Coordinated Care and Reduced Administrative Burdens on Them: Navigating appointments, refills, referrals –
these are pain points patients hope AI can ease. They want the health system to be more coordinated and responsive.
An example challenge: a patient might see multiple specialists and feels like they have to retell their story each time and
chase after records. They would be thrilled if AI could integrate their information so every provider is up to date (a
function of interoperability and AI summarization). Also, tasks like scheduling or medication refills – patients would love
intelligent systems that make these seamless (e.g., an AI that knows when you’re due for a check-up or notices you
haven’t picked up a prescription and sends a reminder or help). This ties to quality of life; patients often feel they are
project managers of their own care – AI could lighten that load by proactively handling some logistics.
• Improved Treatment Experiences and Outcomes: Ultimately, patients and caregivers want better health outcomes –
longer survival, less pain, improved quality of life. They see AI as a potential means to tailor treatments (minimizing trial-
and-error) and to manage chronic conditions better day-to-day. For example, a diabetic patient might want an AI coach
that helps keep their blood sugar stable with personalized tips, or an AI that predicts a flare-up of a condition so they can
act early. Caregivers of elderly or chronically ill patients might want AI monitoring that alerts them or providers if
something is off (like subtle signs of stroke or deterioration in a dementia patient), preventing serious events. Essentially,
they hope AI can provide an “early warning system” and continuous support that current periodic visits cannot.
• Equity in Access: Patients in underserved areas or from marginalized communities often voice that they lack access to
specialists or trials. They hope AI can bridge that gap – e.g., via telemedicine AI or remote monitoring that connects them
to top expertise. They also want assurance that treatments are designed for people like them. For instance, minority
patients might feel more confident if AI can help ensure the care they receive is based on data that includes people of
their background (avoiding one-size-fits-all medicine).
To sum up the challenges: finding the right care faster, coordinating that care, and personalizing it to improve outcomes – these
are what patients really care about. AI that addresses these will be embraced. For example, ACS ACTS users appreciate that the
service helps them “navigate clinical trials and find the best treatment options available”, which was previously overwhelming[47].
Concerns Patients/Caregivers Have about AI:
• Privacy and Data Security: A foremost concern is “What happens to my data?” Patients worry that if AI systems are
pulling together their health information, it could be misused or fall into the wrong hands. They want to know that their
personal details and medical history remain confidential. There’s fear of hacking or leaks. For instance, if an AI is
analyzing their genomic data or insurance records, they ask: will this be shared with third parties, could it affect my
insurance coverage, or could an employer somehow access it? They need reassurance through transparency and strong
privacy protections that AI won’t compromise their confidentiality[48]. Our experience is that patients are often willing
to share data if it helps their care or others, but they want to actively consent and have control. So consent and clear
communication is key.
• Loss of Human Touch and Personal Interaction: Many patients fear healthcare could become impersonal if AI takes on
too much. The human connection with doctors and nurses is a source of comfort; there’s concern that AI might replace
that or make providers overly reliant on screens and algorithms. For example, an elderly patient might worry that a
doctor is paying more attention to an AI recommendation than to listening to them. They want assurance that AI is an
assistant, not a replacement – that it will free clinicians to spend more time caring and talking by handling routine tasks,
rather than erecting a wall of technology. The idea of a “robot doctor” can be intimidating; most patients still want a
human in the loop who has empathy and can interpret the AI through the lens of their individual story.
• Bias and Fairness: Patients, especially from historically underserved groups, are concerned that AI could be biased or
make unfair decisions[49]. They may have heard that algorithms sometimes work less well for minorities or women if not
properly designed. So a Black patient might be wary of an AI risk score dictating their care if they suspect it might
underestimate their needs due to biased training data. Similarly, some could fear discrimination – e.g., could AI deem
someone an “expensive” patient and limit their care (this touches on ethical concerns about cost-optimization possibly
conflicting with patient interest). They want AI to be a tool for health equity, not something that inadvertently deepens
disparities. Transparency about how AI makes decisions is crucial to alleviate this – patients say things like, “I don’t want
a computer to decide I’m not worth treating aggressively because of some statistic.” Thus, explaining that AI is checked
for bias and is meant to complement, not deny, care is important.

• Accuracy and Safety of AI Decisions: There is a general concern about AI making mistakes – “What if the AI is wrong?”
Many patients and caregivers are not automatically trusting of technology; they need proof that these tools are accurate
and won’t lead them astray. If an AI misses something important or gives a false alarm, it could harm them. For example,
if an AI reads an X-ray and misses a fracture, that’s a worry; or if an AI recommends a treatment that turns out
suboptimal, who is accountable? Patients want to know that AI systems are rigorously tested and that there’s oversight.
Caregivers might ask, “Will the doctor double-check what the AI says?” The concept of accountability is key – knowing
that the physician or institution still takes responsibility and that there are processes if the AI is wrong (like second
opinions or the ability to override).
• Understanding and Consent: Patients may feel anxious if AI is used in their care without their knowledge. Some worry
about being “experimented on” by unproven tech. They want to be informed that AI is involved and ideally to agree to it.
This is both a concern and a desire – they want to be part of the decision to use AI, similar to how they consent for
treatments. Also, they often won’t understand how an AI works (and many don’t need the technical detail), but they at
least want a basic explanation in plain language of why the AI’s suggestion is being made. Lack of understanding can
breed mistrust or even fear of the unknown.
• Cost and Access Concerns: Some patients wonder, will AI-driven care cost me more? If a fancy AI recommends some
high-tech test or sends me to a big hospital, can I afford it or will insurance cover it? Conversely, they might fear that AI
is being used by insurance to cut costs at the expense of care (like an AI might deny them a procedure by deeming it
unnecessary). This interplay of cost is on their minds. They want AI to reduce their out-of-pocket costs (maybe by
avoiding unneeded procedures), not to be an extra charge or a gatekeeper for coverage. We’ve heard patients
appreciate when our trial matching finds them options that could be free via a trial, thus saving them potential huge
drug costs – that’s a positive of AI linking to trials. But they would dislike if an AI recommended against a treatment
purely on cost grounds.
Addressing these concerns is vital for acceptance. In our practice, we emphasize that patients remain in control – they consent to
our services and data use, and we keep them informed. For example, ACS ACTS explicitly states it's an “AI-driven clinical trial
matching service” but under the guidance of ACS cancer information specialists[46][50], blending AI and human support to
maintain trust. We take privacy seriously; patients trust ACS and by extension our system because we uphold confidentiality and
they actively sign up for it. Ensuring trust and transparency is foundational.
In designing AI in clinical care, we should involve patient input early. HHS could encourage user-centered design,
including patients in testing. When patients see AI solving their problems – like reducing wait times, simplifying
their journey, or offering hope via new options – they become enthusiastic. But if they perceive AI as an imposed,
opaque system that might compromise their privacy or reduce human care, they will resist.
To sum up, patients and caregivers hope AI will make care more accessible, personalized, and coordinated, helping them
navigate complexity and catch problems early. They want it to amplify the good (better outcomes, more
knowledge) and minimize the bad (bureaucracy, errors). On the flip side, they worry about privacy, losing human
connection, bias, safety, and agency. Any strategy to implement AI at scale should incorporate safeguards and
education around these points: robust privacy protections, maintaining the clinician-patient relationship at the
core, demonstrable fairness and thorough validation of AI tools, and open communication/consent. If we get this
right, patients will not just accept AI – they will demand it, because it will be clear it serves their needs and respects
their values.
Question 10. Priority Areas for AI Research and Evidence – Literature on AI Impact and Economic Aspects
Q10: “Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the adoption of AI as part of clinical care?

Priority Areas for AI Research (to Accelerate Adoption in Clinical Care):
HHS should prioritize research that addresses the gaps between promising AI technology and its practical,
effective implementation in healthcare. Based on the current landscape, we recommend focusing on:
1. Implementation Science for AI in Healthcare: It’s not enough to invent AI algorithms; we need to understand how to
integrate them into real-world clinical settings successfully. HHS should fund research on best practices for deploying AI
in various environments (large academic hospitals, small rural clinics, etc.), including workflow design, change
management, and training methods. This research should capture lessons from both successful and failed
implementations to create playbooks for health systems. For example, studying how an AI sepsis alert was rolled out
across multiple hospitals – what adoption strategies led to usage and improved outcomes versus where it was ignored –
will yield general principles. The outcomes could be guidelines or toolkits for health organizations. This directly

accelerates adoption by turning AI from a theoretical tool into one that is effectively used. HHS’s RFI itself mentions
strengthening implementation science and best practices, especially for complex scenarios[17], highlighting this as a
recognized need.
2. Evaluation and Monitoring Frameworks (Safety, Efficacy, Bias): Research should focus on developing standardized
methods to evaluate AI tools (as we discussed in Q4). This includes metrics for clinical impact, user trust, and economic
outcomes, as well as processes for continuous monitoring post-deployment. Part of this could be creating open datasets
or simulation environments for testing AI (essentially science around how to test AI well). If HHS invests in these, it gives
regulators and adopters confidence in AI, speeding up adoption. It also helps sift out ineffective or harmful tools early.
An example: researching how to do randomized controlled trials or other study designs for AI interventions (there is
ongoing work on cluster RCTs for AI, or adaptive trials where AI is the “intervention”).
3. Bias Mitigation and Fairness in AI: Prioritize research into algorithms that detect and correct bias, as well as methods to
ensure AI tools are equitable. This includes creating diverse training datasets and novel techniques like transfer learning
or federated learning to include data from underrepresented groups without breaching privacy. Also, sociotechnical
research on how biases manifest in AI-driven workflows and how to mitigate them through both technical and
organizational means. The goal is AI that works for all patient populations. For example, HHS could sponsor research to
improve AI for maternal health in Black women, given disparities, or AI in rural health contexts. Published findings have
shown biases (like the care management algorithm example[49]); now research must figure out how to systematically
avoid such biases. This is crucial for trust and broad utility of AI, which underpins adoption.
4. Interoperability and Data Sharing Models: Research how to safely and effectively share data for AI development –
including privacy-preserving analytics, federated learning, and the creation of high-quality synthetic data. HHS should
prioritize research that enables multi-center AI studies without violating privacy (e.g., federated learning pilots across
healthcare systems in a network). Also, research on the standardization of health data for AI (like refining FHIR for AI
needs, or extending mCODE) is important. This can inform policy and standards to make data more usable by AI, which
as we established will fuel AI advancement.
5. AI for Public Health and High-Burden Conditions: HHS might focus on AI research in areas that are national priorities: for
example, chronic diseases that drive cost (like diabetes, heart failure), mental health (like AI for suicide prevention or
substance abuse support), aging-related conditions (frailty, dementia as mentioned[38]), and health crises (like
pandemic response). By prioritizing these, HHS ensures AI is developed where it can have broad impact. For instance, AI
research in geriatrics to predict and prevent falls in elderly or manage polypharmacy could greatly improve quality of life
and reduce costs.
6. Human-AI Interaction & Explainability Research: To enhance trust, research should delve into how AI can effectively
communicate with clinicians and patients. This means studying different explanation approaches (visual aids, confidence
levels, etc.) and how they affect decision-making and outcomes. Also, research on how clinicians make use of AI
recommendations (when do they accept vs override, and why) can guide design improvements. This human factors angle
is sometimes overlooked but is vital for safe and accepted AI.
7. Economic Impact Analysis of AI in Healthcare: We need rigorous health economics research on AI – how it shifts costs
and benefits across stakeholders. This includes micro-level (does adopting AI in X department save money or shift labor?)
and macro-level (if AI reduces hospitalizations for heart failure by 20%, what is the net system effect?). Also, “transfers”
of costs – e.g., does AI reduce provider costs but increase patient or payer costs, or vice versa? HHS should fund studies
and models that simulate these scenarios. This evidence will inform reimbursement and investment decisions. It aligns
with question part (b) on costs and benefits.

(b) Literature on Costs, Benefits, and Transfers of AI in Clinical Care:
The health economics literature is evolving. Key points from what exists:
• Many studies use modeling and simulation to estimate potential cost savings of AI. For instance, a Morgan Stanley
report projected massive system-wide savings (like that $300–$900 billion by 2050 figure) by extrapolating current
efficiency gains across the system[36]. PwC’s analysis predicted over $1 trillion could shift to digital/AI health by
2035[53]. These are forward-looking and make assumptions, but they provide a macro perspective that AI, if widely
used, can bend the cost curve by automating labor, reducing errors, and enabling cheaper virtual care.
• Microsimulation analyses have been done for specific use cases. For example, an analysis might look at an AI for
detecting diabetic retinopathy: it might factor the cost of the AI screening vs the benefit of preventing blindness and find
a favorable cost-effectiveness ratio. Similarly, some cost-benefit analyses have been done on AI for stroke triage,
showing that faster treatment yields savings by preventing disability (translating to fewer rehab/nursing home costs,
etc.). These often appear in specialty journals.
• Some real-world cost evaluations: A study on a hospital’s AI sepsis program might show the program cost X to
implement, but saved Y in ICU days and penalties for sepsis outcomes, yielding net savings. If Y > X, ROI is positive. Some
such data are emerging in conference proceedings or implementation case studies. On transfers: for instance, if AI
reduces hospital admissions, hospitals lose revenue (under fee-for-service) while payers save money – that’s a transfer
of benefits that literature notes. Conversely, under value-based care, the hospital as ACO saves costs and benefits. So,
context matters.
• Productivity and labor transfer: Another angle literature discusses is who benefits from efficiency. If AI cuts down
documentation time (benefit to physician time, which could see more patients or go home earlier), the “financial”
benefit might not directly appear in a ledger, but it could reduce burnout and staff turnover (which is a cost if they
leave). These indirect benefits are discussed qualitatively and increasingly quantitatively (some studies tie decreased
burnout to retention rates, etc.).
• The literature calls for more robust evidence generation. Recognizing this, journals like NEJM Catalyst, JAMIA, BMJ
Quality & Safety have called for publishing not just accuracy of AI but impact on workflow and cost. There are now
frameworks for assessing health AI similar to drug HTA (Health Technology Assessment), incorporating cost-
effectiveness.
In terms of transfers, one key observation in literature: AI might incur upfront costs (software, training, maybe increased imaging if
AI finds more nodules that need follow-up) and savings come later (fewer late-stage cancers to treat, etc.). So, the timeline of
costs/benefits differ among stakeholders. The literature often frames this in terms of who invests vs who gains. For example,

HHS’s Role in Research and Evidence:
HHS should fill the evidence gaps by funding and possibly coordinating multi-site evaluations. For instance, to
understand economic impact, HHS could run or fund a demonstration where a certain AI is implemented in
several diverse sites with analysis of clinical and financial outcomes.
We note that to date, many promising results appear in single-site studies or retrospective analyses. The next step
is more rigorous prospective multi-center studies. HHS is well positioned to drive this via agencies like AHRQ
(for safety and quality studies), NIH (for clinical effectiveness trials of AI), and CMS (through demonstration
projects or model tests focusing on AI).
Also, HHS should prioritize areas that combine high unmet need, potential for AI to help, and alignment with
national initiatives (e.g., Cancer Moonshot, Opioid crisis, rural health). For example, research on AI-assisted

Conclusion of Question 10:
In conclusion, HHS should focus research on bridging the “last mile” of AI adoption: implementation science,
evaluation methods, bias mitigation, interoperability, and strategic high-need clinical domains. Published
evidence so far is promising but not yet comprehensive; it shows AI can improve outcomes and efficiency in
specific instances[34][36], but we need broader validation and economic analysis. Literature on cost-benefit
suggests significant long-term savings and better care, but also points out that benefits may accrue to
different parties than the costs – implying a need for HHS to align incentives (through payment models or
policy) so that those investing in AI also share in the gains. By prioritizing research that produces robust
evidence and frameworks, HHS will build the foundation of trust and knowledge necessary for widespread AI
adoption in clinical care.
