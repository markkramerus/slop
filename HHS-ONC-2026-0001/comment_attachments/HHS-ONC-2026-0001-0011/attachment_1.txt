Opening statement
This submission responds directly to each of the ten questions in the HHS Health Sector AI RFI
and is structured to support cross-program review. Each response stands alone while also
referencing related questions where system-level dependencies exist. The intent is not to
recommend a single policy outcome, but to surface implementation realities, governance gaps,
and second-order effects that emerge when AI systems are deployed within real clinical
workflows.
A workflow-to-entity crosswalk is included to clarify how responsibilities intersect across HHS
StaffDivs and OpDivs and to help avoid gaps between policy intent and operational execution.
EHY Consulting LLC respectfully submits these comments in alignment with the HHS Artificial
Intelligence Strategy to support informed, non-prescriptive decision-making across the health
sector. A consistent finding across AI deployments in clinical environments is that adoption and
safety are determined less by model accuracy in controlled settings than by how AI systems
interact with real-world clinical workflows, governance structures, data stewardship practices,
procurement decisions, and learning feedback loops.
Clinical AI functions as a socio-technical system: it shapes clinician behavior, redistributes
responsibility, and generates compounding learning from data produced through routine
operations. Accordingly, this submission answers each RFI question directly while cross-
referencing related responses to keep system-level implications visible and to inform
approaches to scaling clinical AI responsibly.

Cross-cutting note: acquisition is the control plane that converts policy intent into enforceable
obligations. Interoperability, cloud use, vendor access, and model-update practices are
ultimately determined by contract terms, monitoring rights, and operational controls.
Cross-cutting analytic lens: data, workflows, and learning
Practical governance test for clinical AI deployments
Use case and end-user clarity: identify the decision-maker, operator, and accountable party;
specify the action taken because of the model output.
Data lifecycle boundaries: define what data enters, what leaves, retention periods, and what is
used for care delivery versus model improvement.
Learning accumulation and sovereignty controls: specify whether operational data may be used
for model improvement and under what constraints; treat derived artifacts (e.g., embeddings,
fine-tunes, prompts, logs, telemetry) as governed outputs.
Security posture and access paths: enforce identity, least privilege, logging, incident response,
and subcontractor controls; define cloud and remote access defaults with audit rights.
Bias introduction points and drift: identify where bias can enter (training data, workflow
selection, labeling, clinician reliance, feedback loops) and require continuous monitoring and
rollback.
Across multiple clinical domains (including high-impact areas such as precision oncology and
advanced therapies), the most durable advantage—and the most consequential vulnerability—

often lies not in the AI model or the molecule, but in control over the learning system: the
workflow-generated data, outcomes, failure modes, and process metadata that enable iterative
optimization over time.
Therefore, it is useful to distinguish:
• Privacy: who can see my data.
• Sovereignty: who determines what my data means and how much it is worth.
• Security: who has access to my data and what they can do with it.
Many governance approaches address privacy and baseline security, but do not explicitly govern
learning accumulation (how data becomes compounding capability). In clinical AI, the latter
increasingly determines long-run trust, competitiveness, and resilience.
Responses to RFI questions (aligned to Federal Register ‘Specific
Questions’)
Each response below restates the Federal Register question verbatim (or with minimal
formatting) and provides a direct answer. Cross-references are included where useful for
synthesis across HHS teams.
1. Biggest barriers to private sector innovation and adoption/use in clinical care
Clinical AI frequently crosses mismatched risk frameworks (privacy, cybersecurity, clinical safety,
enterprise risk, and vendor product risk). These frameworks do not align by default, and gaps
emerge at their seams unless governance is explicitly integrated.
Workflow misfit is the dominant barrier. AI systems that perform well in development can fail in
practice when they add documentation burden, disrupt clinician cognitive flow, or create
ambiguous responsibility at the point of care.
Key barriers include:
• Unclear accountability for AI-influenced decisions, especially when AI is adaptive or when
vendor updates materially change behavior.
• Trust gaps driven by opacity, inconsistent performance across subpopulations, and unclear
post-deployment monitoring.
• Data provenance and integration challenges (EHR heterogeneity, fragmented identifiers,
incomplete documentation, variable labeling).
• Operational and security constraints (network segmentation, device integration, patch
management, identity and access management).
• Procurement realities: contracts that do not specify auditability, model-update controls,
data-use boundaries, or subcontractor constraints.
See also: Q2, Q3, Q4, Q7, Q8.

2. Regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes; CFR citations
where applicable
Condition participation or incentives on auditability (minimum logs, update provenance, and
model-change notification).
Make data-use boundaries enforceable, clarifying whether operational data may be used for
model improvement and under what authorizations.
Across regulation, payment policy, and program design, the most leverage comes from making
governance and monitoring enforceable at the workflow level while avoiding incentives for
superficial AI adoption.
Priority areas for HHS to consider include:
• CMS-aligned incentives that reward measurable workflow value and outcomes rather than
mere AI integration (see Q6).
• Programmatic designs that require lifecycle governance (monitoring, drift management,
change control) as a condition of participation in demonstrations, quality programs, or
certifications (see Q4 and Q5).
• Clear, enforceable expectations for data stewardship, auditability, and
vendor/subcontractor controls, implemented through acquisition and contracting
requirements where feasible (see Q3 and Q5).
• Interoperability policies that distinguish data exchange for care delivery from secondary use
for model training and learning accumulation (see Q8).
See also: If HHS seeks specific CFR citations, a targeted follow-on request scoped to particular
programs (e.g., Medicare conditions of participation, quality reporting, certification programs)
would enable more precise citations by commenters.
3. Non-medical devices: novel legal and implementation issues; HHS role
Governance and contracts should explicitly cover workflow metadata and derived artifacts
(prompts, embeddings, fine-tunes, telemetry), which often represent the primary signals of
value and intent rather than mere exhaust.
For non-medical device AI used in clinical care (e.g., workflow, triage, documentation,
scheduling, or administrative decision support), key issues include liability allocation,
indemnification, privacy/security obligations, and governance of adaptive behavior post-
deployment.
Novel legal and implementation issues commonly arise from:
• Ambiguity in responsibility when AI influences clinician behavior or care pathways without
being a regulated medical device.

• Contracting and indemnification gaps that fail to define change control, performance
representations, or audit rights.
• Privacy/security frameworks that govern access and disclosure, but do not explicitly govern
learning accumulation or model improvement using operational clinical data.
• Third-party and subcontractor risk (cloud hosting, analytics, support), especially when access
paths are opaque.
See also: HHS can help by publishing model contract clauses and minimum auditability/lifecycle
governance expectations usable by providers and payers, aligned across OCR, ASTP/ONC,
CIO/ONS, and CMS.
4. Non-medical devices: evaluation methods pre- and post-deployment;
mechanisms
Evaluate impact on clinician behavior, including automation bias, alert fatigue, de-skilling, and
reliance shifts over time.
The most promising evaluation approaches combine pre-deployment validation with post-
deployment monitoring that is sensitive to workflow effects, site-to-site variation, and
distribution shift.
Recommended evaluation components include:
• Human-centered workflow evaluation (cognitive load, alert fatigue, override rates, time-to-
task, downstream workflow changes).
• Robustness and stress testing across settings, subpopulations, and data quality conditions.
• Post-deployment drift detection and performance monitoring linked to outcomes where
feasible.
• Change-control governance for model updates, including criteria for rollback.
See also: HHS can support these via contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and prize
competitions (often with AHRQ/NIH/CMS partners), prioritizing monitoring, auditability, and
workflow-aware evaluation methods.
5. Support for accreditation, certification, testing, and credentialing
Establish a minimum governance checklist for certification programs, including update control,
monitoring, disclosure of data use for training, and incident response.
HHS can amplify private-sector accreditation, certification, industry-driven testing, and
credentialing by helping establish baseline expectations for trustworthy clinical AI that are
measurable and auditable.
Support could include:

• Reference expectations for auditability (logging, traceability of inputs/outputs, update
history) and minimum cybersecurity posture for clinical AI deployments.
• Credentialing frameworks for AI governance roles within health care organizations (see Q7).
• Model contract language and procurement checklists that incorporate security-by-design
and lifecycle monitoring requirements (see Q3 and Q4).
• Public-private benchmarking and validation resources, including shared evaluation protocols
and testbeds (see Q8 and Q10).
6. Where AI met/exceeded vs fell short; novel tools with greatest potential
Distinguish value created for patients and providers from value transferred to vendors through
learning accumulation from operational data.
AI tends to meet or exceed expectations when it is tightly coupled to well-defined workflows
with clear success metrics (e.g., reducing administrative burden, improving triage consistency,
accelerating access, or reducing preventable harm). It tends to fall short when deployed as a
generic overlay without governance, monitoring, or integration into decision rights.
High-potential tool categories include:
• Workflow-aware clinical documentation support that measurably reduces administrative
burden without introducing silent errors.
• Risk stratification and triage tools with clear accountability and monitoring, especially in
resource-constrained settings.
• Tools that surface quality insights by analyzing workflow metadata and outcomes (not only
clinical text).
• Patient-facing navigation and care coordination supports that improve adherence and
reduce friction across care settings (see Q9).
See also: To accelerate learning on ‘what works,’ HHS can support standardized reporting and
privacy-preserving benchmarking (see Q10).
7. Organizational roles and administrative hurdles for adoption
Adoption decisions are typically shaped by clinical leadership, operational leadership,
compliance/privacy, IT/security leadership, and (increasingly) AI governance committees or
enterprise risk management functions.
Primary administrative hurdles include:
• Unclear ownership for AI lifecycle management and monitoring across clinical, IT, and
compliance functions.
• Procurement bottlenecks and contract limitations that impede auditability or change
control.
• Data access and interoperability friction across EHRs and ancillary systems (see Q8).
• Training, credentialing, and change management requirements for clinicians and staff.

• Security approvals and network integration constraints, especially where tools require cloud
connectivity.
See also: This is why HHS-wide coordination across CMS, ASTP/ONC, OCR, CIO/ONS, OGA, and
acquisition functions is essential.
8. Interoperability opportunities; data types, standards, and benchmarking
tools
Interoperability expands the attack surface and secondary-use risk unless paired with access
control, provenance, and enforceable training boundaries.
Enhanced interoperability can widen market opportunities and accelerate AI where it improves
data provenance, reduces integration friction, and enables benchmarking—while still respecting
governance boundaries on secondary use and learning accumulation.
Priority opportunities include:
• Standardized representations of clinical workflows and outcomes (including
provenance/lineage metadata).
• Consistent patient identity and record linkage across care settings.
• Data standards and benchmarking tools that support monitoring and post-deployment
evaluation (see Q4).
• Mechanisms for privacy-preserving benchmarking and federated evaluation where direct
pooling is impractical.
See also: Interoperability policy should explicitly distinguish exchange for care delivery from
secondary use for model training and learning accumulation.
9. Patient/caregiver desired challenges and concerns
Patients increasingly want clarity not only on who sees their data, but who benefits from
derived learning and what recourse exists.
Patients and caregivers often prioritize improvements that reduce friction, shorten wait times,
improve communication, and increase transparency about decisions affecting care access and
quality.
Common concerns include:
• Privacy and security of sensitive health information, including secondary uses beyond direct
care.
• Unclear accountability when AI influences clinical decisions or access to services.
• Bias and inequitable treatment, especially when monitoring and recourse mechanisms are
weak.

• Reduced human agency or difficulty obtaining explanations, corrections, and escalation
pathways.
See also: These concerns reinforce the need for lifecycle governance, auditability, and clear
decision rights (see Q3–Q5).
10. AI research priorities; literature on impact and costs/benefits/transfers
Prioritize methods to measure learning accumulation and value transfer across stakeholders,
and governance models that integrate privacy, sovereignty, and security operationally.
HHS research priorities that would accelerate responsible adoption include implementation
science for clinical AI, workflow-aware evaluation methods, and scalable approaches to
monitoring and governance.
Priority research areas include:
• Methods for post-deployment monitoring, drift detection, and change control in
heterogeneous clinical settings.
• Human-centered evaluation methods that quantify workflow impact and clinician reliance
over time.
• Privacy-preserving benchmarking, federated evaluation, and secure data-sharing
architectures.
• Governance models for adaptive systems that integrate privacy, sovereignty, and security
considerations.
See also: HHS can help curate and translate literature into practical evaluation playbooks and
clarify how costs/benefits/value transfer are measured across providers, payers, vendors, and
patients.
