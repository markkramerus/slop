HHS Health Sector AI RFI Response – SANCIAN LLC
Executive Summary
SANCIAN LLC submits this response to HHS's Request for Information on accelerating AI
adoption in clinical care. Drawing on over 15 years of experience implementing AI
governance across federal health agencies—including HHS, CDC, FDA, and NIH—we offer
an operational framework for translating AI policy into sustainable clinical practice.
Core Position
HHS should treat AI not as technology to be adopted, but as care infrastructure to be
governed. The gap between AI potential and AI reality is not a technology problem—it is a
governance, implementation, and human factors problem.
Five Principles for Acceleration
• Governance-first adoption. Establish accountability structures before technology
procurement. Abstract frameworks fail at the point of care; organizations need
actionable playbooks.
• Readiness before reimbursement. Require organizations to demonstrate governance
maturity as a prerequisite for AI-related payment incentives. Premature adoption
without readiness wastes resources and erodes trust.
• Payment aligned to burden reduction. Incentivize AI that saves clinician time,
reduces documentation burden, and improves access—not simply reward adoption of
new technology.
• Implementation science over model development. Fund research on real-world
deployment, workflow integration, and adoption drivers—not just algorithmic
performance in controlled settings.
• Human capacity as a success metric. Measure cognitive load reduction, time
returned to patient care, and clinician trust. AI that adds burden—even if accurate—
fails the people it aims to serve.
Why Now
With accelerating federal AI strategies, workforce strain, and rising care complexity, the next
24–36 months represent a narrow window to embed governance and human-centered design
before AI becomes irreversible infrastructure. Rural, safety-net, and underserved settings face
the greatest risk of being left behind—or harmed—if equity is not embedded from the start.
SANCIAN's Contribution
This response offers operational frameworks HHS could pilot or scale, including:
organizational readiness assessment (AI ReadyCheck™), equity impact assessment (AI-
EQUITYClear™), governance maturity models, named accountability structures (AI
Executive Owner), and human-centered metrics aligned to our AI SoftLife™ philosophy. We
stand ready to support HHS in translating these recommendations into implementation.

Question 1: Barriers to Private Sector Innovation and Adoption
RFI Question: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care
and its adoption and use in clinical care?
Observed Barriers
Fragmented governance and unclear accountability. Organizations face conflicting
guidance across HHS divisions, with no clarity on which agency's rules apply—particularly
for non-device AI outside FDA's traditional scope. This uncertainty delays procurement and
suppresses investment.
Low organizational readiness. Gaps extend beyond technology to data governance,
workflow optimization, change management, and leadership alignment. Organizations
underestimate the transformation required, leading to failed implementations that erode
confidence in future AI initiatives.
Trust gaps across stakeholders. Clinicians, patients, and administrators express legitimate
concerns about bias, safety, explainability, and liability that current deployments have not
adequately addressed.
Clinician burnout and workflow friction. Consistent with our human-capacity principle, AI
that adds steps or cognitive load creates friction rather than relief—regardless of technical
performance. Exhausted care teams cannot absorb poorly designed tools.
Procurement complexity and reimbursement uncertainty. Traditional processes add 6–18
months to adoption timelines. Unclear ROI projections make business cases difficult to build.
SANCIAN Response
We address these barriers through AI ReadyCheck™ (organizational readiness baseline),
governance-first design aligned to NIST AI RMF, workflow co-design with clinician input,
and equity impact assessment (AI-EQUITYClear™) before deployment.
HHS Opportunity
Standardize readiness and governance expectations across programs. Encourage "readiness
before reimbursement"—ensuring organizations demonstrate governance maturity as a
prerequisite for AI-related payment incentives. Frameworks like those we implement at
SANCIAN demonstrate how this can work operationally.

Question 2: Regulatory, Payment Policy, and Programmatic Changes
RFI Question: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should
HHS prioritize?
Regulatory Recommendations
Create a tiered, risk-based AI governance pathway aligned with NIST AI RMF risk tiers.
Lower-risk administrative AI could follow streamlined pathways; higher-risk clinical
decision support would require rigorous evaluation. This provides predictability while
maintaining proportionate oversight.
Issue guidance for non-device clinical AI accountability. Publish model accountability
frameworks addressing roles, responsibilities, and oversight. Relevant touchpoints: ONC's
Health IT Certification Program (45 CFR Part 170), Conditions of Participation (42 CFR
Parts 482–485).
Enable pilots with real-world evidence requirements. Structured pilots that integrate AI
into care pathways with mandatory evidence collection would build the evidence base for
broader adoption.
Reimbursement Recommendations
Consistent with our payment-alignment principle, CMS should incentivize AI that delivers
measurable value:
• Clinician time saved — Reward AI that reduces documentation and administrative
burden.
• Improved access and continuity — Support AI that expands access to underserved
and rural populations.
• Value-based AI add-ons — Enable payment adjustments under MSSP, ACO
REACH, and bundled payments when AI contributes to outcomes.
The current fee schedule (42 CFR Part 414) requires new CPT codes or modifiers for AI-
assisted services. Our governance playbooks are designed for alignment with emerging
requirements.

Question 3: Novel Legal and Implementation Issues
RFI Question: For non-medical devices, what novel legal and implementation issues exist
and what role should HHS play?
Key Issues
Shared liability. Liability allocation among AI developers, healthcare organizations, and
clinicians remains unclear. Traditional malpractice frameworks assume human decision-
makers—AI disrupts these assumptions.
Auditability of adaptive models. Organizations need mechanisms to audit model behavior,
track changes, and demonstrate deployed models match validated versions.
Data provenance, consent, and security. Questions persist regarding training data sources,
patient consent, and AI-specific cybersecurity vulnerabilities. HIPAA (45 CFR Parts 160,
164) requires interpretation for AI contexts.
Indemnification gaps. Standard vendor contracts do not adequately address AI-specific risks
including performance guarantees, bias warranties, and monitoring obligations.
SANCIAN Approach
We implement role-based accountability matrices, continuous monitoring with model risk
logs, and governance charters embedded in operations—not as standalone policy documents.
HHS Role
Convene cross-sector standards development for liability and auditability. Publish reference
governance architectures. Provide model contract provisions or safe harbor guidance. HHS
could pilot reference architectures such as those we implement to demonstrate operational
feasibility.

Question 4: AI Evaluation Methods
RFI Question: What are the most promising evaluation methods, metrics, and human-
centered evaluation methods? Should HHS support these?
What Works
Pre-deployment: Bias testing across demographic subgroups, scenario-based validation,
usability testing, and "shadow mode" deployment comparing AI recommendations to actual
clinical decisions.
Post-deployment: Outcome drift monitoring, safety signal surveillance, human override
tracking, and periodic re-validation.
Workflow impact: Traditional accuracy metrics are necessary but insufficient. Clinical AI
must be evaluated on operational integration—not just algorithmic performance.
AI SoftLife™ Metrics
Consistent with our human-capacity principle, we recommend measuring:
• Cognitive load reduction — Does the AI decrease mental effort?
• Time returned to patient care — Does the AI free clinicians for presence?
• Trust and adoption — Do clinicians actually use and trust the AI?
We also recommend structured equity evaluation using frameworks such as AI-
EQUITYClear™ to assess algorithmic fairness across demographic subgroups.
HHS Support
Fund applied evaluation frameworks. Support shared testbeds and benchmark datasets—
particularly for rural and safety-net settings where evidence is thinnest. Public-private
partnerships could validate methodologies across contexts.

Question 5: Supporting Private Sector Activities
RFI Question: How can HHS best support accreditation, certification, and credentialing?
The Need
The market needs third-party validation of governance maturity, bias and safety controls, and
operational integration. Healthcare organizations lack reliable signals to distinguish quality
AI products from marketing.
Proposal: AI in Clinical Care Maturity Model
HHS should support development of standardized assessment frameworks for:
• Organizations — Readiness to adopt, implement, and govern AI responsibly.
• Vendors — Product quality, transparency, safety, and governance features.
• Care pathways — Effectiveness of AI integration into specific clinical workflows.
Additional Mechanisms
Recognize private standards from NIST, IEEE, and healthcare bodies by reference.
Provide regulatory safe harbors for certified AI. Fund workforce credentialing through
HRSA for AI competencies.
Our governance maturity and readiness scoring provide a foundation for organizational
assessment components of such models.

Question 6: AI Performance and Opportunities
RFI Question: Where has AI met or fallen short of expectations? What novel tools have
greatest potential?
Where AI Works
Documentation automation (ambient intelligence, AI-assisted notes), imaging triage
(radiology, pathology screening), risk stratification (sepsis prediction, early warning), and
population health analytics (care gap identification, resource allocation).
Where AI Falls Short
Workflow misalignment creates friction. Alert fatigue leads to override of valid
recommendations. Black-box decisioning undermines trust. Equity gaps from non-
representative training data worsen disparities in underserved populations.
High-Potential Applications
Care navigation (guiding patients through complex journeys), social determinants
integration (connecting patients to community resources), chronic disease coaching
(personalized self-management), team-based decision support, and frailty/dementia care
for aging populations.
Consistent with our AI SoftLife™ philosophy: AI should support presence, not replace
judgment. The highest-value applications restore clinician capacity to be fully present with
patients.

Question 7: Decision-Makers and Administrative Hurdles
RFI Question: Which roles have most influence on AI adoption? What are the primary
administrative hurdles?
The Reality
Multiple stakeholders hold effective veto power: CIO/CTO (infrastructure), CMIO/CMO
(clinical safety), Compliance/Legal (regulatory exposure), and CFO (budget). The result: no
single owner of AI outcomes. Decisions stall. Accountability diffuses. Promising initiatives
die in committee.
Primary Hurdles
Procurement and IT security cycles add 6–18 months. Absence of governance structures
forces ad hoc decisions. Medical staff bylaws rarely address AI. Legal review delays from
lack of AI expertise. Change management underinvestment undermines adoption.
SANCIAN Solution
• Named AI Executive Owner model — Single accountable leader with cross-
functional authority.
• Cross-functional AI governance councils — Formalized structures with defined
decision rights.
• Decision clarity before tooling — Governance infrastructure before product
evaluation.
This reflects our governance-first principle. HHS should encourage accountable leadership
models in guidance and grants—including Conditions of Participation updates requiring AI
oversight designation.

Question 8: Interoperability for AI Development
RFI Question: Where would enhanced interoperability widen opportunities and accelerate AI
development?
Priority Data Types
FHIR-based clinical data with bulk access for population AI. Social determinants of
health (currently inconsistent). Patient-reported outcomes captured outside encounters.
Imaging and genomic data for precision medicine applications.
Standards and Benchmarking
Model cards and audit metadata for standardized AI documentation. Benchmark datasets
for fairness representing diverse populations. Provenance tracking for data lineage and
model versioning.
SANCIAN View
Interoperability must include governance data, not just clinical data. Standards for AI
oversight artifacts—audit logs, performance results, bias assessments—would enable
consistent governance and regulatory oversight.
HHS should fund interoperability for AI oversight artifacts and require transparency
standards in funded programs. USCDI expansion should prioritize AI-relevant data elements.

Question 9: Patient and Caregiver Perspectives
RFI Question: What challenges do patients and caregivers want AI to address? What
concerns do they have?
What Patients Want
Access (timely appointments, expanded availability). Continuity (coordinated care across
providers). Clarity (understandable information, timely results). Less friction (reduced
paperwork, streamlined processes). Personalized but fair care (tailored treatment without
discrimination).
Patient Concerns
Bias and exclusion (fear of discrimination). Loss of human connection (AI replacing
empathy). Data misuse (uncertainty about privacy). Lack of recourse (unclear
accountability for harm). Accessibility (barriers for those with disabilities or limited digital
access).
AI SoftLife™ Response
Human-in-the-loop by design. Transparency as trust infrastructure. Equity checks
embedded early. HHS should make patient trust a core success metric—not a soft outcome,
but the foundation of sustainable adoption.

Question 10: AI Research Priorities
RFI Question: What AI research areas should HHS prioritize? What does the literature say?
High-Impact Research Areas
Consistent with our implementation-science principle:
• Implementation science in real clinics — How to deploy effectively, including
change management and adoption drivers.
• Equity and bias mitigation in deployment — Detecting and addressing bias as AI
operates in practice.
• Human-AI teaming — Optimal collaboration patterns and trust calibration.
• Safety and drift monitoring — Detecting failures and ensuring robustness across
contexts.
• Economic impact of burden reduction — Effects on clinician time, costs, and
workforce sustainability.
Literature Gap
Plenty on models. Not enough on adoption, outcomes, and workflow impact. Most
studies evaluate controlled settings; evidence on real-world implementation is thin. Rural and
safety-net settings are particularly underrepresented.
HHS Opportunity
Fund longitudinal pilots tied to outcomes and workforce wellbeing. Support public-private
learning collaboratives for sharing implementation lessons—including failures. This
underscores the implementation gap noted throughout this response.

Closing Position
Our Recommendation

• Improve outcomes for patients across all populations, including rural and safety-net
