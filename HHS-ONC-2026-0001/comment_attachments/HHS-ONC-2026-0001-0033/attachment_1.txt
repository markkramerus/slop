Solicitation of Public Comments
Regulation
As the nation’s principal health regulator, HHS helps shape the environment in which AI for
clinical care is developed, evaluated, and deployed. HHS seeks to establish a regulatory
posture on AI that is well understood, predictable, and proportionate to any risks presented to
enable rapid innovation while protecting patients and the confidentiality of their identifiable
health information and maintaining public trust. We seek feedback on how current HHS
regulations impact AI adoption and use for clinical care.
REPLY-
We view the opportunities presented by artificial intelligence in clinical care as
historically significant, but accompanied by equally significant warnings. As of
December 2025, hundreds of peer reviewed studies both highlight the potential benefits
of AI and raise serious concerns about its limitations and risks. While many studies
demonstrate strong performance in highly controlled settings with apparent clinical
validation, closer examination often shows that these systems do not perform as well
as licensed medical professionals in real world clinical environments. The promise of AI
is most evident in data intensive and objective domains such as medical imaging,
pathology, and genomics, whereas performance degrades as clinical decision making
moves beyond structured data into the more human and contextual aspects of care. A
fundamental constraint is the fragmentation of health data. An estimated three
thousand exabytes of clinical data in the United States are distributed across more than
four hundred ONC certified ambulatory and acute care electronic health record
systems. Even within standard clinical documentation, such as SOAP notes, subjective
and objective information is separated, further limiting the reliability of AI driven
inference. As a result, deploying AI broadly in clinical decision making today without
significant safeguards presents real risk. We are particularly concerned about general
purpose large language models, which can produce outputs that mix valid clinical
knowledge with pseudo science or inaccurate information. Over time, this will
necessitate the development and regulatory recognition of highly constrained, domain
specific language models designed for clinical use. The experience with EHR adoption
is instructive. Since the 2012 federal mandate, HHS has invested tens of billions of
dollars, with industry investing hundreds of billions more, yet there was no measurable
improvement in U.S. life expectancy from 2012 through 2019 prior to COVID. During the
same period, healthcare costs increased substantially, raising legitimate questions
about whether EHR adoption primarily optimized reimbursement and billing workflows
rather than patient outcomes. For these reasons, we believe AI should initially be
deployed primarily outside the core clinical decision making system to support

Reimbursement
HHS’s payment policies and programs have massive effects on how health care is delivered in
the United States, often times with unintended consequences. Hypothetically, if a payer is
taking financial risk for the long-term health and health costs of an individual, that payer will
have an inherent incentive to promote access to the highest-value interventions for patients.
Under government designed and dictated fee-for-service regimes, however, coverage and
reimbursement decisions are slow. Rarely does covering new innovations reduce net
spending; and waste, fraud, and abuse is difficult to prevent, often times leading to massive
spending bubbles on concentrated items or services that are not commensurate with the value
of such products. Given the inherent flaws in legacy payment systems, we seek to ensure that
the potential promises of AI innovations are not diminished through inertia and instead such
payment systems are modernized to meet the needs of a changing healthcare system. We
seek feedback on payment policy changes that ensure payers have the incentive and ability to
promote access to high value AI clinical interventions, foster competition among clinical care AI
tool builders, and accelerate access to and affordability of AI tools for clinical care.
REPLY-
We agree that payment standards established by HHS have a profound influence on
behavior across the healthcare system, often incentivizing providers and intermediaries
to optimize for reimbursement rather than for better outcomes or lower costs.
Historically, this dynamic has almost always resulted in higher aggregate spending.
When Medicare was established in 1965, national health expenditures were
approximately six percent of gross domestic product. Today, as reported by HHS
actuaries, health spending exceeds eighteen percent of GDP and continues to trend
upward, underscoring the structural limitations of current payment models.
We respectfully disagree with the assertion that a payer bearing long term financial risk
for an individual has an inherent incentive to promote the highest value interventions.
This assumption does not adequately account for the “velocity” with which patients
change employers or health plans. In practice, average enrollment duration in employer
sponsored coverage and Medicare Advantage plans is often five to six years. Annual
benefit design changes further allow managed care organizations to indirectly shed
higher risk members. As a result, the costs of underinvestment in prevention or
suboptimal care are frequently externalized to future payers. In reality, the only entity
that consistently bears true long term financial risk is HHS itself, which ultimately
absorbs the downstream consequences of delayed care, neglect, or misaligned
incentives within provider organizations and managed care plans.

From a payment policy perspective, HHS should not reimburse for the use of AI as a
discrete billable service. Reimbursing AI risks repeating the experience of prior
technology mandates that optimized billing workflows without demonstrably improving
patient outcomes. Instead, HHS should focus on fostering innovation by ensuring
patients have meaningful access to their complete medical records in aggregate, rather
than one record or encounter at a time. This would allow consumers to use carefully
constrained and curated AI tools to better understand their health, engage in shared
decision making, and manage chronic conditions. While AI clearly holds significant
value in biomedical research, population health analytics, and drug discovery, those
use cases fall largely outside the clinical reimbursement framework.
To foster competition and innovation, HHS should adopt a model similar to prior health
IT certification approaches, in which AI innovators register with HHS, verify
organizational identity, disclose intended data use including consumer data, and meet
baseline trust and security requirements comparable to those imposed on EHR
developers. HHS should set clear guardrails for safety, privacy, and accountability,
while avoiding mandates that prescribe specific technical implementations. Experience
with Meaningful Use demonstrates that when government mandates narrowly define
requirements, vendors tend to meet the letter of the mandate without meaningful
innovation. Imposing prescriptive requirements on AI risks creating a ceiling on
innovation rather than enabling its full potential.

Research & Development
HHS supports one of the world’s largest health research ecosystems, catalyzing innovation to
supplement the market. By enabling applied AI research & development, care delivery
research and implementation science, as well as AI entrepreneurship in health care, we can
better translate AI technologies from concept to clinical use. We seek input on ways in which
HHS may invest in research & development (including public-private partnerships and
cooperative research and development agreements (CRADAs)) to integrate AI in care delivery
and create new, long-term market opportunities that improve the health and wellbeing of all
Americans
REPLY-
We agree that HHS has the capacity to catalyze meaningful innovation through research
and development, but it also has the capacity to unintentionally suppress innovation
when programs are overly centralized or prescriptive. Government agencies are not well
positioned to predict which technical approaches or use cases will ultimately prove
transformative. In practice, many existing R&D mechanisms favor organizations that are

adept at producing strong grant applications rather than those best positioned to
deliver breakthrough innovation.
If HHS seeks to foster genuine innovation in AI, it should consider an approach more
analogous to a diversified venture capital model, funding a large number of early stage
efforts with relatively small awards rather than concentrating resources in a limited
number of large programs. In parallel, HHS could provide access to standardized, high
quality synthetic or de-ID datasets, for example a large corpus of representative
electronic health record datasets, that innovators could use to explore hypotheses,
develop models, and conduct back testing. This incremental and exploratory approach
would allow promising ideas to emerge organically before larger investments are made.
The experience with electronic health records illustrates the risks of conflating system
replacement with innovation. Over the past two decades, many hospitals have migrated
from one major EHR vendor to another, such as from Meditech to Cerner to Epic. These
transitions reflect vendor consolidation and rent seeking behavior rather than
meaningful advances in care delivery or outcomes. AI research programs should avoid
repeating this pattern by focusing on open experimentation rather than reinforcing
incumbent platforms.
A particularly high value opportunity lies in expanding controlled access to de-ID data.
Today, HHS and its affiliated agencies primarily possess claims and payment data,
which are insufficient on their own to support robust clinical AI development. The most
impactful AI applications require longitudinal clinical data, genetic information, and
linked claims data to understand disease progression, treatment response, and
outcomes over time. By enabling responsible access to these data types through well
governed research partnerships, HHS could significantly accelerate entrepreneurial
innovation and surface insights that centralized programs are unlikely to identify in
advance.

1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
REPLY-
The most significant barrier to private sector innovation and adoption of AI in clinical
care is that, to be practical and safe in most clinical settings, AI systems must perform

at least as well as licensed physicians, and in many contexts demonstrably better. At
present, most AI tools do not meet this threshold in real world clinical environments.
Any realistic assessment must account for the fully burdened cost of AI use, not just
the direct and often subsidized cost of the technology itself. This includes the time
physicians spend prompting, supervising, validating, and correcting AI outputs.
Physician time should be valued based on revenue generating capacity rather than
salary alone, which in many specialties translates to an effective cost of approximately
fifteen to thirty dollars per minute. When these opportunity costs are included, many AI
tools fail to demonstrate economic value. In addition, unresolved questions around
liability remain a major barrier to adoption. When AI recommendations are incorrect, it
is unclear who bears responsibility for malpractice exposure, reputational harm, and
potential licensure risk. Until accountability frameworks are clearly defined, providers
and health systems will remain appropriately cautious in deploying AI in clinical care.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or
programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
Please provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
REPLY-
We are cautious in recommending new regulatory or payment policies related to AI, as
prior experience with electronic health record mandates suggests that prescriptive
approaches are unlikely to produce meaningful clinical or economic benefit. In
particular, HHS should avoid establishing separate reimbursement for the use of AI. If
AI is ultimately integrated into clinical care, its value proposition should be reflected in
reduced costs, improved efficiency, or better outcomes, not in additional billable
services. Creating payment codes for AI would raise fundamental dilemmas, such as
whether a provider who uses AI should be reimbursed at a higher or lower rate than a
provider who delivers the same service without AI, even when outcomes are equivalent.
In practice, AI adoption is already occurring without explicit payment incentives. Based
on published medical reporting, an estimated forty to fifty percent of U.S. physicians
have used tools such as OpenEvidence or similar AI enabled clinical reference systems.
This underscores that clinicians will adopt tools they find useful without regulatory or
reimbursement mandates. The priority role for HHS should therefore be risk mitigation
rather than financial incentivization.
From a regulatory perspective, HHS should focus on strengthening protections around
the use of protected health information in AI systems. In particular, HHS should clarify
and enforce that identifiable patient data must not be disclosed to general purpose
large language models or other AI systems that are not explicitly designed, governed,
for research or clinical use only. Existing authorities under HIPAA, including 45 CFR

3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel legal
and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures
(e.g.,relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
REPLY-
We agree with the historical approach HHS has taken toward non medical devices used
by consumers, which generally relies on a consumer awareness and consumer
responsibility model. In many contexts, this has been an appropriate and pragmatic
posture. Our concern arises, however, when a non medical AI tool becomes affiliated
with, integrated into, or operated by a designated entity under HIPAA, including a
covered entity or a business associate under a business associate agreement.
Once a designated entity is involved, there is a significant legal and governance risk
related to data classification. Consumer generated data that originates outside the
HIPAA framework can effectively be transformed into protected health information
through association with a covered entity or business associate. We believe it would be
inappropriate and unsafe to allow designated entities, including EHR vendors and other
HIPAA regulated actors, to move data back and forth between protected health
information and so called consumer data in order to avoid regulatory obligations. This
practice would undermine the integrity of HIPAA protections and create ambiguity
around liability, privacy, and security responsibilities.
HHS should play a clarifying role by establishing clear guidance that once data is
handled by a covered entity or business associate for clinical or operational purposes,
it must be treated consistently as protected health information and remain subject to
HIPAA safeguards. Clear boundaries around data status would reduce uncertainty for
innovators while preserving accountability and public trust in the use of AI for clinical
care.

As discussed in our prior responses, the most effective way to foster innovation while
managing risk is to avoid overly prescriptive evaluation frameworks for non medical AI
tools used in or adjacent to clinical care. Non medical devices are generally not the
preferred modality for physicians when making clinical decisions, and attempts to force
their integration into core clinical workflows often introduce more noise than signal.
Variability in data quality, alerting logic, and model performance across consumer
oriented tools can create cognitive burden for providers rather than improving care
delivery.
For these reasons, HHS should be cautious in prioritizing large scale evaluation
programs focused on non medical devices within clinical settings. Where evaluation is
appropriate, it should emphasize real world usability, transparency of limitations, and
clear boundaries around clinical reliance, rather than attempting to certify performance
equivalence with medical devices. A limited, venture style funding approach could be
used to support exploratory evaluation efforts, allowing innovators to test hypotheses
and workflows without embedding them directly into EHR decision making.
If HHS chooses to support these processes, the most impactful mechanisms would be
small, time limited grants or prize based challenges that encourage experimentation
and post deployment learning, rather than long term contracts or large cooperative
agreements. This approach would allow HHS to observe what delivers value in practice
without prematurely standardizing evaluation methods in a rapidly evolving AI
environment.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, industry
driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
REPLY-
As noted above, HHS should avoid stifling innovation through expansive accreditation,
certification, or credentialing regimes that add administrative complexity without clear
evidence of improved outcomes. Innovators, inventors, and entrepreneurs should not
be required to assemble large compliance teams simply to engage with HHS or to
explore novel AI applications. Excessive process requirements risk favoring
incumbents and well resourced organizations over genuinely innovative entrants.
A more effective approach would be to apply a limited set of baseline trust
requirements, similar to know your customer standards in financial services, focused
on verifying organizational identity, governance, and accountability. These
requirements should be sufficient to grant access to carefully governed HHS datasets,
while maintaining strict adherence to existing HIPAA obligations and data protection
standards. Beyond these guardrails, HHS should allow market driven testing, peer
validation, and real world adoption to determine which AI tools deliver value in clinical

6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the
greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help
reduce costs?
REPLY-
There have been numerous reports of AI tools meeting or exceeding expectations in
clinical care, particularly with respect to speed and throughput. However, when these
claims are examined in detail, the performance gains often do not translate into
consistent improvements in clinical quality, reliability of outcomes, or total cost
reduction. Many highly positive peer reviewed studies published two or more years ago
were conducted in controlled or retrospective environments that do not reflect the
complexity and variability of real world clinical practice. As a result, reported
performance frequently deteriorates when these tools are deployed at scale.
Publicized benchmarking studies further illustrate this gap. For example, widely cited
industry sponsored evaluations have claimed that certain AI systems outperform
physicians by large margins. When the underlying methodologies are reviewed,
however, these studies often rely on artificial test conditions rather than real time
clinical workflows, and they frequently permit AI systems to leverage knowledge
sources that would not be available to a clinician at the point of care. Even under those
favorable conditions, reported accuracy rates have often been closer to eighty percent,
which falls short of the reliability required for unsupervised clinical decision making.
The greatest near term potential for AI lies not in replacing clinical judgment, but in
supporting highly constrained and objective tasks such as image analysis, pathology
screening, population level quality measurement, and administrative optimization.
Novel AI tools that operate on well defined data types, produce auditable outputs, and
reduce non clinical burden have the strongest potential to improve outcomes, generate
new insights into quality, and reduce costs without introducing unacceptable clinical
risk.

8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and
accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data types, data
standards, and benchmarking tools.
REPLY-
Electronic health record interoperability in the United States has largely failed, and
continued dialogue with large health IT vendors or major hospital systems has not
meaningfully changed that reality. Structural incentives, rather than technical
limitations, are the primary barrier. HHS should shift its focus away from vendor
mediated interoperability and instead prioritize enforcement of HIPAA rights that allow
patients direct access to their complete medical records. Patients are the most
motivated stakeholders to demand portability, continuity, and meaningful use of their
data.
Interoperability has stagnated for more than a decade because true data liquidity would
materially weaken the economic moats of incumbent EHR vendors and reduce
switching costs for health systems. If one EHR platform could efficiently and completely
transfer longitudinal records to another, the financial and operational barriers to
changing systems would be dramatically reduced. Today, hospitals often spend

9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the
adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers
have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
REPLY-
We do not believe that most patients or caregivers want AI to directly treat them.
Patients overwhelmingly want to be cared for by physicians who demonstrate empathy,
shared experience, and an understanding of their personal circumstances, values, and
fears. Trust in care is often relational, not purely technical. In our experience as clinical
administrators, many elderly patients prefer physicians who they feel understand their
stage of life and lived experience. For some patients, particularly older adults or
individuals from culturally tight knit communities, the introduction of AI into clinical
decision making can feel impersonal or unsettling rather than reassuring.
While AI may be perceived as acceptable or even helpful by younger, more
technologically fluent patients, the same tools may generate anxiety or mistrust among
older populations or those with limited digital literacy. AI can simulate empathy through
language and tone, but it cannot genuinely experience empathy, shared suffering, or
moral responsibility. Patients and caregivers are therefore concerned that increased
reliance on AI could erode the human connection that is central to healing, reduce
accountability, and distance clinicians from the lived reality of their patients. Any
