Dr. Keane:
The Radiology Business Management Association (RBMA) appreciates the opportunity to respond to the
Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence.
Established in 1968, RBMA is a professional association that consists of over 2000 radiology practice
business leaders who represent over 800 radiology practices in all 50 states. Included are diagnostic
radiology, interventional radiology, nuclear medicine, Independent Diagnostic Testing Facilities (IDTFs)
and radiation oncology.
RBMA is the trusted partner of radiology professionals, advancing the industry and broadening our
members’ capacity to provide superior patient experiences.
RBMA represents the “business side of radiology.” While our members are deeply familiar with the
clinical benefits and challenges of AI, our responses focus primarily on the financial, operational, and
administrative implications of AI adoption. We defer to physician organizations on detailed clinical
benefits and barriers.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation for AI for healthcare and its adoption and
use in clinical care?
RBMA Comments:
Significant barriers are financial, operational, and contractual in nature.
 Financial: Organizations struggle to determine how to pay for AI, how reimbursement will
evolve, and how to build viable financial models while AI use cases remain fluid.
 Operational: Integrating AI into established workflows can be complex. Workforce readiness
and willingness to adopt AI tools are still uncertain.
 Contractual: Key liability questions remain unresolved: Who is responsible when AI makes an
error? How will malpractice insurers treat AI-assisted care? Additionally, concerns exist
regarding data ownership, vendor data governance, and whether vendors intend to monetize
clinical data.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to

incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why?
RBMA Comments:
HHS should not assume AI will immediately create efficiencies. Early implementation often requires
increased workflow steps, staff time, and capital investment. If HHS wishes to incentivize AI adoption, it
should pursue approaches that do not reduce base reimbursement and instead:
 Support clinicians and providers with funding mechanisms for procurement, implementation,
and ongoing operationalization.
 Create and enforce regulations that clarify ownership of data, liability standards, and
malpractice coverage expectations related to AI use.
 Establish a federal certification framework verifying AI vendor compliance with, and ideally
exceeding, HIPAA and cybersecurity standards.
 Ensure that AI tools and technologies—where applicable—are exempt from tariffs, reducing
financial burden and promoting access.
Clinicians remain unsure how to model the long-term costs and reimbursement of AI; HHS policies that
reduce cost barriers will significantly improve adoption.
3. For non-medical devices, what novel legal and implementation issues exist, and what role should
HHS play?
RBMA Comments:
RBMA agrees that significant legal uncertainties exist—many of which affect both medical and
non-medical AI tools. As noted in response to Question 1, practices face unresolved questions related
to:
 HIPAA and broader privacy compliance
 Malpractice and legal liability
 Data ownership, governance, and vendor rights to reuse or monetize data
HHS can help accelerate adoption by establishing clearer guardrails, reducing ambiguity for providers,
and creating trusted standards for vendors.
4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods, and should HHS
support these processes?
RBMA Comments:
RBMA recommends that HHS support evaluation methods that promote transparency, robustness, and
workflow safety, including:
 Pre-deployment validation using diverse, representative datasets
 Post-deployment monitoring for model drift, error rates, and performance across demographic
groups
 Human-centered workflow assessment to evaluate the real-world impact on clinical staff and
operations
HHS could accelerate progress through grants, cooperative agreements, or vendor certification
competitions that reward reliability, equity, and interoperability.
5. How can HHS best support private-sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, credentialing)
to promote AI use?
RBMA Comments:
HHS can play a critical role by:
 Establishing baseline national standards for AI vendor transparency, cybersecurity, data
governance, and model training disclosures

 Partnering with private-sector accreditation bodies to create aligned, non-duplicative
certification pathways
 Encouraging industry-driven benchmarking tools that incorporate privacy, equity, and
workflow-impact criteria
Such efforts would reduce provider burden and strengthen trust in AI tools.
6. Where has AI in clinical care met or exceeded expectations, and where has it fallen short? What
novel tools hold the most promise?
RBMA Comments:
RBMA’s expertise centers on the business and operational aspects of radiology. However, from a clinical
lens, RBMA notes ongoing concerns regarding bias in AI training datasets. AI models learn from the data
they ingest, and many tools may not be validated against sufficiently diverse patient populations.
Examples include:
 AI in mammography: Were models trained on women across all breast density categories,
varied risk profiles, and diverse racial and ethnic backgrounds?
 AI in pneumonia detection: Were tools trained solely on acutely ill hospitalized patients, or do
they include routine outpatient imaging?
Similar to the HIPAA-oriented certification noted in Question 2, HHS could significantly support
providers by creating a federal certification verifying that AI models were trained and validated on
comprehensive, demographically diverse datasets.
7. Which decision makers drive AI adoption within healthcare organizations, and what are the
administrative hurdles?
RBMA Comments:
Key decision makers typically include executive leaders (CFO, CIO, CMO), radiology administrators, IT
departments, compliance officers, and clinical governance committees. Primary hurdles include:
 Limited capital budgets
 Variable reimbursement and uncertain ROI
 Integration challenges with existing systems
 Legal and compliance ambiguity
 Staff acceptance and training needs
8. Where would enhance interoperability widen AI market opportunities and accelerate
development?
RBMA Comments:
Enhanced interoperability—particularly around imaging data formats, structured reporting, and
standardized benchmarking data—could dramatically advance AI by allowing:
 Easier dataset aggregation for model training
 Seamless integration with PACS/RIS/EHR systems
 More reliable cross-vendor comparisons
 Faster multi-site validation studies
Standards such as DICOM, HL7/FHIR, and imaging-specific structured reporting frameworks are
essential.
9. What challenges do patients and caregivers want AI to address, and what concerns do they hold?
RBMA Comments:
Patients express interest in AI tools that improve access, accuracy, timeliness, and care coordination.
However, they also express concerns related to:

 Data privacy and security
 Equity and bias in AI decision-making
 Whether clinicians remain “in the loop”
 Transparency around how AI is used in their care
Clear communication from providers—supported by standardized HHS guidance—will be essential for
patient trust.
10. Are there AI research areas HHS should prioritize to accelerate adoption?
RBMA Comments:
a. Published findings:
There is growing research on AI's impact on clinical workflows, diagnostic accuracy, and efficiency.
However, much of the literature is early-stage, limited to specific populations, or lacks large-scale
deployment data.
b. Costs and benefits:
Current literature examines cost-effectiveness unevenly; many studies acknowledge potential financial
benefits but do not yet quantify them with sufficient real-world evidence. Additional federally supported
economic studies would help providers model total cost of ownership, reimbursement pathways, and
long-term ROI.
In summary, Artificial intelligence holds substantial promise to improve the quality,
consistency, and safety of radiologists’ interpretations of imaging studies. AI tools can flag
subtle findings, reduce perceptual errors, prioritize urgent cases, and support clinical decision-
making. Yet a central challenge to encouraging innovation in this space is that current Medicare
and commercial payer reimbursement systems are largely grounded in fee-for-service models
that reward physician time, malpractice risk, and practice expenses rather than measurable
improvements in quality or outcomes. If AI improves efficiency or reduces interpretive effort, it
may paradoxically threaten reimbursement, discouraging adoption even when patient care
improves.
Under existing payment structures, radiologist work relative value units (RVUs) are based on
assumptions about time and cognitive effort. AI-assisted workflows that shorten reading times
or reduce uncertainty could be interpreted as lowering physician work, even though the
radiologist retains legal responsibility and malpractice risk. Moreover, practice costs may
increase due to AI licensing, integration, validation, cybersecurity, and compliance
requirements—expenses that are not well reflected in current reimbursement formulas. This
misalignment creates a policy dilemma: innovation that improves quality and safety may be
financially penalized.
Policymakers can address this dilemma through several complementary strategies. First,
reimbursement models should evolve to explicitly recognize quality-enhancing clinical AI as a
form of physician work augmentation rather than substitution. Medicare could create add-on
payments or new RVU components tied to validated AI use that demonstrably improves
diagnostic accuracy, reduces downstream costs, or enhances patient outcomes. Such add-on
payment should be permanent and not time limited models. Second, broader adoption of

value-based payment models—such as bundled payments —would allow providers to capture
the financial benefits of higher-quality, more efficient care enabled by AI.
Third, policymakers should support mechanisms to account for persistent malpractice risk. Even
when AI assists interpretation, radiologists remain legally accountable. Payment policies should
reflect that AI does not eliminate professional liability and may, during early adoption, increase
perceived risk. Finally, federal investment in evidence generation, standards, and regulatory
clarity can reduce uncertainty and lower adoption costs, making it easier for payers to
reimburse AI-enabled care appropriately.
Without payment reform, AI risks becoming a victim of its own success. Aligning
reimbursement with quality, accountability, and long-term value is essential to realizing AI’s
potential to improve radiologic care without undermining the economic foundations of medical
practice.

collaboratively with HHS to advance policies that promote high-quality, cost-effective care for Medicare
