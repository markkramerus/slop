II. Responses to Specific Questions
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for
health care and its adoption and use in clinical care?

The barriers we encounter most frequently are not technical — they are structural and
procedural:
1. Fragmented procurement and IT governance.
Healthcare organizations often lack clear pathways for evaluating and adopting operational AI
tools. Clinical AI (diagnostic, imaging) has established FDA frameworks and clinical validation
processes. Operational AI: scheduling, staffing, administrative automation, falls outside these
frameworks but still faces lengthy procurement cycles (often 12-18 months) because
organizations default to enterprise IT evaluation processes designed for EHRs and clinical
systems. There is no proportionate, streamlined pathway for lower-risk operational AI.
2. Integration complexity with legacy workforce systems.
Health systems operate fragmented technology stacks: one system for scheduling, another for
time and attendance, another for credentialing, another for payroll. These systems rarely
interoperate well. AI tools that could automate scheduling decisions require read/write access to
multiple systems simultaneously: scheduling, credential databases, union rules engines, payroll;
and each integration requires separate security reviews, BAAs, and technical work. The
absence of standard APIs or interoperability requirements for workforce management systems
creates friction that delays deployment by months.
3. Credentialing and compliance data silos.
Real-time scheduling decisions require real-time visibility into staff credentials, licenses,
certifications, and competencies. This data is typically locked in credentialing software that does
not expose it programmatically, forcing manual verification or batch exports. This limits AI's
ability to make compliant staffing decisions autonomously.
4. Risk aversion without clear accountability frameworks.
Healthcare leaders are uncertain about liability when AI systems make or recommend staffing
decisions. Who is accountable if an AI schedules an under-credentialed provider? The lack of
clear guidance from HHS on accountability for non-medical-device AI creates hesitation among
compliance officers and legal teams, slowing adoption even when operational leaders are
enthusiastic.
Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes
should HHS prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and
why?
1. Extend interoperability mandates to workforce management systems.

The 21st Century Cures Act and ONC's information blocking rules have improved data liquidity
for clinical information. Similar interoperability requirements should apply to workforce
management, credentialing, and time/attendance systems. Health systems should be able to
access their own workforce data. Schedules, credentials, availability — through standardized
APIs without vendor lock-in or prohibitive integration fees.
Recommendation: HHS should consider whether workforce management data constitutes a
category of information that, when siloed, impedes the effective delivery of care, and evaluate
extending information blocking provisions or publishing voluntary interoperability standards for
this data category.
2. Clarify accountability frameworks for operational AI.
HHS should publish guidance distinguishing operational AI (scheduling, staffing, administrative
automation) from clinical decision support and medical devices. This guidance should address
liability allocation, documentation requirements, and human oversight expectations for AI
systems that make non-clinical operational decisions.
This clarity would reduce legal uncertainty that currently delays procurement and allow health
systems to adopt operational AI with appropriate, but not excessive, governance.
3. Recognize administrative burden reduction as a quality and safety priority.
CMS quality programs and value-based payment models do not currently recognize
administrative efficiency as a measured outcome. Yet administrative burden is a primary driver
of clinician burnout, which directly impacts care quality, safety, and workforce retention.
Recommendation: HHS should explore whether workforce stability metrics (turnover, vacancy
rates, time-to-fill) and administrative burden measures could be incorporated into quality
reporting or value-based payment adjustments, creating financial incentives for health systems
to invest in operational AI that reduces burden.
Question 7: Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health
care organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical
care? What are the primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical
care?
Key decision-makers for operational AI adoption:
● Chief Nursing Officers (CNOs) and VP/Directors of Nursing Operations — These
leaders feel the pain of scheduling complexity most acutely and are often initial
champions.
● VP of Operations or Chief Operating Officer — Controls budget authority and
operational priorities.

● IT Security and Compliance — Gatekeepers who evaluate data handling, integration
risk, and vendor security posture.
● HRIS/Workforce Management System Owners — Often skeptical of tools that interact
with "their" systems; can slow or block integration approvals.
Primary administrative hurdles:
1. No single owner for operational AI. Unlike clinical AI (which falls under CMO/CMIO
purview), operational AI spans nursing, HR, IT, and operations. This creates confusion
about who evaluates, approves, and owns these tools.
2. Procurement processes designed for large capital expenditures. AI tools often have
SaaS pricing models with lower upfront costs but recurring fees. Procurement teams
trained on capital equipment evaluation struggle to assess these models, leading to
delays.
3. Security review bottlenecks. Enterprise security teams are overwhelmed; vendor
security reviews can take 3-6 months even for low-risk operational tools.
4. Change management resistance. Unit managers and schedulers who have "always
done it this way" can resist AI tools that change their workflows, even if the tools reduce
their burden. Successful adoption requires executive sponsorship and frontline
engagement simultaneously.

Question 9: What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish
to see addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
From our conversations with health system leaders and frontline staff, the challenges most
directly tied to workforce AI include:
For patients:
● Staff continuity. Patients, particularly in post-acute, long-term care, and oncology
settings really value seeing consistent caregivers. AI-optimized scheduling can prioritize
continuity assignments.
● Reduced wait times. Understaffing due to last-minute call-offs creates delays. AI that
rapidly fills gaps improves patient access.
● Safety. Fatigued, burned-out staff make more errors. Reducing administrative burden
and optimizing schedules for appropriate rest improves safety.
For caregivers (clinical staff):
● Schedule predictability and fairness. Manual scheduling often feels arbitrary or
biased. AI can apply rules consistently.
● Reduced last-minute changes. AI can anticipate gaps and fill them proactively rather
than calling staff at the last minute.
● Less time on administrative tasks. Nurses and managers spend hours on scheduling,
swap approvals, and credential checks. Automating this returns time to patient care.
Concern to address: Caregivers express concern about AI making decisions that affect their
work lives without transparency. Any workforce AI must be explainable. Staff should understand
why they were assigned a particular shift and must preserve human override capability.
III. Summary Recommendations

1. Extend interoperability policy attention to workforce management systems to
unlock the data AI needs to optimize clinical operations.
2. Publish clear guidance distinguishing operational AI from clinical AI and medical
devices, addressing accountability, documentation, and oversight expectations
proportionate to risk.
3. Recognize administrative burden reduction and workforce stability as quality-
adjacent outcomes that merit measurement and incentive alignment in CMS programs.
4. Convene a public-private working group on workforce data standards to accelerate
voluntary interoperability for scheduling, credentialing, and time/attendance systems.
IV. Conclusion
AI's greatest near-term impact in healthcare may not be in diagnosis or imaging — it may be in
relieving the operational and administrative burden that drives clinician burnout and workforce
shortages. The barriers to adoption are not primarily technical; they are structural, procedural,
and policy-driven. HHS has an opportunity to accelerate responsible adoption of operational AI
by clarifying accountability, extending interoperability principles to workforce data, and
recognizing administrative efficiency as a component of care quality.
ShiftOS welcomes further engagement with HHS on these issues and is available to provide
additional detail or participate in stakeholder convenings.
