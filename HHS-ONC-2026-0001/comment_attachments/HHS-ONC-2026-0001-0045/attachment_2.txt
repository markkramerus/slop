Executive Summary
Empathetic, Clinically Governed AI Can Reduce Healthcare Costs and
Improve National Health
Artificial intelligence represents one of the most consequential opportunities of this decade to improve
health outcomes while reducing the unsustainable growth of healthcare costs in the United States. As
an AI-enabled care management technology company actively deployed in real-world clinical and
population health settings, we submit this response to emphasize a central premise: AI is most readily
adopted and effectively used in clinical care when it is empathetic, highly engaging, and
embedded within strong guardrails that prioritize patient safety, equity, and trust.
AI as a Force Multiplier for Human Care
The most effective healthcare AI does not seek to replace clinicians or focus only on automation.
Accelerating adoption of AI requires empathy to remain a foundational element of care delivery.
Effective AI solutions extend the reach of care teams, allowing them to identify risk earlier, engage
patients more frequently, and intervene before avoidable deterioration occurs. AI solutions adopted as
In practice, the highest-cost healthcare events—emergency department utilization, hospitalizations,
readmissions, and unmanaged chronic disease—are often preceded by unaddressed behavioral,
physical, or psychosocial signals. AI-enabled care management tools can consistently surface these
signals earlier and reduce risk of unconscious bias vs. traditional models. Earlier detection and action
enable more timely, lower-cost interventions that preserve both patient well-being and system
resources.
Empathy and Engagement Are Core Safety Features
From the patient’s perspective, engagement is not driven by algorithms alone, it is driven by whether an
interaction feels respectful, empathetic, understandable and supportive. AI systems that leverage
natural, conversational modalities (such as voice) and are designed around empathy and clarity
achieve significantly higher engagement rates than transactional or form-based tools.
High engagement is not merely a usability benefit; it is a patient safety imperative. Patients who
engage consistently are more likely to disclose emerging symptoms, emotional distress, medication
concerns, and social barriers to care. This allows care teams to act earlier, reducing clinical risk and
downstream cost. Such interactions are also more likely to initiate behavior changes that enable a
trajectory of improved health.

Patient Safety Requires Clinical Guardrails, Not Just Technical Accuracy
AI used in care management must operate within explicit clinical boundaries. On-going review and
use of rigorous quality assurance processes are required to maintain patient safety and adherence to
clinical guardrails. Effective guardrails include:
● Clear definitions of AI scope and escalation thresholds
● Human-in-the-loop oversight by licensed clinicians
● Continuous performance monitoring and bias evaluation
● Structured clinical review of AI outputs and interventions
● Transparent communication to patients about AI use
When AI solutions are designed with these safeguards, they can safely operate as adjuncts to clinical
care, improving consistency while preserving accountability.
Quality Assurance and Trust Are Prerequisites for Scale
Public trust is essential to national AI adoption. Trust is earned when AI tools demonstrate:
● Clinical validity grounded in real-world evidence
● Measurable improvements in outcomes and utilization
● Strong data privacy and security protections
● Alignment with existing care workflows and standards
HHS can accelerate responsible adoption of non-medical device AI by encouraging evaluation
frameworks that assess clinical impact, safety, equity, and operational performance. Non-medical
device AI is playing a growing role in care delivery and with stronger evaluations frameworks and
governance established, adoption and value would be accelerated.
Deflating Costs Through Early, Continuous, and Equitable Care
AI-enabled care management supports the objectives of “Make America Healthy Again” by shifting the
system from episodic, reactive care toward continuous, preventive engagement—especially for
populations that are traditionally underserved or difficult to reach. By enabling:
● Earlier detection of behavioral and psychosocial risk
● More frequent, lower-cost patient touchpoints
● Better prioritization of clinical resources
● Reduced avoidable utilization
AI can materially reduce the total cost of care while improving patient experience and outcomes.

A Call for Alignment Across Regulation, Reimbursement, and R&D
To fully realize these benefits, federal policy should align incentives so that empathetic, safe, and
clinically governed AI is rewarded—not penalized—for preventing harm and reducing cost. This
includes:
● Modernized reimbursement that values prevention and engagement
● Clear, proportionate governance models for non-medical device clinical AI
● Investment in implementation science and public-private partnerships
With the right guardrails and incentives, adoption of AI-enabled care management solutions will
strengthen the human foundations of healthcare and advance national health and fiscal sustainability.
QUESTION 1:
What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health
care and its adoption and use in clinical care?
AI-enabled care management has the potential to materially reduce healthcare costs while improving
patient experience and outcomes. A foundational element to success is AI developed to be
empathetic, engaging, and governed by strong clinical guardrails. Despite increasing interest and
technological readiness, adoption across the healthcare sector remains uneven. The adoption
challenges are not primarily technical. Organizations are facing structural, operational, and
incentive-driven challenges.
The following outlines the most significant barriers to private sector innovation in AI for healthcare and
its effective use in clinical care, informed by real-world deployment experience.
1. Misaligned Incentives That Reward Treatment Over Prevention
One of the most persistent barriers is the disconnect between where AI creates value and where
the healthcare system pays for care.
Many AI tools, particularly in care management, behavioral health, and population health, deliver value
by:
● Detecting risk earlier
● Preventing avoidable escalation
● Reducing utilization that would otherwise generate billable events
In fee-for-service environments, preventing a hospitalization or emergency department visit often
reduces revenue rather than creating it. Even in value-based arrangements, savings are frequently

2. Workforce Burnout and Change Fatigue Limit Adoption Capacity
Healthcare organizations are operating under unprecedented workforce strain. Nurses, care managers,
and social workers are already managing unsustainable caseloads and administrative burden. These
teams are often the primary users of care management AI, and where patient care shifting to
prevention is most prevalently needed and realized.
While AI can reduce workload over time, initial adoption requires change, including:
● Workflow redesign
● Training and trust-building
● Governance and oversight processes
Organizations experiencing acute staffing shortages often lack the bandwidth to implement new
solutions, even when one of the primary benefits is to to alleviate long-term burnout and re-balance
workload between administrative and clinical care needs.
Real-world impact:
AI initiatives stall not because they lack clinical value, but because frontline teams are stretched too thin
to participate in pilots, feedback loops, or early optimization phases.
3. Lack of Clear Governance for Non-Medical Device AI
Many of the most impactful AI solutions in care management do not meet the definition of a regulated
medical device. These tools may assess risk, guide outreach, or support clinical prioritization without
making autonomous diagnoses or treatment decisions.
However, health systems often struggle with:
● Unclear liability and accountability frameworks
● Uncertainty around appropriate clinical oversight
● Inconsistent internal review processes
● Lack of accepted frameworks for AI system governance and assessment
Ambiguity about governance structure for non-medical device AI leads to risk aversion, prolonged
procurement cycles, and inconsistent decision-making across organizations.

QUESTION 2:
What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should
HHS prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why?
What HHS regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited?
To unlock the full cost-deflation and quality-improvement potential of AI in clinical care, HHS should
prioritize changes that reward prevention, clarify governance, and reduce operational friction,
particularly for AI-enabled care management and non-medical device clinical AI solutions.
1. Modernize Payment Models to Reward Prevention and Engagement
A top priority is to align reimbursement with the outcomes AI is uniquely positioned to improve.
Many AI tools generate value by preventing adverse events rather than producing billable
encounters. HHS should modernize payment policy to recognize AI-enabled activities that
demonstrably reduce total cost of care.
Recommended actions:
● Expand CMS reimbursement pathways for AI-supported assessment, monitoring, and
outreach, particularly in:
○ Chronic disease management
○ Behavioral health and comorbidity screening
○ Transitional care and post-discharge follow-up
● Encourage Medicare Advantage, Medicaid managed care, and ACO models to:
○ Treat AI-enabled engagement as a covered care management activity
○ Allow AI-generated insights to count toward quality and risk-adjustment workflows when
clinically validated, but without specified human review times
Real-World Impact:
Without reimbursement alignment, AI adoption remains limited to pilot programs, even when evidence
shows reductions in ED visits, hospitalizations, and readmissions.
2. Establish Clear, Proportionate Governance for Non-Medical-Device AI
A top priority of AI governance is to reduce uncertainty without over-regulating low-risk, high-value AI.
A significant portion of AI used in care delivery does not meet the definition of a medical device, yet still
influences clinical prioritization and care coordination. Today, governance varies widely across
organizations, slowing adoption.

1. Ambiguity Around Accountability and Liability
One of the most significant challenges is unclear allocation of responsibility when AI informs clinical
workflows but does not make independent medical decisions.
Key questions health systems regularly face include:
● Who is accountable for an AI-generated risk signal that is not acted upon?
● How should responsibility be shared between the technology vendor and the deploying
organization?
● What constitutes appropriate clinical reliance versus over-reliance on AI insights?
In the absence of clear guidance, organizations often default to conservative positions regarding
limiting AI use or requiring excessive manual review. Both tactics create inefficiencies, slow adoption,
and diminish the ability to realize positive outcomes when AI is deployed.
Real-World Impact:
AI tools that could safely surface early warning signs are sometimes constrained to advisory roles with
limited operational follow-through, not due to safety concerns, but due to fear of downstream liability
exposure.
Potential HHS role:
HHS can help by:
● Clarifying expectations for human-in-the-loop accountability
● Providing guidance on reasonable reliance standards for AI that informs—but does not
replace—clinical judgment
● Encouraging shared responsibility models aligned with existing care team structures

Procurement of non-medical device AI often involves prolonged negotiations around indemnification,

HHS can help by:

QUESTION 4:
For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation
methods (pre- and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and
other workflow and human-centered evaluation methods for clinical care?

For non-medical device AI used in clinical care in areas such as care management, patient
engagement, risk identification, and clinical prioritization, traditional pre-market evaluation models are
insufficient on their own. These tools derive much of their value from real-world deployment, continuous
learning, and interaction with humans. As a result, evaluation must be ongoing, contextual, and
human-centered, and expand beyond static performance benchmarks.
1. Pre-Deployment Evaluation: Clinical Appropriateness and Guardrails
Before deployment, the most important evaluation questions include “Is the model accurate?” and “Is
the model appropriate, safe, and clearly bounded in its role?”
Promising pre-deployment evaluation methods include:
● Validation of AI outputs against clinically accepted reference standards
● Clear definition of intended use, limitations, and escalation thresholds
● Scenario-based testing to assess AI behavior under edge cases or ambiguous inputs
● Review by multidisciplinary clinical teams, including nursing, care management, and patient
safety leaders

Real-World Impact:
AI systems that are clinically well-scoped and appropriately constrained are far safer than overly broad
tools with unclear boundaries—even if raw accuracy metrics appear strong.
2. Post-Deployment Monitoring: Continuous Safety and Performance Assurance
Non-medical device AI performance evolves over time due to:
● Changes in patient populations
● Shifts in care delivery models
● Workflow adaptations
● Model updates
As a result, post-deployment evaluation is the cornerstone of patient safety. Key post-deployment
metrics should include:
● Clinical signal detection rates and false-positive/false-negative trends
● Escalation appropriateness and timeliness
● Patient engagement and response consistency
● Disparities in performance across demographic groups
● Downstream clinical and utilization outcomes
Real-World Impact:
An AI system that identifies risk accurately but fails to engage patients consistently, or generates

performance.
3. Human-Centered and Workflow-Based Evaluation
AI in clinical care does not operate in isolation. It operates within complex clinical workflows and human
decision-making environments. Promising evaluation approaches include:
● Workflow impact assessments to understand how AI changes care team behavior
● Cognitive load and alert fatigue analysis
● Qualitative feedback from frontline clinicians and care managers
● Evaluation of how AI supports decision-making rather than overrides it
Real-World Impact:
Many AI failures in healthcare are not algorithmic failures—they are integration failures. Human-
centered evaluation identifies these risks early.

4. Engagement and Empathy as Core Evaluation Metrics
For AI-enabled care management, engagement is a leading indicator of both safety and

Evaluation frameworks should explicitly measure:
● Patient participation and retention over time

● Patient-reported experience and understanding
● Willingness to disclose sensitive information

Real-World Impact:
AI deployed at scale must perform safely and effectively across diverse populations, particularly those
at highest risk and highest cost.

● Grants and cooperative agreements focused on real-world implementation science
● CMMI demonstrations incorporating AI evaluation requirements
● Public-private partnerships to establish shared benchmarks and best practices

Executive Closing Statement
Artificial intelligence has reached a point of maturity where it can meaningfully improve patient safety,
strengthen care delivery, and reduce the total cost of care—but only if it is deployed with empathy,
accountability, and strong clinical governance. Across our responses to this RFI, a consistent theme
emerges: the greatest opportunity for AI in clinical care lies not in automation for its own sake, but in
augmenting human care teams, engaging patients earlier and more consistently, and preventing
avoidable deterioration before it becomes costly or harmful.
Patient Safety as the Foundation for AI Adoption
Patient safety must remain the cornerstone of AI deployment in healthcare. The most effective AI
systems are those that operate within clearly defined clinical guardrails, incorporate human
oversight, and are continuously monitored in real-world use. Safety is strengthened—not weakened—
when AI is used to surface early risk, prioritize outreach, and support clinical judgment rather than
replace it.
Equally important, engagement and empathy are safety features. AI tools that patients trust and
interact with consistently are more likely to detect emerging concerns and less likely to miss critical
signals. Conversely, AI that patients disengage from introduces blind spots that undermine safety,
regardless of technical accuracy.
Improving Outcomes Through Early, Continuous, and Equitable Care
Across clinical settings, the highest-cost and highest-risk events are often preceded by unmet
behavioral, emotional, or social needs. AI-enabled care management offers a practical mechanism to:
● Detect risk earlier
● Maintain consistent support between visits
● Improve coordination across fragmented care environments
● Extend the reach of constrained clinical workforces
When deployed responsibly, these tools improve outcomes for patients while supporting clinicians and
caregivers who are increasingly stretched thin.
Reducing Total Medical Spend by Preventing Escalation
AI’s most powerful economic impact lies in preventing downstream utilization, not optimizing already
expensive interventions. Early identification of risk, proactive engagement, and targeted escalation
reduce:
● Emergency department visits
● Hospital admissions and readmissions
● Avoidable complications of chronic disease
Confidential and Proprietary © 2026
37

However, today’s payment and regulatory structures often fail to reward prevention. Without aligned
incentives, even proven AI solutions struggle to scale beyond pilots.
The Need for Federal Leadership, Support, and Incentives
HHS has a unique and critical role to play in accelerating safe and effective AI adoption. Federal
leadership can:
● Align reimbursement with prevention and engagement
● Clarify governance expectations for non-medical-device AI
● Support standardized evaluation and accreditation frameworks
● Reduce administrative friction and duplication
● Invest in real-world implementation science and public-private partnerships
Importantly, federal action should enable and coordinate private sector innovation, not replace it.
Clear guidance, proportionate oversight, and targeted incentives will allow payers, health systems, and
providers to adopt AI responsibly and at scale.
A Call to Action
AI represents a rare opportunity to advance the shared goals of patient safety, better outcomes,
workforce sustainability, and cost containment. Realizing this opportunity will require:
● Patient-centered design grounded in empathy and trust
● Strong clinical guardrails and continuous quality assurance
● Incentives that reward prevention rather than volume
● Federal policies that reduce uncertainty and accelerate adoption
With thoughtful leadership and collaboration, AI can help make healthcare safer, more humane, and
