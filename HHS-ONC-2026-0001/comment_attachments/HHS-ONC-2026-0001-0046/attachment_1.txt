Executive Summary
This paper announces the Department of Health and Human Services Sector’s request for
public comments on how it can accelerate the adoption of artificial intelligence within healthcare
while upholding ethical considerations and civil liberties. The Department seeks feedback from
those buying, building, evaluating, using, and receiving care from AI tools embedded within clinical
care systems as well as those who wish to do so but face barriers. The RFI poses ten specific
questions addressing barriers to AI adoption, regulatory changes needed, evaluation methods,
interoperability challenges, and patient concerns. HHS aims to establish a forward-leaning,
industry-supportive approach that reduces uncertainty and ensures AI deployment is used effectively
to enhance productivity, reduce costs, and overall improve health outcomes.

Introduction
The U.S. health care system currently stands at a critical crossroad. National health
expenditures reached $4.9 trillion in 2023, with per capita costs at $14,570, and healthcare now
comprises 17.6% of GDP4, a figure projected to increase to 20.3% by 20335. These escalating costs
place an enormous burden on American families, with 44% of adults reporting difficulty affording
healthcare costs and approximately 28% facing problems paying for care in the past year.6
Simultaneously, the system faces an extreme workforce crisis, with projections of a shortage
exceeding 187,000 full-time equivalent physicians by 20377, and approximately 45% of physicians
reporting burnout in 2025.8 Amid these mounting pressures of cost and workforce strain, artificial
intelligence emerges as a potentially transformative tool in reshaping the problems that hinder the
healthcare system today.
Despite AI’s promise, its translation into widespread clinical use has been notably limited.
Research has identified substantial barriers to adoption across technical, organizational, and human
aspects. A systemic review identified 16 key barriers including data quality and bias, infrastructure
limitations, financial constraints, workflow misalignment, inadequate training, and potential lack of
transparency and accountability.9 A 2025 survey of major U.S. health systems found that while
ambient clinical documentation tools achieved 100% adoption activity, success rates varied
dramatically across use cases, with only 38% reporting high success in AI for clinical risk
stratification despite widespread deployment.

There are a multitude of barriers to AI adoption. On the technological front, healthcare
organizations struggle with immature AI tools, poor data quality, and incompatibility with existing
healthcare infrastructure. Among the concerns of allied healthcare professionals are reduced human
contact with patients and perceived reductions in healthcare quality due to the implementation of
AI. Organizationally, there is insufficient leadership support and regulatory uncertainty. A 2025
survey found that 70% of hospital leaders experienced at least one AI pilot failure, while 80% found
it difficult to verify vendors’ AI claims without formal governance.10 On the human front, clinicians
lack adequate training and often resist adoption, fearing replacement or increased workload, which
are both compounded by existing staff shortages.
The European Union has adopted a comprehensive risk-based regulatory framework
through its AI act, which classifies most healthcare AI solutions as “high risk” and requires strict
pre-market testing, validation, and monitoring.11 These international regulatory approaches can act as
an instructive comparison for the HHS. The EU AI act implementation began in February 2025
with compliance deadlines extending through July 202512, establishing one of the world’s most
stringent regulatory regimes. In contrast, the United Kingdom has chosen to not pass specific AI
laws, but instead encourages regulators to apply existing technology-neutral legislation while
adopting a pro-innovation stance.13 Australia has similarly taken an approach of prioritizing a
national risk-based framework without adopting broader AI legislation.14

Rather than implementing a comprehensive regulatory structure, HHS proposes using
regulation, reimbursement, and research & development to accelerate AI adoption while maintaining
public trust. THe RFI explicitly acknowledges the “inherent flaws in legacy payment systems” and
seeks to “ensure that the potential promises of AI innovations are not diminished through inertia.”
This approach represents a bet that market-oriented incentives and strategic R&D investments can
achieve faster AI adoption and better health outcomes than Europe’s precautionary regulatory
model, while avoiding the innovation-stifling effects of slow reimbursement or regulations that have
historically plagued US healthcare technology adoption.

Policy Gaps
Although integrating AI to strengthen healthcare is a promising goal, practical
implementation challenges remain significant. When supporting the hopeful long-term viability, key
elements identified in the proposed policy must be carefully evaluated.
Elements including broad data collection requirements and AI adoption reliance can burden
smaller organizations and local communities. This is true especially for areas with limited resources
as this could reduce participation and limit the policy's overall desired impact. Thus, to minimize risk
and enhance impact, targeted amendments and improvements can be utilized to improve
effectiveness.
When applied, this implementation would demand expanded information collection from a
diverse, wide range of stakeholders among healthcare providers, manufacturers, distributors, and
specified private sector organizations. The collected data could support planning, allow for early
identification of vulnerabilities in the supply chain, and also improve the initiative's alignment with
other national preparedness acts. Over time, AI-based tools could enhance the supply chain's speed
and accuracy in monitoring. Furthermore, this infrastructure could also support rapid AI
deployment in urgent public health emergencies.
Despite these anticipated benefits, potential challenges are still present. First, the scale may
impose a disproportionate burden on smaller organizations, communities, and rural partners.
Reporting requirements and participation in surveys, interviews, and other methods of engagement
may potentially strain limited staffing environments and capacity, reducing participation and creating
disproportionate data quality.
Additionally, long-term established reliance on AI systems risks worsening inequalities in
adoption systems. Well-resourced institutions can more easily adopt advanced AI than underfunded
regions, creating unequal benefits and skewed data representation. Similar disparities emerged
following the implementation of the HITECH Act and its Meaningful Use incentives. Researchers
observed that rural and smaller healthcare providers adopted electronic health records at

significantly lower rates than larger, urban providers, largely due to resource constraints17. These
historical inequities clearly highlight the need for deliberate measures to ensure equitable AI
implementation.
Finally, implementation must be carefully managed to avoid delays and setbacks that could
undermine trust among healthcare professionals and between administrators and the public.
Recommendations
To address these risks, the following recommendations should be considered:
● Streamline data collection processes to reduce administrative burden.
● Provide targeted support for smaller and rural healthcare organizations.
● Establish clear regulatory guidelines for AI adoption in clinical settings.
● Implement continuous monitoring and evaluation mechanisms.
● Foster cross-sector collaboration between federal agencies and healthcare providers.
Policy Implications
These proposed recommendations will accelerate the adoption of AI within healthcare,
providing both benefits and challenges. By encouraging the use of AI to enhance supply chain
resilience, these recommendations will improve the speed and accuracy of healthcare logistics
nationwide. Data collected from healthcare providers, manufacturers, distributors, and private
organizations could allow for early identification of risks and, in turn, create quicker and more
effective responses to public health emergencies. In the long term, this could create a more
responsive healthcare system capable of managing large scale crises.
There are several implications which warrant careful consideration. First, the extensive data
collection and AI adoption may place a disproportionate burden on small healthcare organizations,

improvements can ensure AI integration is effective without widening existing inequalities.
Conclusion
The Department of Health and Human Services’ (HHS) initiative to accelerate AI adoption
across the healthcare sector reflects an important step in the modernization of the U.S. healthcare
system. The integration of AI offers the potential to enhance productivity, improve care delivery, and
strengthen the healthcare supply chain. However, the success of this effort will depend on a dual
focus on innovation and equity. This policy must take into consideration the steps to ensure that
smaller and rural healthcare providers are not excluded from the benefits of AI due to resource
constraints or regulatory uncertainty.
To realize AI’s promise effectively, HHS must pursue streamlined data collection, targeted
technical and financial support to under-resourced institutions, and transparent regulatory guidelines.
Furthermore, continuous evaluation and stakeholder engagement will help create and maintain
public trust and ensure that AI adoption is aligned with ethics.
