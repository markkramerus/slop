and do not create new barriers.
AI Use in Coverage Decisions and Prior Authorization
One of the most consequential and concerning applications of AI in health care today is its
use by payers in coverage determinations and prior authorization. Lawmakers across the
political spectrum have acknowledged that traditional prior authorization processes already
delay or deny necessary care. In a recent hearing held by the Energy and Commerce
Committee, Committee Chair Brett Guthrie raised concerns about the impacts of AI, stating
that “people are being denied care.” Committee Ranking Member Frank Pallone similarly
warned that new AI-driven models risk creating “another barrier” for patients who already
face significant obstacles to accessing care.
The use of AI to automate or accelerate coverage decisions raises the stakes further. When
algorithms are trained on historical spending patterns rather than clinical need, they risk
embedding past inequities into future decisions at scale. Lower spending is often
misinterpreted as lower need, disproportionately affecting low-income, rural, and minority
patients who have historically faced barriers to care. We have long held concerns about the
influence of cost effectiveness analyses using quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and similar
measures that devalue people with disabilities and chronic conditions, underscoring the
need for transparency of the data used to automate coverage decisions. Speed alone does not
equal value if it comes at the cost of accuracy, fairness, or transparency.
Patient Harm, Bias, and Accountability Risks
2

The consequences of AI-driven denials are not hypothetical. National survey data show that
93 percent of physicians report prior authorization harms clinical outcomes, and nearly one
in four has witnessed a serious adverse event — including hospitalization, disability, or
death — linked to delayed or denied care. 1 Data showing that the majority of appealed
denials are overturned suggests that many initial decisions are wrong, yet patients bear the
burden of delay, confusion, and financial stress in the meantime.2
Bias in AI systems can go unnoticed until harm is already widespread. One widely cited
example involved an algorithm used to identify patients for high-risk care management
programs that significantly under-identified Black patients by relying on historical health
care spending as a proxy for need.3 Black patients have historically received less care despite
similar or greater illness burden, leading the algorithm to systematically exclude patients
who would have benefited from additional support. When AI equates spending with need, it
quietly carries inequities forward.
The Importance of Human Oversight and Transparency
AI tools should support, not replace, clinical judgment. When AI is used to inform clinical
decision-making, clinicians should be transparent with patients about when and how these
tools are assisting their care. Patients consistently express concern that increasing reliance
on automated systems may erode the human connection that is central to effective,
compassionate health care. Clear communication and clinician accountability are essential
to maintaining trust and ensuring that AI augments — rather than substitutes for —
meaningful patient–provider relationships.
Maintaining meaningful human oversight is particularly important in clinical settings, where
AI-generated insights may influence diagnosis, treatment planning, or disease management.
Over-reliance on automated recommendations risks undermining clinician expertise and
could lead to inappropriate care if outputs are accepted without sufficient clinical context. AI
systems should be designed to function as decision-support tools, with clinicians retaining
full responsibility for interpreting and applying information based on individual patient
needs.
Transparency must also extend to data governance. Patients should retain ownership of
their personal health data, with clear protections governing how data are collected, used,

When considering potential policy changes, HHS should prioritize programmatic and
payment policies that require meaningful human review and transparency for AI-enabled
tools used in clinical care and administrative decision-making. This includes establishing
standards for disclosure, auditability, data protections, and appeal rights when AI systems
are used in Medicare and other federally-supported programs. Existing policies governing
utilization management and clinical decision support should be revisited to ensure
automated tools do not supplant clinical judgment or undermine beneficiary protections.
Building Patient Trust Through Governance and Participation
A core challenge in the current AI landscape is that patients — the individuals most directly
affected by AI-enabled decisions — are rarely meaningfully involved in the design,
governance, or evaluation of these tools. When patient voices and lived experience are
absent, systems may prioritize efficiency or cost savings while overlooking real-world
impacts on access, understanding, and trust.
HHS has an opportunity to set expectations that patient and caregiver input be integrated
throughout the AI lifecycle, from development and testing to deployment and post-market
evaluation. Transparency, shared decision-making, and patient-centered metrics are
essential to building public confidence in AI-enabled care.
Aligning AI Innovation With Public Trust
HHS has stated its commitment to combating fraud, waste, and abuse while ensuring patients
receive appropriate care. These goals are not mutually exclusive. However, AI models that
financially benefit vendors or payers when care is withheld create misaligned incentives that
warrant careful scrutiny. Clinicians have also called for greater investment in AI research so
tools are rigorously tested for safety and effectiveness, deliver clear value, and do not
introduce avoidable risks or costs. To ensure AI advances quality care rather than
exacerbates disparities, HHS should pair stronger evidence generation with policies that
align reimbursement and program design with patient outcomes, not short-term cost
avoidance. AI can be a powerful tool for improving care, but only if accountability,
transparency, and patient protections are built in from the start.
