Subject: Public Comment on Accelerating AI Adoption in Clinical Care
To the Department of Health and Human Services,
As a junior pre-medical student at Stanford University, my perspective on the integration of Artificial
Intelligence (AI) into clinical care is shaped by distinct but complementary experiences: extensive shadowing of
physicians across various clinical settings and my own encounters navigating the healthcare system as a patient.
From the provider side, I have consistently witnessed physicians dedicating a substantial portion of their valuable
time to administrative tasks, particularly documentation and navigating complex, often unintuitive, electronic health
record (EHR) systems. From the patient side, I have experienced firsthand the long wait times, fragmented
communication, and inefficiencies that define much of the modern healthcare experience. It is from this dual
vantage point that I offer this comment, emphasizing the strategic deployment of AI to alleviate these burdens,
enhance provider productivity, and ultimately strengthen the patient-doctor relationship.
One of the most immediate and impactful applications of AI in clinical care lies in the automation of
documentation. My shadowing experiences have frequently shown doctors spending hours on "charting," often
extending beyond their scheduled clinical shifts. This administrative overhead is a critical contributor to physician
burnout and directly reduces the time available for meaningful patient engagement. The widespread adoption of AI-
powered ambient listening tools, capable of recording and summarizing patient encounters into detailed clinical
notes, is essential. By integrating such technologies into the clinical workflow, HHS can help redirect the physician's
attention back to the patient, rather than the computer screen. Furthermore, by reducing the time required for each
patient encounter through automated documentation, AI can effectively augment the capacity of our existing
physician workforce, thereby alleviating the growing national doctor shortage without compromising the quality of
care.
The U.S. healthcare system also grapples with exceptionally high administrative costs when compared to
international counterparts, such as Taiwan. A considerable portion of this financial burden originates from intricate

2
billing and coding requirements. AI presents a significant opportunity to streamline these processes. Specifically, AI
should be leveraged to automate administrative billing and insurance claims processing. This would not only lead to
a substantial reduction in administrative expenditures but also minimize errors that frequently result in claim denials
and delays in patient care. To prevent the fragmentation that characterized the early adoption of EHRs, HHS must
proactively establish clear standards for the use of AI in billing and administrative tasks. Without a unified
framework for these AI tools, there is a considerable risk of creating new layers of incompatibility, which would
ultimately increase rather than decrease costs.
A critical barrier to the broader adoption of AI in healthcare is the substantial "switching cost" associated
with transitioning away from dominant, legacy EHR systems like Epic. While these systems are often criticized for
being slow and unintuitive, they form the operational backbone of most hospital systems. It is my belief that a more
realistic and efficient approach involves the government partnering with these established EHR providers, rather
than expecting nascent startups to completely disrupt the market. HHS should implement incentives designed to
encourage legacy providers to modernize their systems, transforming them into streamlined, AI-powered platforms.
My observations confirm that current EHR interfaces can be taxing and inefficient for providers. Therefore,
government incentives should specifically target the integration of AI-driven, intuitive user interfaces within
existing EHRs to facilitate rapid and widespread adoption. This approach would allow for mass implementation at
an accelerated pace, leading to improved patient-doctor interaction time and helping to address the current shortage
and inefficiencies within our physician population.
Beyond administrative efficiency, AI holds immense potential to improve health equity and access,
particularly for underserved and rural populations where healthcare resources are strained. AI can serve as a crucial
first-line triage tool, helping patients assess the urgency of their symptoms. For instance, an AI-powered initial
screening could help a patient determine whether their condition warrants an immediate, and often hours-long, drive
to an emergency room or if the issue can be safely addressed through a scheduled telehealth visit. By providing this
initial guidance, AI can reduce the burden on over-capacity emergency departments and ensure that limited
healthcare resources are allocated to the most pressing cases.
Furthermore, AI can revolutionize diagnostic processes by enabling the multi-purpose use of medical scans.
Currently, imaging studies like MRI or CT scans are typically ordered to investigate a single condition, representing
a one-dimensional layer of analysis. Research has shown that AI models can be trained to analyze a single scan for a

3
multitude of different conditions simultaneously. HHS should support a future where one scan can yield a broad
range of preliminary diagnoses for a physician to confirm, rather than the current paradigm of one scan for one
issue. This would not only be more efficient but would also increase the likelihood of catching incidental findings
and other potential issues that might otherwise be missed.
Finally, to ensure the successful and safe integration of these tools, AI must become a core component of
clinical training. As a future medical student, I believe it is imperative that the next generation of physicians is
taught not only the fundamentals of medicine but also how to work effectively with AI. This training must go
beyond basic use and include a deep understanding of AI's limitations, including its potential to "hallucinate" or
produce convincingly incorrect answers. Physicians should be trained to prompt AI systems skillfully to reduce error
rates and to critically parse AI-generated information. The goal should not be to wait for a completely error-free AI,
which may never be realized, but to train clinicians to use AI as a powerful, imperfect tool. This approach, where the
human remains in the loop and is accountable for the final judgment, will foster trust and ensure that the
combination is greater than the sum of its parts.
Thank you for the opportunity to provide this perspective.
Sincerely,
Ryan Kern
