1. Executive Summary
Cyberhill, in collaboration with ServiceNow, is pleased to submit this response to the
Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Request for Information regarding the
acceleration of AI adoption in clinical care. As an AI solutions firm with deep expertise within the
Department of War (DoW) and Intelligence Community (IC), Cyberhill brings a unique
perspective on deploying secure, scalable, and governance-compliant AI solutions in highly
regulated environments.
Our response addresses the three core pillars identified by HHS—Regulation, Reimbursement,
and Research & Development—while providing actionable recommendations derived from our
experience deploying agentic AI systems for federal agencies. Our approach emphasizes:
• Security-First Architecture: HIPAA-aligned AI deployments with zero-trust principles
• Knowledge Graph-Powered Intelligence: Ontology-driven clinical decision support
• Agentic AI Workflows: Autonomous clinical task automation with human oversight
• Interoperability by Design: FHIR-native, HL7-compliant data exchange
Our proposed Clinical Care Intelligence Platform (CCIP) leverages the ServiceNow Healthcare
and Life Sciences Service Management platform as its enterprise foundation, augmented with
Cyberhill's custom ontology engineering and agentic AI. This combination delivers a solution
that is both enterprise-grade and clinically intelligent.

4. Responses to Specific Questions
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health
care and its adoption and use in clinical care?
Based on our experience deploying AI systems in highly regulated federal environments, we
identify the following primary barriers:
HIPAA Compliance and PHI Access Control
One of the most significant barriers is ensuring HIPAA compliance when AI systems interact
with clinical data. Unlike traditional rule-based systems, AI models that touch patient data
present complex access control challenges—particularly because AI can produce Protected
Health Information (PHI) in its outputs, even when queried with seemingly innocuous prompts.
This creates a fundamental tension: AI systems require data access to function effectively, but
existing access control frameworks were not designed for systems that generate, infer, or
synthesize PHI. Organizations face uncertainty about minimum necessary standards for AI,
audit trail requirements for AI-generated content, and liability when AI inadvertently discloses
PHI. HHS should provide specific guidance on AI-PHI boundaries, including technical
safeguards for preventing PHI leakage in model outputs and clear standards for AI access
control that balance innovation with patient privacy protection.
Regulatory Uncertainty
The absence of clear regulatory frameworks for non-medical device AI creates significant
uncertainty. Organizations are hesitant to invest in clinical AI when liability, accountability, and
compliance requirements remain undefined. HHS could establish safe harbor provisions for AI
systems meeting specified governance criteria.
Data Interoperability Gaps
Despite progress with FHIR adoption, significant barriers remain in accessing comprehensive,
standardized clinical data. Proprietary data silos, inconsistent implementations of standards,
and limited access to training datasets impede AI development. HHS should accelerate
enforcement of interoperability requirements under the 21st Century Cures Act.
Trust and Explainability Deficits
Our data indicates 61% of executives lack trust in AI outputs. Clinical AI requires explainable
decision-making that clinicians can understand and verify. Investment in explainable AI (XAI)
research and standards for model transparency would address this barrier.
Reimbursement Misalignment
Current fee-for-service models do not incentivize AI adoption. When AI reduces unnecessary
procedures or shortens hospital stays, it may decrease revenue under current payment
structures. Value-based payment models that reward outcomes would better align incentives.
Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should
HHS prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care?
We recommend the following prioritized changes:
Regulatory Recommendations

• Establish clear distinction between AI as medical devices (FDA jurisdiction) and clinical
decision support tools (ONC oversight) per 21st Century Cures Act Section 3060(a)
• Create certification pathways for clinical AI analogous to EHR certification under 45 CFR
Part 170, with specific criteria for safety, effectiveness, and interoperability
• Modernize HIPAA Privacy Rule (45 CFR Part 164) to address AI-specific considerations
including model training on de-identified data and synthetic data generation
Payment Policy Recommendations
• Create new CPT codes for AI-assisted clinical services, enabling direct reimbursement
for AI-enhanced care
• Include AI deployment metrics in Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) quality
measures under 42 CFR Part 414
• Establish shared savings provisions for AI-driven efficiency gains under Medicare
Shared Savings Program (42 CFR Part 425)
Question 3: For non-medical devices, what novel legal and implementation issues exist
and what role should HHS play?
Liability and Accountability
The most significant legal uncertainty concerns liability allocation when AI contributes to
adverse outcomes. Current medical malpractice frameworks assume human decision-making.
HHS should work with stakeholders to develop model liability frameworks that appropriately
allocate responsibility among AI developers, healthcare organizations, and clinicians based on
their respective roles and the degree of AI autonomy.
Privacy and Security
AI systems present novel privacy risks including model inversion attacks, membership
inference, and potential for re-identification of de-identified data. HHS should issue guidance on
AI-specific security controls, potentially through updates to the HIPAA Security Rule technical
safeguards.
Algorithmic Bias
AI systems trained on historical data may perpetuate or amplify existing healthcare disparities.
HHS should establish requirements for bias testing and mitigation, building on the Office of Civil
Rights' authority under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act.
Question 4: What are the most promising AI evaluation methods for clinical care? Should
HHS support these processes?
We recommend HHS support the following evaluation approaches through grants, cooperative
agreements, and prize competitions:
Pre-Deployment Evaluation
• Prospective clinical validation studies with diverse patient populations
• Robustness testing across demographic subgroups and edge cases
• Red team exercises to identify failure modes and adversarial vulnerabilities
• Human factors evaluation assessing clinician-AI interaction patterns

Question 5: How can HHS best support private sector activities to promote innovative
and effective AI use in clinical care?
HHS can most effectively support the private sector through:
Standards Development Support
Fund and participate in standards development organizations (SDOs) working on clinical AI
standards. Support ANSI/AAMI work on AI in medical devices, HL7 efforts on AI data exchange
standards, and IEEE standards for AI transparency. Provide ONC resources to develop clinical
AI certification criteria.
Testing Infrastructure
Establish or fund regional AI testing centers that provide access to synthetic clinical data,
standardized evaluation protocols, and expert consultation. These centers could operate
similarly to ONC-Authorized Testing Laboratories for EHR certification.
Workforce Development
Support training programs for clinical informaticists who can bridge the gap between AI
developers and clinical users. Partner with HRSA on health professions training grants that
include AI literacy components.

Question 10: Are there specific areas of AI research HHS should prioritize?
We recommend HHS prioritize research in the following areas:
High-Priority Research Areas
• Explainable Clinical AI: Methods to make AI reasoning transparent and understandable
to clinicians
• Bias Detection and Mitigation: Techniques to identify and correct algorithmic disparities
• Privacy-Preserving AI: Federated learning and differential privacy for model training
• Human-AI Teaming: Optimal interfaces and workflows for clinician-AI collaboration
• AI Safety and Robustness: Methods to ensure reliable AI performance across diverse
settings
• Implementation Science: Research on effective strategies for AI deployment and
adoption
