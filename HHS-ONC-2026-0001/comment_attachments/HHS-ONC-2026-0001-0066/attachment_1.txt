Public Comment on HHS Request for Information: Accelerating
AI Adoption in Clinical Care
Thank you for the opportunity to comment on this request for information regarding
accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence (AI) in clinical care. Our
organization has extensive experience deploying AI-driven decision-support tools in
obstetrics, which informs our perspective on the opportunities and challenges of
integrating AI into clinical workflows.
Background
We are responding specifically to HHS’s request for input on regulatory frameworks,
reimbursement structures, and research priorities that can enable safe, effective, and
equitable AI adoption in clinical care.
PeriGen is a perinatal technology company that uses supervised machine learning to
provide clinicians with early‑warning notifications of concerning trends in both maternal
and fetal clinical status. PeriGen products are FDA cleared and utilized in 22% of American
hospital and health systems that provide childbirth services. In 2025, our AI obstetric
software provided technology support for 800,000 births. Our comments focus on maternal
and neonatal health, a high-acuity area where AI can significantly improve outcomes.
Analysis
Artificial intelligence offers significant potential to enhance patient safety, reduce clinician
burden, and lower healthcare costs. In obstetrics, for example, predictive analytics can
identify early warning signs of maternal and fetal complications, enabling earlier
intervention and reducing the incidence of preventable adverse events. These events
impose substantial financial, emotional, and reputational costs on patients, families,
communities, and healthcare providers alike. However, several barriers impede adoption:
• Regulatory Uncertainty: Current frameworks for non-device software lack clarity,
which can create delays and discourage innovation.
• Payment Misalignment: Fee-for-service models rarely incentivize preventive
technologies. AI tools that reduce complications and improve efficiency should be
recognized within value-based care programs.
• Data Interoperability: Fragmented systems and inconsistent standards limit AI’s
effectiveness. Enhanced interoperability and pathways for intentional workflow
integration would widen market opportunities and accelerate innovation.
These barriers risk slowing progress toward HHS’s stated goals of improving outcomes and
reducing costs. Additionally, without clear guidance, smaller providers and rural hospitals
may struggle to implement AI solutions, exacerbating health disparities.

Recommendations
To address these challenges, we recommend that HHS:
1. Modernize Regulatory Pathways: Establish clear, proportionate guidelines for AI-
enabled clinical tools, including non-device software, while maintaining patient
safety and trust.
2. Align Reimbursement with Outcomes: Create payment policies that incentivize
technologies proven to reduce complications and improve efficiency.
3. Invest in Interoperability and Research: Support initiatives that enhance data
liquidity and fund applied research to validate AI’s impact on clinical outcomes,
particularly in complex care scenarios such as obstetrics.
4. Prioritize Equity: Ensure implementation strategies include support for resource-
limited settings to prevent widening disparities.
Conclusion
AI can be a powerful tool to improve maternal and neonatal outcomes, reduce costs, and
enhance provider efficiency. By modernizing regulations, aligning reimbursement, and
investing in interoperability and research, HHS can accelerate safe and equitable adoption
of AI in clinical care.
