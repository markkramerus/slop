The Markkula Center for Applied Ethics at Santa Clara University appreciates the opportunity to
inform federal strategy for accelerating the responsible adoption of artificial intelligence in
clinical care. Our work sits at the intersection of healthcare delivery, biotechnology, and ethics.
AI capability is no longer the primary constraint. Integration is.
Across the country, technically sound systems stall before routine use because of procurement
timelines, uncertainty about liability, fragmented validation practices, and payment models that
do not reward safe implementation. The central challenge for federal policy is institutional rather
than computational.
To move from isolated pilots to systemic improvement, HHS can use existing statutory and
programmatic authorities to create clearer expectations for transparency, oversight, and
economic viability. Below we respond directly to priority domains raised in the RFI.
Specific Responses to RFI Questions
1. Barriers to Private Sector Innovation and Clinical Adoption
The major obstacles are operational friction and trust gaps.
Procurement versus model evolution. Health system purchasing and integration cycles can
extend well beyond a year. During that time, clinical practices, patient populations, and data
environments change. Without mechanisms for reassessment and updating, systems risk
implementing tools whose original validation assumptions no longer match reality.

Liability uncertainty. Health systems and clinicians remain unclear about how responsibility

Workflow failure. Tools that increase alerts or cognitive burden without fitting existing care

2. Regulatory and Programmatic Priorities
HHS already possesses meaningful policy levers through ONC Health IT Certification, CMS
payment policy, and FDA device authority. The opportunity is alignment and operational
execution across these mechanisms rather than creation of new ones.
Recent reporting underscores why implementation details matter. A Reuters investigation
examined post-market device reports associated with Johnson & Johnson subsidiary Acclarent’s
TruDi Navigation System after artificial intelligence capabilities were introduced. Reuters found
that, following the AI addition, FDA received a substantially larger number of unconfirmed
malfunction and adverse event reports, including allegations that surgeons were misinformed
about instrument location during sinus procedures. The article also emphasized a critical
limitation: Medical Device Reports are not designed to establish causality and may be
incomplete.
Regardless of ultimate causation, these reports demonstrate an operational reality. Frontline
users, purchasers, and health systems often do not have easy access to clear, comparable,
version-specific information describing what changed, what evidence supports the current
version, the contexts in which performance is validated, and the conditions necessary for safe
use.
ONC Health IT Certification
45 CFR § 170.315(b)(11)
Current certification criteria for Predictive Decision Support Interventions already require
extensive transparency through “source attributes.” These include intended use, intended users,
patient populations, development data characteristics including demographic representativeness,
input features, external validation processes, quantitative performance measures, ongoing
monitoring expectations, and update or maintenance information.

However, these disclosures are frequently difficult to compare across products and are not
consistently structured for operational decision making.
Recommended federal action
ASTP/ONC should translate existing disclosure obligations into a standardized, computable
format. A model-card style artifact aligned with the National Institute of Standards and
Technology AI Risk Management Framework could be machine-readable and consistently
presented across vendors.
At minimum, it should identify:
1. the exact software and model version in clinical use
2. a clear history of modifications affecting AI functionality
3. validation scope for each version
4. known limitations, contraindicated uses, and foreseeable failure modes
5. required user competencies, training, and workflow dependencies
The central gap is not absence of authority. It is standardization, usability, and the ability of real
organizations to operationalize what is already required.
FDA statutory framework
FDCA § 520(o)(1)(E)(iii)
Software may fall outside device regulation only if a healthcare professional can independently
review the basis of the recommendation so that the professional does not primarily rely on the
software’s output. FDA guidance elaborates four criteria, all of which must be met, with
independent review being pivotal.
For many modern machine-learning systems, what constitutes sufficient independent review
remains uncertain for developers, purchasers, and clinicians.
Recommended federal action
HHS should promote tighter coordination between FDA and ASTP/ONC so expectations for
transparency, explainability, and documentation are interpreted consistently across certification
and device policy. Greater clarity would reduce regulatory arbitrage and market confusion.

In safety-critical environments, FDA should further operationalize independent review through
explicit expectations regarding human factors, training, labeling, and post-market monitoring
when AI functionality is added to products already in use.
The Reuters findings regarding increased reporting following the TruDi AI introduction do not
determine fault. They do, however, illustrate why version control, user-visible change
communication, and actionable documentation are essential components of safe deployment.
Manufacturers of AI-enabled systems should therefore be expected to maintain and provide
customers with version-specific safety materials, including update-triggered risk analyses, user-
facing limitations, and monitoring plans capable of identifying performance drift or workflow
confusion early.
3. Legal and Implementation Issues for Non-Device AI
For functions such as ambient documentation or generative drafting, the unresolved question is
allocation of responsibility.
Vendor agreements frequently designate the clinician as the final authority. Yet in high-
throughput environments, time pressure can limit meaningful verification.
Recommended federal action:
HHS can convene stakeholders and publish model contractual principles or safe-harbor
approaches that distinguish:
• systematic or repeatable model defects, and
• patient-specific clinical judgment.
This would create predictability without requiring new legislation.
4. Evaluation Infrastructure
Today, validation is fragmented. Hospitals repeatedly test similar tools with limited resources,
producing inconsistent evidence.
Recommended federal action:
HHS, including NIH and AHRQ, should support regional or federated evaluation collaboratives

5. Reimbursement and Incentives
Payment policy heavily shapes behavior.
If AI saves time, adoption is straightforward. If AI improves quality but requires additional
clinician effort to verify safely, organizations may be penalized financially.
Recommended federal actions:
• CMS should explore billing mechanisms or add-on payments that recognize clinician
time spent validating complex algorithmic outputs.
• MIPS and other quality programs could reward documented governance practices such as
bias auditing, monitoring, and incident reporting.
• CMS could evaluate pathways that allow qualifying Software as a Medical Device
products to demonstrate value through reductions in utilization or complications.
6. Evidence from Successes and Failures
Federal policy should follow real-world performance, not marketing claims.
Example of relative success: Autonomous diabetic retinopathy screening achieved uptake in
part because it fit a defined workflow and aligned with reimbursement structures.
Example of caution: Independent evaluation of a widely used proprietary sepsis model found
poor sensitivity and large numbers of false alarms. The lesson is straightforward. Performance in
development environments does not guarantee effectiveness in live care settings.
Recommended federal action:
Strengthen expectations for post-deployment monitoring and transparent reporting of real-world
outcomes.

8. Interoperability and Data Standards
Without reliable metadata, learning health systems risk confusing AI-generated content with
human-authored information.
Recommended federal action:
ASTP/ONC should continue work through USCDI+ and FHIR implementation guidance so that
provenance information can identify which system generated a recommendation, under what
version, and at what time.
10. Research Priorities
The most urgent gaps are in implementation science.
We need longitudinal evidence regarding automation bias, clinician trust calibration, and
whether reliance on AI alters professional skill development over time.
The United States does not lack innovation capacity. It lacks predictable, repeatable pathways to
deploy innovation responsibly.
By standardizing transparency under existing ONC authority, aligning interpretation of FDA’s
statutory CDS boundaries, modernizing incentives within CMS, and investing in shared
evaluation infrastructure, HHS can transform trustworthy AI from aspiration into operational
reality.
