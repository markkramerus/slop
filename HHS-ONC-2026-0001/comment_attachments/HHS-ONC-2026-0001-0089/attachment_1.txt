1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption
and use in clinical care?
From our perspective as an active deployer of clinical AI, several interconnected barriers stand out:
● Misaligned payment incentives: Current fee-for-service structures reward visit length rather than
efficiency or quality. Tools that dramatically reduce documentation time or enable more consistent
follow-up do not generate proportional revenue under traditional billing, discouraging broader
adoption even when they clearly improve care delivery and provider well-being.
● Regulatory uncertainty for non-device AI: Ambient documentation, chart auditing, and
workflow agents occupy an ambiguous space, not traditional SaMD (software as a medical
device) but still carrying potential liability exposure in audits, malpractice claims, or enforcement
actions. The lack of clear, proportionate guidance creates hesitation among providers and
platforms.
● Evaluation and trust gaps: Without standardized benchmarks for behavioral health AI
performance (e.g., accuracy in capturing emotional nuance or avoiding cultural bias), it is hard to
build confidence or compare solutions.
● Complex web of regulators with differences in priorities: Behavioural health is regulated by
federal and state agencies, including DEA, CMS, SAMHSA, HHS OCR, state licensing boards,
and law enforcement bodies, each enforcing overlapping but not fully aligned rules on prescribing,
privacy, reimbursement, and professional conduct. With FDA approvals necessary for CDS
systems, navigating this complex web of regulatory bodies is not only complicated but potentially
unresolved in the current legal framework.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why?
We recommend the following prioritized actions, each of which would accelerate responsible adoption
based on our internal results:
● CMS payment modernization: Introduce reimbursement mechanisms (e.g., add-on codes,
modifiers, or value-based incentives) that explicitly recognize time savings and outcome
improvements from AI-augmented services, ambient documentation, real-time compliance review,
or automated adherence support. When efficiency translates directly to financial benefit, adoption
surges.
● Proportionate, clear guidance for non-device AI: Publish targeted HHS/ONC/ASTP guidance
distinguishing low-risk assistive tools (requiring transparency, consent, and human review) from
higher-risk diagnostic/predictive software. This reduces uncertainty without imposing unnecessary
pre-market burdens.
● Establish a cross-agency AI in behavioural health committee. Given the distinct nature of the
agencies that regulate behavioural health, a cross-agency body (including the DEA and FDA)
focused on resolving barriers to AI innovation would significantly accelerate unified
decision-making across multiple regulatory landscapes.
● Behavioral health-focused interoperability enhancements: Expand the United States Core Data
for Interoperability (USCDI) to better include mental health elements such as visit transcripts,

3–5. For non-medical-device AI tools: What novel issues arise? What evaluation methods seem
most promising? What should HHS's role be in supporting private-sector evaluation activities?
Novel issues
● Consent and transparency for ambient audio processing (e.g., granular disclosure, handling of
bystanders, easy opt-out).
● Liability allocation in multi-vendor ecosystems (who is responsible if an AI-drafted note
contributes to an oversight?).
● Heightened privacy risks with conversational data in mental health contexts.
● Subtle biases in language models that may misinterpret cultural idioms, emotional tone, or
non-verbal cues.
● Security and privacy attack vectors (such as prompt injection), which are already managed using
guardrails (automated systems that review AI before and after it is invoked).
● The non-deterministic nature of AI means that different results can be obtained for the same input.
Strategies like averaging across multiple tries or evaluations help manage that.
● Workflow disruption if tools require excessive clinician editing or produce inconsistent outputs.
Promising evaluation methods
● Pre- and post-deployment comparative studies measuring note accuracy (vs. gold-standard manual
drafts), time saved, patient satisfaction, and clinical outcomes.
● Continuous real-world monitoring dashboards tracking error rates, human escalation frequency,
bias indicators across demographics, and provider-reported usability.
● Simulation-based testing under realistic conditions (accents, background noise, interrupted
sessions).
● Longitudinal tracking of downstream effects (e.g., improved follow-up rates, medication
adherence, or reduced no-shows due to less admin burden).
● Using a separate instance of AI to evaluate (LLM as a Judge) the outputs of the original AI system
for compliance with a specified guideline, rule, or condition.
● Human annotation of AI outputs feeds back into improvement in how AI is set up.
● Evaluating alignment between independent AI output and human output.

6. Where have AI tools in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost expectations, and
where have they fallen short? What novel AI tools have the greatest potential?
Exceeded expectations
Our ambient Chart Generation AI routinely produces notes that are more complete and chronologically
accurate than manual drafts, with clinicians editing in under two minutes instead of 10–15. Chart Review
AI flags compliance and quality issues at 100% scale, impossible manually, helping maintain standards
without added overhead. Chart Review AI doesn’t replace human oversight and supervision, which is
performed to the full degree necessary by relevant compliance requirements, but provides an additional
layer of protection. These have yielded measurable time savings (hours of work per day in case of Chart
Generation AI), fewer documentation deficiencies, and improved provider satisfaction.
Fallen short
Generic foundation models without behavioral health fine-tuning often miss sarcasm, contextual history,
or cultural expressions of distress, producing bland or inaccurate outputs.
Given that AI systems require continuous improvement and caution should be exercised, Chart Review
AI can be overly restrictive in its judgments, resulting in false positives (raising a red flag where a clinical
committee doesn’t see one).
Greatest potential
Modular, supervised AI agents for: routine follow-ups and dosage titration; real-time evidence-based
suggestion during visits; proactive compliance auditing; and scalable virtual support in high-shortage
regions. In mental health, these could expand access dramatically while preserving human judgment for
complex cases.

9. What challenges and concerns do patients and caregivers have regarding AI in clinical care?
Patients value faster access, reduced wait times, and providers who appear more engaged. Many
appreciate AI freeing clinicians from typing. Primary concerns include:
● Privacy of recorded conversations.
● Fear that AI could diminish empathy or replace human judgment.
● Potential for bias disadvantaging certain cultural or linguistic groups.
We mitigate these through clear upfront consent, visible opt-out mechanisms, explicit statements that
clinicians retain final decision-making authority, and transparent explanations of how tools are used.
10. What specific AI research areas should HHS prioritize?
● Multimodal models combining audio, text, validated scales, and outcomes for improved
behavioral health support.
● Implementation science examining AI integration into telehealth without disrupting therapeutic
relationships.
● Cost-effectiveness analyses in value-based care settings.
● Equity and bias research ensuring performance across demographics, languages, and
socioeconomic groups.
● Public-private pilots generating real-world evidence in shortage areas.
● Patient risk modelling for adverse side effects and drug abuse potential
