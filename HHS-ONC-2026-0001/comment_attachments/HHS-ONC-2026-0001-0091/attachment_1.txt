US Healthcare Current State – Evidence Gap (AI in Healthcare):
While healthcare data is now abundant, it remains siloed and difficult to transform into actionable
evidence. Today, only 14% of daily medical decisions are based on high-quality evidence—a disconnect
known as “The Evidence Gap.” Traditional clinical trials are limited and exclude nearly 70% of real-world
patients, including those with comorbidities, as well as underrepresented populations such as minorities,
children, and seniors, among others.
Generating high-quality insights typically takes months and significant resources, confining the process to
expensive, academic research settings. The current system places significant strain on U.S. healthcare:
many patients do not receive evidence-based care, leading to avoidable adverse outcomes, under or
delayed treatments, and increased costs.

Specific Questions
In addition to the general requests for information above regarding AI regulation,
reimbursement, and research & development, HHS seeks input on the following specific
questions:
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption
and use in clinical care?
The biggest barriers to private sector AI innovation and adoption in clinical care are regulatory
and liability uncertainty, slow and fragmented health system governance, and lack of
standardized evaluation and procurement pathways. These are compounded by difficult EHR
integration, data access and interoperability constraints.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or
programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care? Please
provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.

3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel legal
and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures (
e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
For non-medical device AI used in clinical care, novel legal and implementation challenges
include unclear liability and indemnification when AI-informed decisions contribute to harm,
ambiguity around accountability between clinicians, health systems, and vendors, and evolving
privacy and security risks associated with large-scale data use and model updating. These
challenges strain existing governance structures and contribute to conservative adoption. HHS
can play a critical role by clarifying liability and accountability expectations, issuing guidance on
privacy, security, and responsible data use for clinical AI, and promoting standardized
governance and contracting frameworks that reduce risk and administrative friction while
protecting patients.
4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and post-
deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered evaluation
methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so, which
mechanisms would be most impactful ( e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or
prize competitions)?
For non-medical device AI in clinical care, the most effective evaluation combines strong pre-
deployment validation with continuous post-deployment monitoring and human-centered
workflow assessment. Before go-live, models should be tested across sites and subpopulations,
evaluated in silent trials, assessed for usability and workflow fit, and stress-tested for robustness
to data shifts. After deployment, ongoing monitoring for performance drift, safety, and real-world
clinical and operational impact is essential. Evaluation should focus not only on accuracy, but
also calibration, clinical utility, equity, and clinician interaction. HHS support would meaningfully
accelerate safe and scalable adoption by establishing shared evaluation infrastructure and
standards, with cooperative agreements, targeted grants, and reference implementations as the
most impactful mechanisms.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities ( e.g., accreditation, certification, industry-
driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the
greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help
reduce costs?

The greatest future impact will come from actionable, transparent decision support grounded in
real-world evidence at the point of care; multimodal tools that improve high-stakes medication
decisions and prevent adverse events; and AI that surfaces care gaps and quality and equity
insights in real time. These approaches offer the strongest path to improving outcomes and
quality while reducing avoidable utilization and clinician burden. Atropos has proven this at
scale working with leading health system organizations across the US.
7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations have
the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary administrative
hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
The adoption of AI for clinical care is most strongly influenced by clinical leadership (CMO,
CMIO, CNIO, department chairs), who determine clinical relevance and frontline trust; executive
leadership (CEO, COO, CFO), who set strategic priorities, risk tolerance, and funding; IT and AI
governance bodies (CIO, digital health and AI committees), who control integration, security,
and scalability; and compliance, legal, and quality leadership, who shape regulatory
interpretation, liability posture, and patient safety standards. The primary administrative hurdles
include fragmented governance and slow, multi-layered approval processes; regulatory and
liability uncertainty around clinical AI; integration challenges with EHRs and clinical workflows;
lack of standardized evidence and evaluation frameworks to support purchasing decisions; data
governance and interoperability constraints; and procurement and contracting processes that
are misaligned with the pace of AI innovation.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and
accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data types, data
standards, and benchmarking tools.
Enhanced interoperability would materially expand market opportunities, accelerate innovation,
and improve the real-world impact of AI in clinical care by enabling timely, standardized access
to high-quality longitudinal data across settings and populations. The greatest impact would
come from broader adoption of common data standards for structured EHR data to support
generalizable real-world evidence; improved linkage of clinical, utilization, pharmacy, claims,
and patient-reported outcomes data across care settings to assess downstream outcomes,
safety, and cost; and the development of shared benchmarking and evaluation frameworks so
AI tools can be consistently validated and trusted across institutions and populations..
9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the
adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers have
related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
Patients and caregivers want AI in clinical care to address persistent challenges such as
variation in care, delays in diagnosis and treatment, lack of personalization, and limited

transparency in clinical decision-making. AI tools that leverage real-world clinical data can help
clinicians make more consistent, timely, and patient-specific decisions by showing how similar
patients have been treated and what outcomes were achieved.
At the same time, patients and caregivers express concerns about trust, bias, and the
appropriate role of AI in care. They are wary of AI systems that appear to replace clinician
judgment, lack transparency, or are trained on non-representative data. Patients also
emphasize the importance of data privacy, equity, and clear accountability for clinical decisions.
Overall, patients and caregivers are most supportive of AI that augments clinician decision-
making with interpretable, real-world evidence, improves equity and consistency of care, and
operates within strong governance and privacy safeguards.
