February 18, 2026
Re: Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part
of Clinical Care (RIN 0955–AA13)
Dear Deputy Secretary and ASTP/ONC Leadership,
Thank you for the opportunity to provide input on this Request for Information. I am an
independent healthcare technology consultant with a background in quality improvement and
healthcare operations. My comments are informed by direct experience advising healthcare
organizations on technology adoption, clinical workflow optimization, and regulatory
compliance. I submit these comments as an individual.
AI adoption in clinical care is accelerating rapidly, and this momentum should be sustained.
However, the speed of adoption must be matched with proportionate diligence to ensure that AI
tools serve the best interests of patients and practitioners alike. My comments address four areas
of the RFI where I believe HHS action can have the greatest impact.
Question 1: Barriers to Innovation and Adoption
Data fragmentation remains the most significant structural barrier to effective AI in clinical care.
Health data continues to be siloed across delivery systems, payers, and public health entities,
limiting the representativeness and quality of datasets that underpin AI models. When AI
algorithms are trained on incomplete or non-representative data, the resulting tools risk
perpetuating existing health disparities and producing biased clinical recommendations. Research
has documented that AI bias in biomedicine can originate from insufficient data, sampling bias,
and the use of health-irrelevant features (Yang et al., Journal of Biomedical Informatics, 2024,
DOI: 10.1016/j.jbi.2024.104646).
HHS should prioritize policies that enable responsible data sharing across care settings while
maintaining rigorous patient privacy protections. Breaking down data silos through expanded
interoperability requirements, standardized data formats, and clear safe harbor provisions for
data sharing in AI development will empower AI developers to build tools grounded in high-
quality, representative datasets. High data quality is the prerequisite for high algorithmic
accuracy, and high accuracy is the prerequisite for patient safety.
Additionally, small and medium-sized practices face disproportionate barriers to AI adoption due
to limited capital, technical infrastructure, and administrative bandwidth. HHS should consider
incentive structures (whether through grants, technical assistance programs, or favorable
reimbursement policies) that lower the adoption threshold for these organizations, ensuring the
benefits of clinical AI are not concentrated among large health systems.
An emerging dimension of this challenge is the growing ability of clinicians themselves to
develop AI tools tailored to their practice. The increasing accessibility of open-source AI models

and low-code development platforms means that a physical therapist can now build a pose
estimation tool for movement assessment, or a primary care clinic can develop a predictive
scheduling model using their own operational data. These clinician-developed tools have the
advantage of being built by domain experts who understand the clinical context intimately.
However, most clinicians building these tools lack the regulatory and compliance expertise to
evaluate whether their creation crosses the threshold into software as a medical device (SaMD)
territory, or to conduct appropriate clinical validation. HHS should support this practitioner-led
innovation through accessible compliance guidance, technical assistance resources, and grant
programs that help clinicians build safe, validated tools while making clear that any AI tool used
in clinical workflows must be developed and applied within the clinician’s scope of practice and
domain expertise.
Questions 3–4: Evaluation Methods, Legal Issues, and Accountability
For non-medical device AI tools used in clinical care, evaluation frameworks must reflect the
clinical context in which these tools operate. Specifically, the calibration of false positive and
false negative rates should be aligned to the clinical risk profile of the use case. An AI tool that
assists in screening for a life-threatening condition requires a fundamentally different error
tolerance than one optimizing appointment scheduling. HHS should encourage or support the
development of risk-stratified evaluation standards that require AI developers to demonstrate
how their models manage the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity relative to patient
harm.
Liability and Accountability
A central governance challenge for clinical AI is aligning accountability with decision-making
authority. I offer the following framework for the Department’s consideration, built on the
distinction between human-in-the-loop and autonomous AI architectures:
• Augmented workflows (human-in-the-loop): When an AI tool provides clinical
decision support and a licensed clinician retains decision-making authority,
accountability for patient outcomes should rest with the clinician, consistent with existing
standards of professional practice. The AI tool is an instrument; the clinician is the
decision-maker. This is no different from a clinician who misinterprets a diagnostic
image or disregards a contraindication, the professional who exercises judgment bears
responsibility for that judgment.
• Autonomous workflows (no human-in-the-loop): When an AI tool operates without
clinician intervention at the point of clinical decision, accountability should be shared
between the deploying organization and the AI product owner. The deploying health
system bears responsibility analogous to its existing credentialing and privileging
obligations: it must validate, monitor, and govern the AI tool’s performance within its
care environment, just as it would a newly credentialed clinician. The AI product owner

carries product liability for the tool performing as represented. This dual accountability
mirrors existing institutional and professional liability structures and provides a familiar
governance model for health systems.
Scope of Practice Congruency
Regardless of whether an AI tool operates in an augmented or autonomous capacity, its use and
validation should be bounded by scope of practice and domain expertise. It would not be
appropriate for a physical therapist to rely on a clinical decision support tool designed for
neurologists, nor for a clinic to deploy a near-autonomous surgical AI system outside the
oversight of appropriately credentialed surgical professionals. Validation of autonomous AI tools
should follow peer-level review logic, which is analogous to the peer review processes used in
quality management systems, where the evaluators possess domain expertise congruent with the
tool’s clinical function. HHS guidance on clinical AI governance should reinforce this principle
to prevent scope mismatches that could compromise patient safety.
Transparency and Automation Bias Mitigation
Even in human-in-the-loop architectures, automation bias presents a material patient safety risk.
Clinicians may defer to AI output without exercising independent judgment, particularly under
time pressure or when the tool’s reasoning is opaque. Addressing this risk requires action on two
fronts:
1. AI developers should be expected to design tools that are transparent in their reasoning,
surface confidence levels, and clearly communicate limitations, enabling meaningful
clinician oversight rather than perfunctory confirmation.
2. Clinical training programs and continuing education should evolve to build competency
in evaluating AI-generated recommendations critically, treating AI literacy as a core
clinical skill rather than an elective technical interest.
Scoping reviews of EHR-integrated digital technologies have identified challenges including
alert fatigue, workflow integration difficulties, and algorithmic bias, with a noted research gap on
whether these tools address or worsen health inequities (Murthi et al., Journal of Medical
Systems, 2024, DOI: 10.1007/s10916-024-02097-5). These findings underscore the need for
post-deployment surveillance that accounts for differential performance across patient
populations and for transparency requirements that support genuine clinical oversight.
Question 6: Where AI Has Met or Fallen Short of Expectations
Ambient AI documentation tools represent one of the most promising and well-evidenced
applications of AI in clinical care today. A prospective study at Stanford Health Care found that
ambient AI scribes produced statistically significant reductions in physician task load and
burnout, with improvements in usability and perceived documentation quality (Shah et al.,
JAMIA, 2025, DOI: 10.1093/jamia/ocae295). Similarly, a quality improvement study at the

University of Kansas found that an ambient AI platform significantly improved clinician
perceptions of documentation ease and reduced after-hours EHR work, with 48% of respondents
reporting capacity to see an additional patient (Albrecht et al., JAMIA Open, 2025, DOI:
10.1093/jamiaopen/ooaf013).
A 2025 systematic review confirmed these patterns across multiple implementations, finding that
9 of 10 studies reported efficiency improvements and 7 of 10 reported positive effects on
clinician wellness (Hassan et al., Applied Clinical Informatics, 2025, DOI: 10.1055/a-2597-
2017). However, the same review noted that AI scribe accuracy varies and frequently requires
manual edits, reinforcing the need for continued clinician oversight even in well-performing
applications.
Reducing clinician burnout is not merely a workforce satisfaction issue, it is a patient safety
imperative. Burned-out clinicians are more prone to medical errors and less able to engage
meaningfully with patients. AI tools that restore clinician time and attention to direct patient care
represent a high-value investment. HHS should consider how reimbursement structures and
programmatic incentives can accelerate adoption of these administrative AI tools, particularly in
resource-constrained settings where documentation burden is most acute.
Question 9: Patient and Caregiver Concerns
Patients and caregivers increasingly encounter AI-generated health information, both within
clinical settings and through direct-to-consumer channels. A critical concern is that patients often
cannot distinguish between reliable, clinically validated AI output and AI-generated content that
may contain inaccuracies or hallucinations. When patients act on erroneous AI-generated
medical information, whether by deferring necessary treatment or pursuing inappropriate
interventions, the consequences can be serious.
HHS should consider how any AI product that provides medical information directly to patients
can be held to standards commensurate with the clinical impact of that information. This does
not necessarily require a single regulatory framework, but it does require clarity on
accountability. Patients deserve to know whether the AI tools informing their care decisions have
been validated against clinical evidence and whether a licensed professional stands behind the
output.
At the same time, AI has significant potential to improve the patient experience by enabling
more personalized treatment recommendations, reducing wait times through more efficient
clinical operations, and supporting shared decision-making between patients and their care
teams. Ensuring that clinical AI tools are built on high-quality, representative data is the
foundation for delivering on this promise equitably across patient populations.

Conclusion
The pace of AI adoption in clinical care should be sustained, but it must be accompanied by
deliberate action to ensure data quality, algorithmic accountability, clinician trust, and patient
safety. As the evidence base for clinical AI matures, HHS should anticipate a future in which the
adoption of validated AI tools becomes an expected component of the standard of care and
should ensure that the regulatory and reimbursement environment supports that transition
responsibly rather than reactively. Accountability structures should align with decision-making
authority, with clinicians responsible for the professional judgment they exercise using AI tools,
and AI product owners and deploying organizations sharing responsibility for autonomous
systems. These frameworks should be grounded in existing clinical governance principles,
including scope of practice, peer-level validation, and continuous quality monitoring, which are
adapted for a technology-augmented care environment.
I would respectfully urge the Department to recognize that rigorous data quality standards and
transparent validation processes are not impediments to rapid adoption: they are prerequisites for
the patient trust that sustains it. Insufficient attention to governance and compliance when AI
innovations enter clinical workflows acts as a silent killer of adoption: trust does not collapse in a
single event but erodes incrementally, degrading patient confidence in AI-assisted care until
adoption stalls and the root cause is difficult to trace. Reducing oversight prematurely risks high-
profile failures that would set back public confidence in clinical AI and ultimately slow the very
adoption trajectory HHS seeks to accelerate. If patients do not trust AI-assisted care, they will
avoid providers who use it, withhold information from AI-enabled systems, or seek redundant
second opinions, all of which undermine the cost savings and outcome improvements that
clinical AI promises. Building trust through demonstrated rigor is the fastest path to durable,
broad-based adoption.
HHS is uniquely positioned to use its regulatory, reimbursement, and research authorities to
create an environment where evidence-based AI tools can flourish—improving outcomes,
reducing burden, and making care more equitable and accessible. I appreciate the Department’s
commitment to seeking broad public input on these critical questions.
