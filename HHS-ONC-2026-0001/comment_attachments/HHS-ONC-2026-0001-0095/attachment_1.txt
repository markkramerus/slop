InStride Health Response to HHS RFI: Accelerating the Adoption
and Use of AI in Clinical Care
About InStride Health
InStride Health is a multi-state pediatric mental health provider delivering specialty care for
children, adolescents, and young adults with complex anxiety and obsessive-compulsive
disorder. As a provider organization directly accountable for clinical outcomes, licensure, and
patient safety, we engage with AI tools as clinical decision support and operational support,
embedded in real-world care delivery.
Our comments reflect direct experience implementing and governing AI-supported clinical
and operational workflows across fragmented state regulatory environments, particularly in
high-risk mental health and pediatric settings, including work examined in a recent case study
conducted in collaboration with The Wharton School’s Health Care Management Department.
The case study evaluated InStride’s implementation of an AI scribe and documented
measurable reductions in clinician documentation burden and improvements in clinician
experience through a human-in-the-loop, clinically governed AI deployment model.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health
care and its adoption and use in clinical care?
InStride Response:
For multi-state healthcare organizations seeking to innovate responsibly, the most immediate
barrier to AI innovation is state-by-state regulatory fragmentation combined with the
absence of clear federal baseline guidance. Requirements governing consent, data use, and
clinical accountability, particularly in mental health and pediatric care, vary significantly across
states. Regulations in states such as Illinois impose stricter standards that often necessitate
designing to the most restrictive interpretation or managing complex, state-specific
implementations. Without a consistent federal framework for AI use in clinical care, this
fragmentation significantly increases development cost, slows testing, and impedes responsible
scaling.
A second major barrier is uncertainty around accountability and liability when AI supports
clinical workflows. Existing clinical, regulatory, and legal frameworks were designed for human
decision-making and have not yet been clearly adapted to AI-supported care. Many AI tools

influence clinical judgment without being regulated as medical devices, and vendor contracts
typically place responsibility entirely on providers. In the absence of clear federal guidance on
how accountability is assigned in these scenarios, organizations that prioritize patient safety
must limit experimentation and narrowly scope AI use cases, regardless of technical readiness.
An additional and increasingly salient barrier to adoption that we’ve observed is concern
among clinicians about the energy intensity and environmental impact of AI
technologies. Clinicians are becoming more aware of the computational and energy demands
associated with certain AI applications, and these concerns can meaningfully limit willingness to
adopt or expand AI use in care delivery settings. In the absence of shared standards,
transparency, or best practices regarding energy-efficient AI deployment, organizations are left
to navigate these tradeoffs independently. HHS could play an important coordinating role in
advancing guidance, shared learning, and innovation around lower-energy AI approaches or
offsetting efficiency gains, helping ensure that responsible AI adoption aligns with broader
sustainability goals in healthcare.
Collectively, these barriers reflect the need for clearer, more consistent federal frameworks that
enable innovation while maintaining accountability, patient safety, environmental responsibility,
and public trust.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes
should HHS prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care
and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited
to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care? Please provide
specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
InStride Response:
HHS should prioritize regulatory and programmatic approaches that promote responsible AI
use in clinical care, where AI supports but does not replace human clinical judgment.
Clear federal guidance that distinguishes decision-support tools from autonomous clinical
decision-making would reduce uncertainty and enable providers to innovate with confidence
while maintaining patient safety.
Regulatory expectations should explicitly establish that AI-generated outputs are advisory,
require human review, and do not shift accountability away from licensed professionals. This
clarity is particularly important for multi-state providers, as consistent federal guidance would
help mitigate fragmented state-level interpretations that currently complicate responsible AI
design and deployment.
From a payment policy perspective, HHS should align incentives with demonstrated
improvements in care quality, safety, and access, rather than narrowly rewarding efficiency
or throughput. In this context, care quality should be defined by clinically meaningful outcomes

and care processes, such as symptom improvement measured through validated clinical scales,
appropriate level-of-care matching, timely access to services, treatment engagement and
retention, continuity of care, and reductions in safety events or avoidable escalation. Many AI
applications that meaningfully reduce clinician burden or improve continuity of care lack clear
reimbursement pathways, limiting investment in higher-value use cases. Payment models that
reward outcomes rather than prescriptive processes would give providers flexibility to adopt AI
tools that support high-quality care while maintaining appropriate human oversight.
Outcome-aligned payment models would encourage innovation that benefits patients while
discouraging premature deployment of unproven or overly expansive tools.
HHS could more effectively accelerate responsible adoption by expanding value-based and
outcomes-oriented reimbursement models that allow providers to determine how best to deliver
care. Such approaches would create durable incentives for providers to evaluate, adopt, and
govern AI tools based on real-world clinical impact, while preserving accountability and public
trust.
3. For non-medical devices, what novel legal and implementation issues
exist and what role should HHS play to help address them?
InStride Response:
AI used in clinical workflows raises novel legal and implementation challenges because it
increasingly influences care without fitting neatly into existing regulatory categories. Many AI
tools are neither regulated medical devices nor purely administrative systems, yet they shape
how clinicians interpret information, prioritize tasks, and frame clinical decisions.
This creates a structural accountability gap. In practice, vendor contracts typically disclaim
clinical liability, while clinicians and provider organizations remain fully responsible for care
decisions. At the same time, existing legal and regulatory frameworks provide limited guidance
on the appropriate scope, safeguards, and expectations for AI-supported workflows. The result
is a system in which responsibility is concentrated on providers, but the rules governing
acceptable AI use remain underdeveloped.
HHS is uniquely positioned to address this gap by clarifying accountability structures without
shifting clinical responsibility away from licensed professionals. This includes reinforcing that
clinicians remain responsible for care decisions, while also establishing clear federal
expectations around permissible AI use, required human oversight, and baseline standards that
AI tools must meet when used in clinical contexts. Such guidance would enable both providers
and innovators to design within well-defined boundaries, reduce uncertainty, and support
responsible innovation while maintaining patient safety and public trust.

4. What are the most promising AI evaluation methods for clinical care, and
should HHS support them?
InStride Response:
Promising AI evaluation methods must extend beyond technical accuracy to include
human-centered and workflow-based measures. In clinical care, safety and effectiveness
depend not only on model performance, but on how AI affects clinician judgment, introduces or
mitigates bias, and interacts with real-world workflows.
Effective evaluation should include:
● Pre-deployment testing in representative clinical settings
● Post-deployment monitoring for drift and unintended consequences
● Assessment of impact on care quality, safety, and patient experience
HHS support for applied evaluation frameworks, through grants, cooperative agreements, or
public-private partnerships, would help standardize responsible AI deployment and accelerate
learning across the healthcare system.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation,
certification, industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote
innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
InStride Response:
HHS can best support innovative and effective AI use by encouraging use-case–specific,
industry-driven validation frameworks rather than one-size-fits-all certification requirements.
AI used in clinical care varies widely in risk and impact, from administrative support to decision
preparation, and validation approaches should reflect that variation.
Rather than focusing exclusively on model-level certification, HHS should support frameworks
that assess:
● intended use and scope,
● required level of human oversight,
● integration into clinical workflows,
● and ongoing monitoring after deployment.
Supporting industry-led validation efforts, informed by providers and grounded in real-world care
delivery, would promote safety and trust without slowing innovation. This approach would also
allow standards to evolve alongside technology, rather than hard-coding requirements that
quickly become outdated.

6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded
performance and cost expectations, and where have they fallen short?
What kinds of novel AI tools would have the greatest potential to improve
health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
InStride Response:
AI has met or exceeded expectations in areas that support clinicians and enhance
decision-making, including administrative efficiency, documentation support, information
synthesis, workflow coordination, and clinical decision support. In certain well-defined use
cases, such as matching patients to appropriate levels of care or identifying clinical fit, AI tools
can perform at or above human benchmarks. When designed appropriately, these applications
reduce cognitive and administrative burden and allow clinicians to operate at the top of their
licenses, spending more time on clinical judgment, patient engagement, and care delivery
rather than clerical tasks.
Challenges arise not from AI’s analytical capability, but from how decision-support outputs
are framed, scoped, and integrated into clinical workflows. AI tools can introduce risk when
outputs are presented without appropriate context, limitations, or uncertainty signals, or when
they are applied beyond the conditions under which they were validated. In complex and
nuanced care scenarios such as mental health, where therapeutic relationships, patient history,
and developmental context are central, overgeneralization or overconfidence in AI outputs can
unintentionally influence clinical judgment.
The most promising AI tools are those that:
● operate within clearly defined, bounded workflows aligned to validated use cases,
● make uncertainty, limitations, and confidence levels explicit, and
● are designed to be reviewed, contextualized, and integrated by clinicians as part of
decision-making, rather than used in isolation.
AI tools built with these principles have the greatest potential to improve outcomes, enhance
care quality, and reduce costs while preserving clinical autonomy, patient safety, and trust.
7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care
organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical
care? What are the primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in
clinical care?
InStride Response:

AI adoption decisions in healthcare are influenced by a combination of clinical leadership,
compliance and risk management, IT and security teams, and executive leadership. Successful
adoption requires alignment across these groups, as each brings a distinct perspective on
patient safety, operational feasibility, regulatory risk, and organizational priorities.
Primary administrative hurdles include:
● uncertainty around liability and accountability when AI supports clinical workflows,
● challenges integrating AI into existing clinical workflows in ways that support clinical
judgment and care delivery without increasing cognitive burden or disrupting patient
care, and
● lack of clear regulatory guidance that leadership teams can rely on when making
adoption decisions across multiple jurisdictions.
Clear federal expectations around permissible scope, required oversight, and accountability for
AI-supported care would materially reduce internal friction. Such guidance would allow
organizations to evaluate AI tools based on clinical and operational value, rather than perceived
regulatory or legal risk, and would support more confident, responsible adoption.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel
research, and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please
consider specific data types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
InStride Response:
Enhanced interoperability would most effectively accelerate AI development when it enables
reliable, well-governed access to clinically meaningful data, rather than simply increasing
data volume. For care delivery organizations, the greatest value lies in interoperable access to
structured clinical data, longitudinal patient histories, outcomes data, and operational signals
that reflect real-world care processes.
Standardization that preserves data provenance, context, and auditability is particularly
important for AI used in clinical workflows. Without clarity on how data were generated,
transformed, and applied, AI outputs can be difficult to evaluate, validate, or trust.
Interoperability efforts that include benchmarking tools and shared evaluation standards would
further support responsible AI development by enabling comparison across populations,
settings, and time.
HHS can play a critical role by advancing interoperability frameworks that support safe,
transparent, and accountable AI use, ensuring that data exchange accelerates innovation while
maintaining patient trust and clinical integrity.

9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to
see addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what
concerns do patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and use
of AI in clinical care?
InStride Response:
Patients and caregivers want AI to address practical, day-to-day challenges in healthcare,
including difficulty accessing care, long wait times, administrative complexity, and fragmented
care experiences. AI that helps clinicians spend more time with patients and less time on
administrative tasks aligns closely with these expectations.
At the same time, patients and caregivers express concerns about privacy, accuracy,
transparency, and the risk that AI could replace human judgment or weaken therapeutic
relationships. These concerns are especially pronounced in mental health care for children and
adolescents, where trust, developmental context, and continuity of care are central to effective
treatment.
Responsible AI adoption must therefore prioritize clear communication about how AI is used,
strong human oversight, and safeguards that reinforce, rather than diminish the human
elements of care that patients and families value most.
10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to
accelerate the adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
a. Are there published findings about the impact of adopted AI tools and their use in
clinical care?
b. How does the literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI as part
of clinical care?
InStride Response:
HHS should prioritize applied, real-world research that examines how AI performs when
embedded in actual care delivery environments. In particular, research is needed on the impact
of AI-supported workflows on clinical decision-making, patient outcomes, clinician workload, and
equity over time.
Mental health and pediatric populations warrant focused attention, as risks, benefits, and ethical
considerations differ meaningfully from adult medical care. Longitudinal studies that assess
safety, bias, and durability of AI performance are essential to building trust and informing
responsible scaling.

While the existing literature typically focuses on technical performance or theoretical cost
savings, greater emphasis is needed on implementation science in terms of how AI tools
change care processes, redistribute work, and affect quality and access in practice.
Public-private partnerships and pragmatic trials embedded in care settings would help translate
innovation into measurable clinical benefit.
