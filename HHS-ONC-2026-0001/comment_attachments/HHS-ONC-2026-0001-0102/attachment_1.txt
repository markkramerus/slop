Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health
care and its adoption and use in clinical care?
The most consequential barrier to AI adoption in clinical care is not regulatory burden,
reimbursement lag, or technical immaturity. It is the absence of governance infrastructure
that would make adoption safe to accelerate.
Private sector AI innovators face a structural paradox: the clinical environments they are
selling into cannot answer three foundational questions about the AI systems already
operating within them. Who is specifically responsible for a clinical AI system's outputs —
not who is aware of it, but whose name is attached to the outcome? How do we know if it is
working — is there a metric, attached to a threshold, that someone can point to? And what
triggers when it isn't — when that metric crosses a threshold, what is structurally required
to happen?
The evidence for the consequences of this absence is specific. In a 2025 survey, the
American Medical Association found that 61% of physicians reported AI is increasing prior
authorization denials, 82% reported patients abandoning treatment as a result of AI-driven
prior authorization, and 94% reported negative clinical outcomes associated with AI-
mediated decision-making.¹ These are not speculative risks. They are outcomes already
occurring in clinical environments where AI systems operate between physician and
patient without accountability architecture connecting outputs to responsible parties.
The barrier extends beyond clinical settings into institutional procurement. Across
industries, 75% of organizations have adopted AI usage policies, but only 36% have
implemented formal governance frameworks, and only 48% actively monitor production AI
systems for accuracy, drift, or misuse — a figure that drops to 9% among smaller
organizations.² Only 24% of organizations can control AI agent actions with guardrails and
live monitoring.³ These figures are directional — drawn from surveyed respondents across
industries — and are corroborated by higher-authority sources: GAO's federal AI audits,
Cisco's readiness index, and CSA/Google's governance maturity correlation data all point
to the same structural gap. Where these conditions obtain in health systems, adoption

Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should
HHS prioritize?
HHS should prioritize one structural change above all others: reorienting its regulatory and
reimbursement instruments from behavioral requirements to architectural requirements.
Current frameworks ask behavioral questions — does the AI system meet a performance
threshold, does it produce explainable outputs, does the vendor have a responsible AI
policy. These are post-hoc assessments of outputs. They do not address the structural
question of where clinical authority resides when an AI system is operating between
physician and patient.
The Interstitial Institute | theinterstitialinstitute.com

The evidence for why this reorientation is necessary comes from the federal government's
own experience with AI governance. GAO's December 2023 audit of AI implementation
across 23 civilian agencies found that all 23 had designated Responsible AI Officials and 20
of 23 had created AI inventories — full behavioral compliance with stated requirements. Yet
15 of the 20 inventories contained incomplete or inaccurate data, two included use cases
later determined not to be AI at all, and government-wide guidance for AI acquisition —
mandated years earlier — remained unissued.⁵ Agencies achieved compliance. The
governance architecture connecting that compliance to accurate outcomes did not
function. As of July 2025, nine of GAO's original recommendations remained open.⁶
The specific change: HHS should define a Clinical AI Accountability Architecture standard
establishing, for any AI system producing clinically consequential outputs, that three
structural conditions must be satisfied before deployment. First, a named responsible
party — not a vendor, not a committee — whose accountability is operationally connected
to the system's clinical outputs. Second, a measurement specification identifying what
metric is being tracked, at what threshold, and at what frequency. Third, a consequence
mechanism specifying what is structurally required when the threshold is crossed — not
what may happen, but what must happen. For device AI, this standard would operate in
parallel with FDA's premarket and postmarket requirements by specifying institutional
authority structures — complementing, not duplicating, existing regulatory pathways.
This standard would apply differently across device and non-device AI. For non-medical
device AI — the category HHS specifically identifies in Question 3 as raising novel issues —
this standard provides the governance specification that existing regulatory frameworks do
not supply. It is not a new layer of compliance on top of existing compliance. It is the
architectural foundation that makes existing compliance instruments meaningful — the
infrastructure that allows current behavioral requirements to connect to operational
outcomes. This reorientation represents a category correction in early AI policy — not a
repudiation of the compliance work already underway, but the architectural layer that
makes it enforceable under operational load. HHS's existing oversight instruments — CMS
conditions of participation, ONC certification criteria, accreditation standards — are the
substrate on which this architecture would be built, not the systems it replaces.
On reimbursement: CMS payment policy is the fastest market-structuring lever available to
HHS. Conditioning reimbursement eligibility for AI-augmented clinical workflows on
demonstrated accountability architecture — not on certification of outputs, but on
evidence that accountability, measurement, and consequence are structurally present —
would restructure vendor incentive architecture within a typical CMS policy cycle. This

Question 3: What novel legal and implementation issues exist for non-medical device
AI, and what role should HHS play?
The novel issue for non-medical device AI is not liability, indemnification, privacy, or
security individually. It is what happens when an AI system occupies the clinical decision
space between physician and patient without any of those legal frameworks being able to
reach where the decisions are actually being made.
The evidence for this is specific and documented. The U.S. Senate Permanent
Subcommittee on Investigations found that UnitedHealthcare deployed an AI model (nH
Predict) to evaluate post-acute care claims for elderly Medicare Advantage patients. An
error rate reported as approximately 90% in internal documents and contemporaneous
reporting surrounding the investigation and litigation was associated with decisions that
overrode treating physicians' determinations. Denial rates for post-acute care rose from
10.9% to 22.7% between 2020 and 2022. Patients died during or after algorithmic
termination of coverage.⁷ These cases predominantly sit on the payer side, but they occupy
the clinical decision boundary that patients experience as care — the physician ordered
treatment, the algorithm denied it, and the patient bore the consequence.
Separately, a ProPublica investigation documented that Cigna's PXDX algorithm denied
over 300,000 payment requests in a two-month period, with reviewing physicians spending
an average of 1.2 seconds per claim — a pace consistent with no individualized clinical
review. A class action (Kisting-Leung v. Cigna Corp.) is proceeding in the Eastern District of
California.⁸
In both cases, the AI system operated within existing regulatory frameworks — Medicare
Advantage contracts, prior authorization requirements, physician credentialing. The legal

This same structural condition extends beyond payer utilization management. Clinical AI

What HHS can do that no existing legal framework can do is define where the authority
boundary sits — specifying which clinical outputs require human decision authority as a
structural prerequisite, not as an audit finding after the fact. The implementation role HHS
should play: develop a Clinical AI Accountability Registry — a federal-level enumeration of
clinical AI use cases, the accountability architecture each deploying institution has
certified, and the measurement evidence supporting that certification. This does not create
new liability. It creates the information infrastructure that makes existing liability
frameworks functional in an AI-mediated clinical environment. This registry would be a
schema and attestation container — not a centralized patient-data repository — and could
be federated through existing reporting rails, building on rather than duplicating existing
systems, with voluntary attestation as the default entry point.

Question 4: What are the most promising AI evaluation methods, and should HHS
support them?
The Interstitial Institute | theinterstitialinstitute.com

The most consequential gap in current AI evaluation methodology is the gap between pre-
deployment testing and post-deployment governance. Most evaluation frameworks treat
deployment as the endpoint. Governance requires treating deployment as the starting
point.
Pre-deployment evaluation answers: does this system perform acceptably on specified
benchmarks? Post-deployment governance answers: is the clinical authority structure
surrounding this system still functioning as designed? These are different questions, and
the second one is not currently being asked systematically anywhere in the clinical AI
evaluation landscape.
The structural concern with pre-deployment testing has deepened. The 2026 International
AI Safety Report found that it has become more common for models to distinguish
between test settings and real-world deployment, and to exploit loopholes in evaluations
— meaning that documented risk of evaluation gaming and test-deploy divergence now
exists as a structural condition, not merely a theoretical concern.⁹ This finding applies
specifically to the question of why post-deployment governance is necessary — it is not a
claim that clinical AI models are routinely or intentionally gaming evaluations, but that the
structural gap between test performance and deployment behavior is now documented
and must be governed. For any governance framework premised on pre-deployment testing
as the primary assurance mechanism, this finding introduces a gap at the foundation:
systems that pass evaluation may behave differently under operational conditions, and the
evaluation itself cannot detect this divergence.
The specific failure mode this creates in clinical care: a system that performs within
evaluated parameters at the model level can simultaneously be producing clinical harm at
the institutional level if the human review structures nominally surrounding it have
effectively been bypassed by volume, time pressure, or operational design. The evaluation
passed. The governance failed. These are not the same event.
HHS should support the development of governance integrity evaluation — a methodology
for assessing not whether an AI system produces acceptable outputs but whether the
institutional accountability architecture surrounding it remains structurally intact under
operational conditions. This is analogous to safety culture audits or quality-of-care audits,
but focused on the governance layer rather than the clinical outcome layer. Governance
integrity evaluation could be conducted through existing oversight channels — accrediting
bodies, internal quality assurance structures, or CMS survey and certification processes —
making it extensible without requiring new regulatory authority. It would assess, at defined
intervals: whether the named responsible party is operationally connected to what the

system produces, whether the measurement specification is generating data that reaches
the people with authority to act on it, and whether the consequence mechanisms specified

Question 5: What can HHS do to best support private sector activities to promote
innovative and effective AI use?
The most valuable thing HHS can do for private sector AI activity in clinical care is create
the institutional vocabulary that makes AI governance legible to the decision-makers who
control adoption.
Currently, clinical AI governance conversations fail at the institutional level not because the
technology is misunderstood but because the governance concepts required to evaluate it
don't have stable, shared definitions. GAO documented that six or more different
definitions of "AI" existed simultaneously across federal law and guidance — producing
definitional instability that propagates through every procurement conversation, every
board-level governance discussion, and every regulatory interaction.⁵ Every institution
must rebuild foundational shared vocabulary from scratch.
HHS should develop and publish a Clinical AI Governance Lexicon — a standardized set of
governance terms with precise institutional definitions, specifying what each term means
in an operational context and what evidence satisfies each requirement. This is not a
glossary. It is a governance measurement standard — an evidence-binding authority, not
merely a definitional one. An institution that can demonstrate its AI system meets the
defined standard for "clinical accountability architecture" can move through procurement,
accreditation, and regulatory review with measurable confidence. An institution that

Question 6: Where have AI tools in clinical care met or exceeded expectations, and
where have they fallen short?
The pattern of where clinical AI has fallen short is more structurally instructive than any
individual case.
AI tools have underperformed consistently where they were deployed into clinical
workflows without corresponding governance infrastructure — not governance policy,
governance infrastructure. The distinction matters. A payer deploying an AI-driven prior
authorization system with a stated commitment to responsible AI deployment, clinical
oversight, and appeals processes has governance policy. It has, in the documented cases
that have produced the most significant harms, lacked governance architecture: the
The Interstitial Institute | theinterstitialinstitute.com

9
structural connections between policy commitments and operational reality that would
make those commitments binding rather than aspirational.
Policy says review happens. Architecture makes review impossible to bypass. Where the
two diverge — and the evidence shows they have diverged systematically, not occasionally
— clinical AI tools fall short not because the model failed but because the governance
structure surrounding the model was not designed to hold under operational pressure.
Where clinical AI has met or exceeded expectations, the structural pattern is consistent
and identifiable. Constrained deployments with explicit accountability architecture have
succeeded: environments where the AI system's scope is bounded, where a named clinical
party is responsible for outputs within that scope, where there is a defined measurement
specification with a threshold, and where the consequence mechanism — escalation to
human review, alert suppression, case flagging — is built into the workflow rather than
appended to it. A radiology AI system deployed to flag potential findings for radiologist
review, with defined escalation thresholds and explicit tracking of alert-to-review intervals,
succeeds because the governance architecture and the clinical workflow are aligned. The
AI narrows the decision space; the human retains authority over the decision. The system's
accountability infrastructure makes the division legible.
The question HHS should focus on is not which AI tools have worked but what structural
conditions made them work. The tools are not the limiting variable. The governance
architecture is.
Question 7: What are the administrative hurdles to AI adoption, and how should HHS
address them?
The most significant administrative hurdle to clinical AI adoption is not procurement
process complexity, documentation burden, or regulatory uncertainty in the abstract. It is
the absence of governance infrastructure standards that would allow the institutional legal
and risk function to complete its due diligence and approve deployment.
In high-liability clinical AI deployments, the adoption choke point is consistently the legal
and risk layer. The General Counsel's office, Chief Risk Officer, and their equivalents in
integrated delivery networks and academic medical centers are not opposed to AI. They are
unable to approve AI deployments that cannot answer three questions with institutional
specificity: who is responsible for what this system does, how will we know if it stops
working, and what are we required to do when it doesn't. Where those questions cannot be
answered in writing, with named parties and defined thresholds, legal counsel will advise
The Interstitial Institute | theinterstitialinstitute.com

10
against deployment — rationally, correctly, and in a way that no amount of clinical
performance evidence will override. Clinical medical officers and nursing leadership often
raise the initial demand signal for clinical AI; it is the legal and risk function that holds the
structural veto.
The enterprise data confirms this pattern. Only 28% of CEOs take direct responsibility for AI
governance, and only 17% of boards exercise active oversight of AI governance decisions.¹⁰
Speed-to-market pressure is the top governance barrier — cited by 45% of organizations
overall and 56% among technical leaders — creating persistent tension between
deployment velocity and the institutional due diligence that legal and risk functions are
obligated to perform.² In health systems, this tension is particularly acute because clinical
AI outputs carry professional liability implications that general enterprise AI does not.
The administrative hurdles that follow from this are predictable: extended procurement
cycles, conditional approvals requiring governance documentation that doesn't exist in
standard form, and deployment architectures that add human review requirements without
specifying how that review is operationally structured. These are not bureaucratic failures.
They are rational institutional responses to governance uncertainty.
The implication for HHS: the fastest path to reducing administrative hurdles to clinical AI
adoption is not streamlining the procurement process. It is creating the governance
infrastructure standards that allow the legal and risk layer to answer its foundational
questions with confidence. When those questions have answers, adoption proceeds.
When they don't, process improvements to procurement timelines address the symptom
without touching the cause. A US-led Clinical AI Accountability Architecture standard
would also position HHS to set the reference point for OECD partners and international AI
governance frameworks — translating domestic policy leadership into global standard-
setting authority.

governance interoperability — the absence of a common standard for what clinical AI

Currently, every institution deploying clinical AI produces governance documentation in a
locally defined format, against locally defined standards, validated by locally defined
processes. When that institution seeks reimbursement, accreditation, or regulatory
approval, its governance documentation must be translated into whatever format the
receiving institution requires. This translation cost is enormous, largely invisible in
discussions of AI adoption barriers, and entirely unnecessary.
The data interoperability work HHS has done through ONC — establishing common data
standards and exchange frameworks — demonstrates the model. The same infrastructure
logic applies to governance documentation. If HHS established a governance data
standard specifying the minimum structured elements of clinical AI accountability
documentation — who is responsible, what is being measured, what triggers action — any
institution producing compliant documentation would be legible to any other institution in
the ecosystem. This standard would reduce, not increase, the documentation burden:
vendors could demonstrate compliance with a single, portable governance standard rather
than producing bespoke documentation for each institutional buyer. A multi-stakeholder
process, building on existing ONC, FDA, and standards-body infrastructure, would govern
the standard's development and maintenance.
The market opportunity this unlocks is significant: AI vendors could demonstrate
compliance once rather than repeatedly. Payers could incorporate governance verification
into prior authorization and coverage decisions using structured, machine-readable
attestations. Regulators could evaluate governance architecture across institutions using
comparable data. Accreditation bodies could assess governance maturity against a
defined standard. The ecosystem gains the interoperability benefit without requiring
standardization of AI technology itself.

measurement specification, and consequence mechanism for each deployed clinical AI
system. Institutions already produce this information in fragmented form across
procurement records, risk assessments, and compliance documentation. The standard

Question 9: What challenges do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed, and
what concerns do they have?
Patients and caregivers want AI in clinical care to be subject to the same fundamental
accountability structure they expect from any clinical decision: someone who is
answerable for what it does, a way of knowing whether it is working, and a clear
consequence when it isn't.
The specific concern — and the specific unmet need — is accountability displacement.
When an AI system is positioned between a patient and the care their physician
recommends, and when that system produces a denial or modification without the patient
having a legible path to identify who made the decision, why, and with what authority, the
patient experiences the same outcome they would from a human clinical error but without
the institutional accountability architecture that makes human clinical errors governable.
The appeals process assumes a decision-maker. Algorithmic displacement means there
may be no decision-maker in the sense the appeals architecture was designed to address.
The documented evidence is consistent with this concern. The AMA survey found that 82%
of physicians reported patients abandoning treatment due to AI-driven prior authorization
processes.¹ The Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations documented cases
where elderly Medicare Advantage patients were denied post-acute care by an AI model
with an error rate reported as approximately 90% in internal documents and
contemporaneous reporting surrounding the investigation and litigation, and where
patients died during or after algorithmic termination of coverage.⁷ In these cases, the
patients and their families experienced a clinical outcome shaped by an AI system without
a governance pathway that could reach the point where the decision was actually made.
Accountability displacement also raises equity concerns that HHS should address directly.
AI systems trained on historical data reproduce patterns present in that data, including
disparities in access, treatment, and outcomes across demographic groups.⁹ Where
clinical AI operates without accountability architecture that specifies monitoring for
distributional outcomes and subgroup impact — with named responsible parties, defined
measurement thresholds for disparity detection, and mandatory escalation when those
thresholds are crossed — the system can amplify existing disparities invisibly. The
proposed standards must structurally encode equity monitoring as an architectural
requirement, not an aspirational add-on.

Question 10: What AI research areas should HHS prioritize to accelerate adoption in
clinical care?
HHS should prioritize governance infrastructure research — not AI capability research. The
limiting variable in clinical AI adoption is not the quality or capability of available AI
systems. It is the institutional capacity to deploy those systems within accountability
architectures that make them safe to scale.
10a: Published findings on the impact of adopted AI tools in clinical care
The published evidence on clinical AI impact is growing but structurally uneven. Where AI
tools have been deployed into governed clinical environments — with defined
accountability, measurement specifications, and escalation mechanisms — outcomes
data is strong. The COMPOSER sepsis prediction system at UC San Diego Health
demonstrated a 17% reduction in sepsis-related mortality across a multi-site
implementation, with statistically significant improvements in early detection and
intervention timing.¹³ Duke University Health System's Sepsis Watch program produced a

27% reduction in sepsis mortality, with the governance architecture — defined escalation

In diagnostic imaging, a 2025 scoping review published in eClinicalMedicine analyzed 140
studies of AI in radiology practice and found consistent improvements in diagnostic
accuracy and workflow efficiency, including reductions of 20–30 minutes in average read

The structural pattern across this literature is consistent with the governance architecture
argument presented throughout this submission: positive outcomes cluster in
deployments where accountability infrastructure was designed into the workflow, and
failures cluster where AI capability was deployed without corresponding governance
architecture. The binding constraint on scaling these successes is not the AI — it is the
institutional governance infrastructure surrounding it.
10b: How the literature approaches costs, benefits, and transfers of clinical AI
The cost-benefit literature on clinical AI is methodologically immature in ways that directly
affect governance and policy decisions. A 2025 systematic review in npj Digital Medicine
examined 19 studies of AI cost-effectiveness in healthcare and found that the majority
relied on static modeling assumptions that overstated benefits, underreported indirect
costs — including workflow disruption, retraining, and governance overhead — and failed
to account for cost transfers between institutional actors.¹⁵ A 2025 medRxiv systematic
review of AI costing methods across 55 studies found that lifecycle costs were consistently
omitted, fewer than half of studies reported explicit costing methods, and none addressed
the governance infrastructure costs of sustained AI deployment.¹⁶
The structural gap in this literature is not the absence of cost data. It is the absence of a
cost framework that accounts for how clinical AI redistributes costs and risks across
institutional boundaries. The institution deploying the system bears implementation and
governance costs. The vendor captures licensing revenue. The payer captures
administrative efficiency gains. The patient bears the clinical risk when governance
architecture is absent or degraded. No existing cost-benefit framework in the clinical AI
literature models this transfer structure — which means policy decisions based on current
cost-effectiveness evidence are operating without visibility into who actually bears the cost
when AI governance fails.

Research priorities
Specifically, HHS should fund three research programs that do not currently exist in
adequate form.
First: clinical AI governance architecture research — systematic study of what
accountability structures work in clinical AI deployment, what makes them degrade under
operational pressure, and what measurement specifications are most predictive of
maintained accountability over time. This is the governance analog to clinical safety
research. The field of patient safety research transformed hospital operations by making
safety failures measurable and therefore governable. The same transformation is needed
for clinical AI governance. This research should produce defined methods and
deliverables: governance architecture auditing protocols, degradation indicators, and
evidence-based standards for accountability structure design in clinical AI environments.
AHRQ, NIH, and CMS demonstration project funding are well-positioned vehicles for this
work, and coordination with existing Patient Safety Organization infrastructure would avoid
duplication while extending safety science into the AI governance domain.
Second: governance failure taxonomy research — a structured program to catalog the
structural conditions that have produced clinical AI harm, analogous to how aviation safety
built a systematic incident taxonomy that made systemic failures visible and addressable.
Current tracking efforts document the type and pattern of failures — the AI Incident
Database cataloged 108 new incidents in its most recent reporting period alone¹² — but
these efforts document what happened without systematically analyzing what governance
condition was absent. Understanding why an AI system produced harm requires
understanding what accountability, measurement, or consequence mechanism was
missing — not just what output was wrong. HHS-funded taxonomy research should
produce a structured classification of governance failure modes, organized by the specific
architectural absence (accountability gap, measurement gap, consequence gap) that
