ONC ASTP Feb 2026 RFI Initial Response 2/12/26
1. Overview
UpDoc appreciates the opportunity to respond to the HHS Office of the Deputy Secretary and
ASTP Request for Information on accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence in
clinical care.
UpDoc is a software medical device developer that produces physician-supervised clinical AI
workflow solutions designed to extend care capacity while preserving physician accountability.
Our platform enables AI-facilitated clinical interventions that operate exclusively within
preauthorized, physician-defined care pathways, and under the direction of a prescribing
physician. All actions are digitally documented and auditable. Consequently, the suggestions
that are proposed in this RFI Response only apply to AI systems that facilitate interventions
under the oversight and prescription of a physician.
We believe the principal barriers to responsible AI adoption are misalignment between existing
reimbursement structures and workflow-integrated AI healthcare delivery. Current time-based
fee-for-service billing models do not include productivity-enhancing tools. While CMS has
accommodated coverage and payment for certain Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) medical
equipment under Medicare Part B physician services, it has only done so in a few instances and
by forced cross-walks that are largely inapplicable. The lack of predictable coverage and
payment for productivity-enhancing medical device software may fragment care delivery and
weaken physician adoption.
We respectfully offer three guiding principles for federal policy consideration:
1. Payments should increasingly reimburse physicians for both care outputs and
validated workflows, not only for software licenses or time.
2. AI-enhanced clinical care must remain physician-anchored, with defined guardrails
that preserve accountability and safety.
3. Digitized workflows enable both scalability and oversight, providing auditability, fraud
mitigation, and a pathway toward outcome-based reimbursement models.
Our responses below address the specific questions posed in the RFI.
Please note: This response is NOT intended to provide feedback for facilitation of AI systems
that deliver care independently of physician oversight or prescription authority that may
involve other legal state authorities, governance structures, and other payment regimes.

2. Responses to Specific Questions
Response to Question 1: What barriers impede adoption of AI in clinical care?
Direct Answer:
The primary barriers to adoption are structural reimbursement misalignment within existing
CMS payment methodologies, such as time-based billing constructs that do not account for
improvements in efficiency. There presently does not exist accountability frameworks for
AI-enabled clinical actions.
Explanation:
Under the Physician Fee Schedule methodology, medical device software expenses are generally
treated as indirect, non-allocable practice expenses rather than direct medical equipment
inputs. This categorization creates structural reimbursement barriers for workflow-integrated AI
medical device software technologies which are not generic off-the-shelf IT.
When workflow-integrated AI functions as core clinical infrastructure, classification as indirect
practice expense fails to reflect its role in delivering physician-directed care.
In addition, many asynchronous care services and codes (e.g., Digital E/M, Care Management
Services such as CCM, RPM, etc.) require documentation of human time spent supervising care.
When AI systems increase productivity and reduce required clinician time, physicians may
paradoxically experience revenue reduction, creating a financial disincentive to adoption.
Organizations face uncertainty as AI-enabled workflows are operationalized. Clear federal
guidance reinforcing coverage and payment of software as a medical device-enabled workflows
would materially reduce uncertainty and accelerate responsible adoption.
Response to Question 2: What payment policy changes would accelerate
adoption?
Direct Answer:
CMS should establish clear, non-time-based reimbursement pathways for asynchronous care,
much like it has begun to do by creating Advanced Primary Care Management (APCM) services.
CMS created APCM service codes under the existing FFS methodology to support primary care
providers in delivering comprehensive, continuous, and coordinated care while omitting
time-based requirements to facilitate a shift towards value-based care. Similarly, CMS should
explore creating additional services (i.e. HCPCS codes) to recognize and reimburse

physician-supervised, AI-enhanced clinical actions that reflect Medical Decision Making (MDM)
complexity. In parallel, CMS should broaden and strengthen reimbursement pathways for
asynchronous care to align payment with the evolving clinical realities of AI-enabled care as it

Explanation:
Near-term reforms could include:
● Explicit recognition that AI-facilitated clinical interventions under physician supervision

● Creation of new services or HCPCS G-codes for asynchronous E/M services that leverage

● Clear CMS guidance that validated workflow substitution by AI may serve as a lower-cost

These clarifications could be implemented within existing statutory authority and CPT

care capacity without penalizing physicians economically.
Response to Question 3: Legal and governance considerations
Direct Answer:
AI-enabled clinical care should operate within human physician-anchored accountability
frameworks that preserve prescribing authority, enforce deterministic care boundaries, and
ensure full auditability of interventions.
As AI capabilities evolve, governance frameworks should scale proportionately, ensuring that
systems operating with higher degrees of autonomy are accompanied by commensurate
human-in-the-loop safeguards, transparency, and accountability.
Explanation:
AI systems delivering clinical interventions under physician oversight and payment structures
should:
● Operate exclusively within preauthorized, physician-defined care pathways.
● Block actions outside approved clinical parameters.
● Maintain comprehensive digital logs of all patient interactions and interventions.

● Reinforce that ultimate clinical responsibility remains with the supervising physician, and
that AI systems should not independently initiate or modify treatment plans outside

The above applies only to AI systems that facilitate interventions under the oversight and
prescription of a physician. For these systems, payment models that operate independently of

Response to Question 4: Evaluation Methods
Direct Answer:
AI-enabled clinical care should be evaluated at the clinical workflow level, with emphasis on
real-world evidence, pathway adherence, substitution effects, safety monitoring, and
auditability rather than solely on isolated algorithmic performance metrics.
Explanation:
Evaluation frameworks should assess:
● Clinical outcomes achieved through AI-facilitated workflow substitution.
● Adherence to defined, physician-approved care pathways.
● Safety, utilization, and outcome metrics in real-world settings.
● Reduction in higher-cost synchronous encounters where clinically appropriate.
● Embedded digital audit capabilities supporting post-market monitoring.
Evaluation should also assess whether AI-enabled workflows substitute for higher-cost
encounters without compromising clinical quality and should recognize that AI systems
deployed within bounded clinical workflows differ materially from standalone algorithmic tools.
Response to Question 5: Accreditation or Certification Pathways
Direct Answer:
Accreditation frameworks should distinguish between AI systems that facilitate interventions
under the oversight and prescription of a physician, and AI systems that deliver care
independently of physician oversight or prescription authority.

For AI systems that facilitate interventions under the oversight and prescription of a physician,
physician-supervised AI workflows should be evaluated for transparency, guardrails, and
accountability in addition to algorithmic validation. There should be proportional oversight
standards to systems operating with higher degrees of independent clinical authority.
Explanation:
Accreditation should emphasize system-level safeguards, not only algorithm validation in
isolation.
Certification standards may include:
● Clear physician oversight and accountability structures.
● Configuration controls that prevent out-of-pathway actions.
● Inspectable audit logs documenting all AI-facilitated interventions.
● Workflow-level transparency regarding how clinical actions are initiated and validated.
Such standards would reinforce trust while allowing innovation to scale responsibly.
Response to Question 6: Where AI Has Succeeded or Fallen Short
Direct Answer:
AI has shown success in structured, protocol-driven care environments but has faced challenges
in fragmented spaces that operate outside physician-led care coordination.
Explanation:
Earlier generations of standalone Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) tools demonstrated
strong algorithmic performance but often struggled to achieve durable adoption when not
embedded within physician-led workflows.
Success is most durable when AI augments physician capacity rather than bypassing established
care relationships.
AI performs well in medication titration, chronic disease management, and other longitudinal,
protocol-driven care management settings where clinical boundaries are clearly defined by
physicians who give authorization for AI to join care teams under their pre-set conditions.
Adoption is more limited when reimbursement structures separate AI-enabled services from
established care teams or create economic disincentives for physician participation. Such

Response to Question 7: Organizational Adoption Barriers
Direct Answer:
Organizational barriers include revenue cannibalization risk under time-based billing,
uncertainty regarding liability allocation and compliance expectations, integration complexity
within existing care infrastructure, and change management challenges within clinical teams.
Explanation:
Health systems are reluctant to adopt technologies that may reduce billable time without
corresponding payment recognition. Clear MDM-based reimbursement pathways would reduce
financial uncertainty.
In addition, ambiguity regarding physician accountability and compliance expectations can slow
internal approval processes. Clear federal coding / reimbursement action and guidance
reinforcing physician-supervised AI models would mitigate this hesitation.
Finally, AI adoption requires integration into existing EHR systems, care team workflows, and
operational governance structures. Interoperability alignment and implementation clarity are
essential to reduce integration burden.
Published analyses have demonstrated meaningful reductions in synchronous visit utilization
when AI-enabled workflows are properly integrated into physician-supervised care models.
Response to Question 8: Interoperability
Direct Answer:
AI-enabled workflows require structured, interoperable data aligned with ONC-adopted
interoperability standards to ensure safe, scalable integration into clinical systems.
Explanation:
Standardized data exchange is essential for ensuring AI interventions are context-aware,
clinically appropriate, and safely integrated into existing care workflows.

Data liquidity enables substitution of encounters, real-time documentation, and quality
monitoring. Alignment with federal interoperability standards reduces integration friction and

Interoperability policy should prioritize bidirectional data exchange that supports both clinical
action and transparent documentation of AI-facilitated care.
Response to Question 9: Patient and Caregiver Concerns
Direct Answer:
Patient trust depends on clear disclosure of AI involvement. For AI systems that facilitate
interventions under the oversight and prescription of a physician, there should be transparent
physician oversight, clinically bounded pathways that prevent overuse, and assurance that AI
tools operate within defined safety constraints.
Explanation:
For AI systems that facilitate interventions under the oversight and prescription of a physician,
transparent communication regarding the physician’s continued oversight of AI is central to
maintaining patient trust.
For these systems, digitized workflows enhance safety monitoring and preserve accountability
by ensuring all AI-facilitated interventions are documented and reviewable.
Clinically bounded, defined guardrails mitigate concerns about inappropriate utilization or
overreach.
Clear policies and regulatory structures detailing physician supervision vs independent
autonomy can reassure patients that AI tools are being regulated and used responsibly.
Response to Question 10: Research Priorities
Direct Answer:
HHS should prioritize research evaluating AI-enabled clinical workflow substitution models,
measurable cost and utilization impacts from asynchronous care, and transition pathways
toward outcome-based reimbursement frameworks.
Explanation:

Research should measure both cost substitution effects and patient outcome metrics under
controlled guardrails.
Pilot programs, including those conducted through the CMS Innovation Center, could evaluate

3. Conclusion
UpDoc appreciates HHS’s leadership in examining how AI can responsibly expand clinical
capacity. Clear alignment between reimbursement, governance, interoperability, and physician
accountability will be essential to accelerating safe and trusted adoption of physician-supervised
AI in clinical care.
