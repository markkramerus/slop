PUBLIC COMMENT
RE: RIN 0955–AA13, Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of
Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care (FR Doc. 2025–23641)
Submitted via: Regulations.gov
I am a senior leader responsible for digital health and inclusion-focused innovation at a
safety-net academic medical center in Brooklyn. I am developing an institutional
framework for responsible AI governance and adoption, informed by direct
implementation experience deploying patient-facing AI tools in a safety-net setting. I
submit these comments in my individual professional capacity. I am responding to RFI
Question 2 (policy priorities), Question 9 (patient and caregiver needs), and Question
10 (research priorities).
This comment focuses on patient- and caregiver-facing clinical AI: portal chatbots, voice
agents, automated intake workflows, and AI-enabled outreach used directly by patients
and caregivers. These tools can improve access and efficiency, but those benefits will
not reach everyone unless tools are built for real-world conditions—limited English
proficiency, low health literacy, disability access needs, and uneven digital access. In
safety-net settings, the most helpful federal actions are practical requirements that
protect patients without imposing unrealistic burden.
Existing civil rights and accessibility obligations apply to AI-enabled care
HHS should make clear that AI-enabled patient communication does not reduce or
replace existing legal responsibilities. Title VI and Section 1557, the ADA and Section
508, and the Plain Writing Act apply to AI-enabled communication pathways just as they
apply to in-person, written, and telephonic communication. HHS can accelerate
adoption by translating these obligations into baseline requirements that are clear for
vendors and feasible for under-resourced providers.
Four baseline requirements for patient-facing AI (RFI Q2 and Q9)
1. Transparency
Patients should be told at the start of an interaction that AI is involved, what it can and
cannot do, and how to reach a person at any time. Recommended baseline: plain-
language disclosure at the start of the interaction, plus a persistent “get help from a
person” option.
2. Language access
In safety-net settings, language access is a basic safety requirement. If disclosures and
instructions are only available in English, patients with limited English proficiency are
shut out of safe use. Recommended baseline: support English and the top 2–3
languages by patient volume at the site, with an easy language switch and a clear
pathway to human help in all supported languages.
3. Plain language

Transparency only works if patients understand what they are being told. Patient-facing
AI should be written at or below a 6th-grade reading level, consistent with the Plain
Writing Act, and should prioritize short, single-task instructions. Recommended
baseline: plain-language scripts, one action per step, and brief comprehension supports
for critical actions (for example, “Would you like me to repeat that?”).
4. Human escalation
AI tools should not become a gatekeeper to care. If a patient requests help or an issue
remains unresolved after one clarification attempt, the tool should immediately route the
patient to appropriate staff—without additional automated menus. Recommended
baseline: immediate escalation on request, with appropriate routing (nurse, pharmacist,
scheduling), and clear emergency guidance when clinically appropriate.
A brief example: a portal chatbot provides accurate information but only in English, with
high reading complexity and no confirmation of understanding. A caregiver
misunderstands next steps, leading to delayed care. This is preventable through
multilingual support, plain language, comprehension prompts, and reliable escalation.
Pre-deployment readiness and ongoing monitoring (RFI Q2)
Before go-live, organizations should define readiness criteria in advance, stress-test
predictable failure modes through structured scenario-based simulation (covering
language, literacy, and device barriers), and verify performance against explicit Go/No-
Go thresholds. Informal usability checks are not sufficient.
Once in use, providers and vendors should monitor completion, fallback, and escalation
rates—disaggregated by language group—as early warning indicators of uneven
performance. HHS should also encourage organizations to integrate AI-related risks into
existing patient safety monitoring systems, classifying AI workflow incidents as high-
impact when they could affect clinical decisions, documentation accuracy, or care
access.
Recommended baseline: if fallback or escalation rates exceed 20% for any language
group for two consecutive months, require corrective action plans and pause high-risk
pathways until issues are resolved.
Research and development priorities (RFI Q10)
Federal grants and contracts should prioritize tools developed with and validated in
diverse populations—Medicaid beneficiaries, people with disabilities, limited English
proficiency populations, and safety-net settings. Too often, tools are built and validated
in well-resourced systems and then deployed elsewhere with weaker performance and
new access barriers.
HHS should also support scenario-based simulated caregiver persona testing as an
accepted preliminary validation step in proposals for patient-facing AI tools. This pre-
deployment method identifies language, literacy, accessibility, and device-specific
failures before patients are exposed—reducing compliance risk, lowering remediation
costs, and preventing avoidable care delays. Alongside this, HHS should fund

implementation research on adoption in resource-constrained settings and support
shared infrastructure that under-resourced providers can use without prohibitive
licensing costs.
Closing
I urge HHS to establish four baseline requirements— transparency, language access,
plain language, and reliable escalation—via guidance regulation, or contract language.
Pairing these baselines with practical pre-deployment readiness criteria, simple ongoing
monitoring, and targeted research investment will protect patients, reduce avoidable
burden, and improve access and outcomes across diverse communities.
