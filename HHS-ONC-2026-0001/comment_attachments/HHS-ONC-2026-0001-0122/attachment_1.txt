The regulatory frameworks surrounding the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in clinical care is
characterized by significant uncertainty. The absence of clear, tailored guidelines for AI
technologies generates hesitation among both developers and healthcare providers. This
uncertainty not only fosters doubt but also contributes to a slowdown in the innovation
process and the deployment of AI solutions within clinical environments.
Current regulatory requirements demand rigorous pre-market approval for AI tools, which
can be lengthy and costly. This poses a substantial barrier to entry, particularly for startups
and smaller companies that may lack the resources to navigate complex approval
processes. As a result, promising innovations that could enhance patient care and
operational efficiency are delayed or entirely sidelined.
Compliance with regulations such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability
Act (HIPAA) complicates the data-sharing processes that are essential for training AI
algorithms. The requirements for patient data privacy make it challenging to collect and
utilize the extensive datasets needed to develop effective AI tools.
Uncertainty surrounding liability when AI systems provide clinical recommendations can
deter healthcare providers from adopting AI technologies. Concerns over accountability
can lead to reluctance in integrating AI into clinical practices, providers fear potential legal
repercussions stemming from AI-driven decisions.
Current reimbursement models further complicate the adoption of AI tools. These models
often prioritize traditional, established procedures and technologies, creating a financial
deterrent for providers to implement AI solutions that may offer better outcomes but lack
established reimbursement pathways.
While AI tools can be integrated into existing electronic health records (EHRs) and
workflows, regulatory frameworks that do not promote interoperability can severely restrict

their functionality and usability. Without the necessary support for integration, the potential
benefits of AI in improving clinical efficiency and patient care may be lost.
Current funding constraints on AI research slow down and delay innovation. Regulatory
policies must adapt to support R&D and encourage stronger collaboration between public
and private sectors.
If clinical validation is mandated for AI tools, this requirement can pose additional barriers
due to resource intensive and time consuming nature of the validation process. Such
challenges can deter developers from pursuing AI innovations.
Existing federal regulations can act as both facilitators and barriers to the adoption of AI in
clinical settings. While regulations are designed to ensure patient safety and protect
sensitive health information, the regulations must adapt to accept and support the
integration of AI technologies into healthcare.
Specific Questions
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
One of the primary challenges is regulatory uncertainty. The absence of clear and
consistent guidelines specifically tailored to AI technologies creates an atmosphere of
hesitation among developers and investors. This uncertainty will slow down the pace of
innovation and the deployment of AI solutions limiting the benefits that AI technologies can
offer.
Current regulations impose complex approval processes for AI tools. These processes are
lengthy and costly, which deters startups and smaller companies from entering the market.
As a result, the diversity and volume of innovative solutions available to healthcare
providers is significantly reduced and delays the overall progress in the field.
Concerns regarding data privacy and security further complicate the landscape. For
example, compliance with HIPAA imposes strict requirements on how patient data is
handled. These regulations can make data sharing challenging, as data is essential for
training AI algorithms and developing AI tools.
Integration challenges also pose a barrier to the effective use of AI in clinical settings.
Integrating AI solutions into existing healthcare systems and workflows can be difficult,
particularly when there is a lack of interoperability between AI tools and electronic health

records (EHRs). Without seamless integration, the usability and effectiveness of AI tools
may be restricted.
Reimbursement models further complicate the adoption of AI in healthcare. Current
reimbursement structures often favor established procedures and technologies over
innovative AI solutions. The slow transition to value-based care models can limit the
financial incentives for providers to incorporate AI tools into their practices.
Liability and accountability issues present another significant hurdle. The uncertainty
surrounding who is liable for AI driven clinical decisions can deter healthcare providers
from adopting these technologies. If providers fear being held accountable for decisions
influenced by AI they may be reluctant to implement AI tools in their practice.
Cultural resistance to change within healthcare organizations can exacerbate these
challenges. There may be an inherent reluctance to adopt new technologies, particularly
AI. Concerns about the reliability of AI systems, potential job displacement and the need
for staff training on new tools can create barriers to embracing innovation.
The lack of standardization in metrics and evaluation frameworks for AI tools can
complicate the assessment of their effectiveness. This absence of uniformity makes it
challenging for healthcare providers to determine which AI solutions are truly beneficial to
their practices.
By recognizing, understanding and addressing these barriers, stakeholders in healthcare
can facilitate the innovation and adoption of AI technologies improving overall healthcare
outcomes.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS
regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to
develop or use AI in clinical care? Please provide specific changes and applicable
Code of Federal Regulations citations.
One critical area is the premarket approval process for AI tools. HHS should revise this
process to allow for more flexible and expedited pathways, particularly for low-risk AI
applications. This change could involve modifying the requirements established in 21 CFR
Part 807, which governs premarket notification (510(k)) submissions, to create a tiered
regulatory framework that differentiates AI tools based on their risk levels.
HHS should issue clear guidelines specifically tailored to AI technologies. These guidance
documents should outline the necessary steps for compliance, including data

requirements and validation processes. This initiative could leverage the Quality System
Regulation found in 21 CFR Part 820, adapting it to include specific guidelines for AI tool
development and deployment.
Promoting interoperability standards is essential for the effective integration of AI tools with
existing electronic health record (EHR) systems. HHS could prioritize the development and
enforcement of these standards, drawing on 45 CFR Part 170, which establishes
certification criteria for electronic health record technology. Expanding these criteria to
include AI interoperability standards would ensure seamless data exchange and improve
the usability of AI applications within clinical workflows.
HHS should accelerate the shift toward value-based reimbursement models that
incentivize high-quality outcomes rather than merely the volume of services provided.
Revisiting regulations defined in 42 CFR Part 400, which governs the Medicare program,
could help align payment methodologies with performance-based metrics related to AI
adoption in clinical care.
HHS could create incentives for conducting clinical trials that evaluate the effectiveness of
AI tools in improving patient outcomes. This effort could involve adapting the
investigational new drug application (IND) process outlined in 21 CFR Part 312 to
accommodate AI technologies aimed at clinical trials.
Encouraging public-private partnerships that promote collaborative research and
development in AI for clinical care is another important step. HHS could leverage various
regulations found in 42 CFR Parts 400-499, which govern Medicare and Medicaid programs,
to facilitate collaboration between government entities, academic institutions, and private
sector companies.
Investing in education and training programs that equip healthcare professionals with the
necessary skills to effectively utilize AI technologies is crucial. HHS could expand the
conditions for participation outlined in 42 CFR Part 486, which currently pertains to
education and training for health professionals, to include AI competency training.
HHS can create a more conducive environment for the development and use of AI in
clinical care by revisiting these specific regulations, policies, and programs.

3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise
novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and
accountability structures ( e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and
security). What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any,
should HHS play to help address them?
One of the primary concerns revolves around liability. As AI systems become increasingly
autonomous in making clinical recommendations, the question of who is liable for errors or
adverse outcomes becomes complex. If an AI tool provides a recommendation that leads
to a negative patient outcome, it may be unclear whether the liability lies with the
healthcare provider, the developer of the AI system or the institution utilizing the
technology. This ambiguity can create hesitation among providers to adopt AI solutions,
fearing potential legal repercussions.
Another significant issue is indemnification. The lack of clear guidelines regarding
indemnification agreements between developers and healthcare providers can complicate
the adoption of AI technologies. Providers may be reluctant to utilize AI tools without
assurances that they will not be held financially responsible for the consequences of AI
driven decisions. Establishing frameworks for indemnification that delineate
responsibilities and liabilities could help alleviate these concerns and encourage broader
adoption.
Privacy and security concerns are also at the forefront of the challenges associated with AI
in clinical care. The use of AI systems often involves the handling of sensitive patient data,
raising questions about data protection and compliance with regulations such as the
HIPAA. As AI algorithms require vast amounts of data for training and improvement,
ensuring that patient information is protected while still allowing for effective data sharing
poses a significant challenge. The potential for data breaches or misuse of patient
information can further erode trust in AI technologies among both providers and patients.
By providing clear definitions of accountability and establishing protocols for
indemnification, HHS can help mitigate the legal uncertainties that currently hinder the
adoption of AI technologies. HHS should prioritize the creation of data privacy and security
standards tailored specifically for AI applications in healthcare. This includes facilitating
the development of best practices for data governance that ensure patient information is
handled securely while allowing for the necessary data sharing to enhance AI training and
functionality.

4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre-
and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-
centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these
processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most impactful ( e.g., contracts, grants,
cooperative agreements, and/or prize competitions)?
Pre-deployment evaluation methods involve simulation testing. AI algorithms are validated
against historical clinical data. This approach allows developers to assess the AI's
predictive accuracy and performance in a controlled environment before implementing it in
actual clinical practice. Metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and
negative predictive value are crucial as they provide quantitative measures of the AI's
diagnostic capabilities. Testing is essential to evaluate how AI tools perform under various
scenarios, including different patient populations and clinical conditions. This ensures that
the algorithms are resilient and reliable across diverse use cases.
Post-deployment evaluation methods focus on continuous monitoring of AI performance in
real-time clinical settings. This can be achieved using feedback loops that allow healthcare
providers to report outcomes and experiences with the AI tool. Metrics such as clinical
outcomes, user satisfaction and workflow integration should be regularly assessed to
gauge the AI's effectiveness in improving patient care. Human centered evaluation
methods are vital, as engaging end-users in the evaluation process can provide insights
into usability, acceptance and areas for improvement.
HHS can ensure that AI tools meet high standards of performance and efficacy by providing
resources and frameworks for AI evaluation. The establishment of grants dedicated to
funding research and development initiatives focused on AI evaluation methodologies
would encourage academic institutions and healthcare organizations to collaborate on
projects that explore innovative evaluation techniques and metrics.
HHS should facilitate cooperative agreements with private sector partners to foster the
development of standardized evaluation frameworks for AI applications in clinical care. By
creating a collaborative environment, HHS can promote the sharing of best practices and
lessons learned from various implementations, enhancing the overall understanding of AI's
impact in healthcare.

5. How can HHS best support private sector activities ( e.g., accreditation,
certification, industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and
effective AI use in clinical care?
HHS should support the private sector by establishing clear accreditation standards
specifically designed for AI technologies in healthcare. By developing a comprehensive
framework for the accreditation of AI tools, HHS can provide a benchmark that ensures
these technologies meet high standards of safety, efficacy and reliability. This framework
would not only enhance public trust in AI applications but also facilitate the adoption of
these tools by healthcare providers.
HHS should create a streamlined certification process for AI applications that demonstrate
compliance with established standards. This certification would serve as an important
signal to healthcare providers, payers and patients about the quality and reliability of the AI
tools.
HHS can support the development of rigorous testing protocols that evaluate AI tools in
real-world clinical environments by fostering collaborations between private sector
companies and academic institutions. This collaborative testing framework can help
validate the effectiveness and safety of AI applications, providing valuable data that can
inform both developers and healthcare providers.
HHS can help ensure that providers are equipped with the necessary skills and knowledge
to effectively integrate AI tools into their workflows by offering training and certification
programs that focus on the responsible use of AI in clinical care. This investment in
workforce development not only promotes the effective use of AI but also fosters a culture
of innovation within healthcare organizations.
HHS should provide funding opportunities to encourage the development of innovative AI
solutions and the establishment of testing and certification processes. HHS can drive
advancements that improve patient outcomes and operational efficiency by allocating
resources to support research and development initiatives focused on AI in clinical care.
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and
cost expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools
would have the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights
on quality, and help reduce costs?
AI-driven diagnostic tools in radiology have demonstrated remarkable accuracy in
interpreting medical images, such as X-rays, MRIs, and CT scans. These AI systems can

analyze vast amounts of imaging data rapidly, often identifying patterns that may be missed
by the human eye. As a result, early detection of conditions such as cancer has improved,
leading to timely interventions that ultimately enhance patient outcomes. The use of AI in
radiology has streamlined workflows, reducing the time radiologists spend on image
interpretation and allowing them to focus more on patient care. 1
AI tools in predictive analytics have been effective in identifying patients at high risk for
conditions such as heart disease or diabetes. By analyzing electronic health records and
other patient data, these tools can provide healthcare providers with actionable insights,
enabling early interventions that can prevent costly hospitalizations and improve overall
patient management. This predictive capability not only enhances patient outcomes but
also contributes to significant cost savings for healthcare systems.2
However there have been instances where AI algorithms have demonstrated biases due to
the data on which they were trained, resulting in disparities in care for certain patient
populations. These issues highlight the importance of ensuring that AI systems are
developed with diverse datasets and that they are thoroughly validated in real-world
clinical settings before widespread implementation. 3, 4
There are numerous novel AI tools that hold great potential to further improve healthcare
outcomes, provide new insights on quality and help reduce costs. Natural language
processing (NLP) technologies, for example, could revolutionize clinical documentation
and data entry, allowing healthcare providers to focus more on patient interactions rather
than paperwork. By automatically extracting relevant information from unstructured clinical
notes, NLP tools can streamline administrative processes and enhance the efficiency of
clinical workflows.
AI-powered telemedicine platforms also present an exciting opportunity to expand access
to healthcare, particularly in underserved areas. By utilizing AI to triage patients and
facilitate remote consultations, these platforms can help reduce barriers to care and
ensure that patients receive timely medical attention without the need for in-person visits.

7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care
organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are
the primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
Chief Medical Officers (CMOs) play a crucial role in evaluating the efficacy and applicability
of AI tools in their respective areas and are often responsible for assessing the clinical
benefits of AI solutions, determining whether they align with the organization’s strategic
goals, and advocating for their integration into practice.
Chief Information Officers (CIOs) and Chief Technology Officers (CTOs) are pivotal in the
adoption of AI, as they oversee the integration of new technologies into existing systems.
Their expertise in information technology and data management is essential for ensuring
that AI tools are compatible with electronic health record (EHR) systems and other clinical
workflows.
The involvement of governance bodies, such as clinical committees and quality assurance
boards, is vital in evaluating the potential risks and benefits associated with AI applications.
These groups are responsible for establishing guidelines and protocols that ensure patient
safety and uphold clinical standards. Their endorsement can significantly influence the
decision-making process regarding the adoption of AI tools.
One of the primary challenges is the lack of clear regulatory frameworks and guidelines
specifically tailored to AI technologies. This uncertainty can create hesitation among
decision makers, causing delays in the approval and implementation of AI solutions.
Organizations may also struggle with understanding the legal and ethical implications of
using AI, particularly concerning patient privacy and data security.
Healthcare organizations often operate with established processes and clinical workflows
that may not readily accommodate new technologies. Resistance to change among staff
can further complicate the adoption of AI. Healthcare organizations often face limitations in
accessing high-quality, diverse datasets, which can hinder the development and
effectiveness of AI applications. Addressing this data gap is essential for fostering trust in AI
technologies and demonstrating their clinical value.
Reimbursement challenges pose a significant barrier to the widespread adoption of AI in
clinical care. Current payment models may not adequately recognize or reward the value of
AI-driven interventions, making it difficult for healthcare organizations to justify the
investment in these technologies.

8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research,
and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data
types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
One area where enhanced interoperability can make a substantial impact is in the
integration of electronic health record (EHR) systems. Currently, many healthcare
organizations utilize different EHR platforms that often operate in silos, making it difficult to
share patient data across systems. By establishing standardized data formats and
protocols for EHR interoperability, healthcare providers can ensure that patient information
is accessible and actionable across various platforms. This accessibility allows AI
algorithms to analyze comprehensive datasets that encompass diverse patient
populations, leading to more accurate predictions and improved clinical decision-making.
EHR interoperability, the standardization of clinical data types is essential for the effective
development of AI technologies. By adopting consistent terminologies and coding systems,
such as those outlined in the Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) framework,
healthcare organizations can facilitate smoother data sharing and integration. This
standardization not only simplifies the process of collecting and analyzing data but also
enhances the ability of AI tools to interpret clinical information accurately. With
standardized data formats, researchers can more easily aggregate data from various
studies and clinical trials, allowing for larger sample sizes and more robust findings that
can inform the development of AI applications.
Benchmarking tools also play a critical role in enhancing interoperability and accelerating
AI development. By providing standardized metrics for evaluating the performance of AI
algorithms, benchmarking tools enable healthcare organizations to assess the
effectiveness of different AI solutions in real-world clinical settings. These tools can
facilitate comparisons across various implementations, helping organizations identify best
practices and optimize AI applications for improved outcomes.
Enhanced interoperability also has the potential to fuel research initiatives by enabling data
sharing among academic institutions, industry partners and healthcare providers.
Collaborative research efforts that leverage interoperable data can lead to groundbreaking
discoveries and advancements in AI applications for clinical care.

9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see
addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do
patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
Challenges patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by AI adoption is personalization
of care. Patients seek AI's ability to tailor treatment plans to their unique health needs,
including genetic factors and lifestyle choices. 1 AI has the potential to analyze large
datasets to provide insights that promote personalized medicine. Caregivers desire AI tools
that can assist in making informed decisions about treatment options, facilitating better
health management for their loved ones.2 AI can help bridge gaps in care, especially in
underserved areas, by providing remote monitoring and telehealth solutions that enhance
access to healthcare services.
There are significant concerns about the privacy of personal health data and the potential
for misuse of information. AI systems require vast amounts of data to function effectively,
including sensitive personal health information (PHI) such as medical histories, genetic
data, and treatment outcomes. The sheer volume of data collected raises concerns about
how securely it is stored and managed. The risk of data breaches is a significant concern.
Healthcare organizations are prime targets for cyberattacks, and breaches can lead to
unauthorized access to sensitive information, resulting in identity theft, fraud, and other
malicious activities.3, 4
Patients worry that AI algorithms may perpetuate existing biases in healthcare, leading to
inequitable treatment outcomes for certain populations. Biased algorithms can lead to
misdiagnosis, inappropriate treatment plans, or inadequate access to care for marginalized
populations. For example, AI models used for predicting patient deterioration may not
accurately reflect the risk profiles of certain racial groups, leading to delayed interventions.
When patients perceive that algorithms do not fairly represent their health needs, their trust
in healthcare systems may diminish. This lack of trust can lead to lower engagement with
healthcare services and poorer health outcomes overall.5, 6

10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the
adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
One critical area for research to accelerate is the development of AI algorithms that focus
on predictive analytics. By harnessing vast datasets from electronic health records and
other sources, researchers can create AI systems that identify patients at high risk for
developing certain conditions, such as heart disease or diabetes. This proactive approach
enables healthcare providers to implement preventive measures and tailored
interventions, ultimately enhancing patient care and reducing the burden on healthcare
systems.
Another essential area for HHS to focus on is the integration of AI with telehealth
technologies. The COVID-19 pandemic has underscored the value of telehealth in providing
accessible care, especially for patients in remote or underserved regions. Research into AI
applications that can enhance telehealth interactions such as virtual health assistants or AI
driven triage tools can improve patient engagement and satisfaction.
HHS should prioritize research into AI applications that address health disparities and
promote health equity. By developing algorithms that account for social determinants of
health and other contextual factors, researchers can create AI tools that better serve
diverse populations.
Research into data privacy and security in the context of AI is another area that HHS should
prioritize. AI systems often rely on large datasets, ensuring the protection of sensitive
patient information is paramount. Investigating innovative methods for safeguarding data
while still allowing for effective AI training and operation can help mitigate privacy
concerns.
a. Are there published findings about the impact of adopted AI tools and their use
clinical care?
One significant area of impact is in medical imaging, where AI tools have been shown to
assist radiologists in interpreting images more accurately and efficiently. Studies have
reported that AI algorithms can match or even exceed human performance in detecting
conditions such as breast cancer in mammograms, lung cancer in chest X-rays, and
various abnormalities in MRI scans. For instance, a meta-analysis published in the

journal Nature found that AI systems demonstrated a high level of sensitivity and specificity
in identifying cancerous lesions, leading to earlier detection and treatment.1,2
AI has also made strides in predictive analytics. Research has shown that AI tools can
analyze electronic health records (EHRs) to identify patients at risk for adverse events, such
as hospital readmissions or severe complications. For example, a study published in the
journal JAMA Internal Medicine highlighted an AI model that successfully predicted hospital
readmissions for heart failure patients with a high degree of accuracy. This capability allows
healthcare providers to implement targeted interventions that can reduce readmission
rates and improve overall patient management.3,4
AI has been applied to enhance patient engagement and adherence to treatment plans. AI-
driven chatbots and virtual health assistants have been developed to provide patients with
personalized health information, medication reminders, and support for chronic disease
management. Research has indicated that these tools can lead to improved patient
adherence to treatment regimens and increased satisfaction with care. A study published
in The Journal of Medical Internet Research found that patients who interacted with AI-
powered health assistants reported higher levels of engagement in their care compared to
those who did not use such tools.5,6
AI technologies have also been utilized to optimize operational efficiencies within
healthcare organizations. Research has demonstrated that AI can improve scheduling,
resource allocation, and patient flow management in hospitals. For instance, an analysis
published in Health Affairs revealed that AI algorithms could predict patient demand for
services, facilitating better staff planning and reducing wait times for patients. This not only
enhances patient satisfaction but can also lead to cost savings for healthcare facilities.7,8
Published findings indicate that AI tools have the potential to transform clinical care by
enhancing diagnostic capabilities, improving patient outcomes, increasing operational
efficiency, and fostering patient engagement, paving the way for a more effective and
responsive healthcare system.

b. How does the literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI as
part of clinical care?
The implementation of AI technologies in healthcare requires significant upfront
investments in software, hardware, and training. Healthcare organizations often face high
costs related to acquiring AI tools, integrating them into existing systems, and maintaining
these technologies over time. In addition to initial costs, organizations must consider
ongoing operational expenses, including data management, system updates, and
personnel training to ensure that staff can effectively utilize AI tools.
The benefits of using AI in clinical care offer improved Patient Outcomes. AI has the
potential to enhance patient outcomes by providing more accurate diagnostics,
personalized treatment plans, and predictive analytics that can lead to early interventions.
Studies have shown that AI algorithms can outperform human clinicians in certain
diagnostic tasks.1
AI can streamline clinical workflows, reduce administrative burdens, and minimize errors,
ultimately leading to cost savings. By automating routine tasks, AI allows healthcare
professionals to focus on more complex clinical decisions, enhancing overall productivity.2
AI tools can provide clinicians with evidence-based insights and recommendations,
improving the quality of decision-making. This capability can enhance clinical judgment
and support more informed treatment choices.3
The deployment of AI technologies raises questions about equity and access to care. If AI
solutions are not designed with inclusivity in mind, marginalized populations may face
disparities in access to advanced healthcare technologies.4
The literature provides a view of the costs, benefits and transfers associated with the use of
AI in clinical care. While the potential benefits of improved patient outcomes, efficiency

and enhanced decision-making are significant, healthcare organizations must also be
mindful of the costs involved. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for the successful
integration of AI into clinical practice.
