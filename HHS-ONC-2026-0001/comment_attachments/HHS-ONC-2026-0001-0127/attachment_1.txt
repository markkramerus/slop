Getting to “On”
Generative AI differs from prior healthcare innovations in ways that can make clinical
leadership reluctant to adopt and scale it. Because generative AI is probabilistic, its
performance must be validated not only before deployment but also continuously over time,
requiring skills and resources that may be scarce in healthcare settings. Its costs—driven by
compute, storage, and model usage—are also highly variable and difficult for organizations to
predict, budget, or govern using traditional software pricing models.
At the same time, AI delivers meaningful benefits: improved clinician productivity, reduced
documentation burden, lower burnout, more timely clinical decision-making, fewer missed
orders, improved throughput, and better patient engagement. Over time, these gains can
translate into reduced staffing pressure and overtime costs, higher-quality documentation,
improved access and satisfaction, and better clinical outcomes through earlier interventions.
Realizing these benefits requires healthcare leaders to move past understandable initial
hesitation and turn AI features on. While familiarity and trust with new technology develop
over time, we believe there are concrete steps the Department can take now to better align
incentives and accelerate adoption.
Documenting ROI
One question any responsible healthcare administrator will ask before deploying new AI
technology is whether it represents a sound investment. Healthcare organizations operate
today on thinner margins than ever; for many, it simply is not an option to invest in
technologies that will not pay off financially. We believe many applications of generative AI in
healthcare do present sufficient return on investment (ROI).
The Department could help document ROI data in a way that healthcare providers can both
rely and act on. For example, studies show that AI scribe technology—in which AI records and
transcribes clinical interactions and then drafts corresponding notes—can reduce the time
clinicians spend working after hours. Less “pajama time” for providers, however, does not by
itself offset the cost of the technology. The true ROI of AI scribes appears downstream: happier
clinicians are less likely to burn out or leave, reducing recruitment and onboarding costs.
The Department could address this gap by assembling a repository of AI use cases with
demonstrated ROI. By conducting independent research, such a repository would validate
vendor claims and highlight metrics relevant to healthcare administrators’ bottom-line
decisions. Such a repository would be vendor-agnostic, focusing on use cases rather than
specific implementations, to not advantage those vendors that get their claims published.
Done right, it would serve as a trusted reference for organizations evaluating AI investments.

Bridging the Adoption-to-ROI Gap
Even when an AI use case has a proven ROI, initial adoption may still be financially challenging.
The benefits of AI are rarely immediate and often require workflow changes, training, and
iteration to be fully realized. Early phases of adoption are typically marked by uneven usage,
ongoing configuration, and infrastructure costs that scale before productivity gains materialize.
As a result, organizations may incur real costs before seeing offsetting savings, creating a
financial mismatch that can slow or stall adoption.
One response to these challenges would be fee-for-service reimbursement for AI use, but this
approach could quickly become costly and, for AI with proven ROI, unnecessary. Organizations
primarily need support through the initial scaling period; once broad adoption is achieved, AI
that delivers value will pay for itself.
Epic has had success spurring AI adoption among customers with our Jump Start Cloud
Infrastructure Package, a program we created to simplify and support early adoption of
generative AI. It covers the cloud infrastructure costs for roughly 50 AI features with a flat
monthly fee based on organization size. This approach encourages responsible, broad
adoption rather than narrow pilots driven by short-term cost avoidance.
The results have been impressive. Jump Start customers have demonstrated median month-
over-month AI resource utilization growth of 45%, compared to 18% for non-Jump Start
customers. They also adopt more new AI features—5.5 on average, compared to 4.6 over the
same period. Overall, they use 17 times more AI resources than non-Jump Start customers of
comparable size. These differences are not explained by organization size or early-adopter
effects; in fact, Jump Start customers are slightly smaller on average. Rather, Jump Start
succeeds because it aligns incentives to encourage rapid scaling. Once customers reach broad
adoption, they see the value of AI—and continue using it.
The Department could achieve similar incentive alignment via AI adoption grants that cover
infrastructure costs for a fixed period of time rather than a fixed amount of money. Traditional
grants incentivize slow scaling to extend funding; time-based grants would encourage
organizations to scale quickly, assess value, and determine which use cases merit continued
investment. After the grant period ends, organizations could discontinue low-value AI while
sustaining AI that delivers sufficient benefit—without ongoing government spending.
Such programs could be structured as true grants or as fixed-price arrangements similar to
Jump Start. In the latter case, however, pricing should scale with organization size to avoid
disadvantaging smaller or lower-resourced providers. (If desired, pricing could even be
structured disproportionately to give an advantage to smaller organizations.) Properly

Making Local Validation Possible
Another concern AI adopters rightly have is safety. Many healthcare AI use cases fall outside
the oversight of the Food and Drug Administration, and even regulated products are poorly
served by traditional evaluation methods. Especially for AI that is probabilistic, pre-deployment
testing on generalized datasets has limited predictive value for real-world performance.
The better method for validating AI performance in healthcare is “local validation,” in which the
healthcare facility, independent of the developer, evaluates how the AI performs on its own
data while embedded in its own workflow. This can be done both prior to deployment, using
local but retrospective data, and on a continual basis post-deployment (“ongoing monitoring”)
to make sure performance does not “drift” over time. This way, AI features can be deemed safe
no matter what patient acuity mix is being served by a particular healthcare organization.
Local validation also matters because AI performance can be sensitive to the workflow into
which the AI is integrated. For example, a popular use case for predictive modelling forecasts
the likelihood of imminent medical deterioration. The output of such a model is not binary but
instead a probability. When implementing this model, a deployer must decide what probability
threshold should trigger an alert for the care team to investigate further. This choice has a
significant impact on the “performance” of the model. If an organization sets the alert
threshold low, there will likely be more false positives; if the organization sets the threshold
higher, there will likely be more false negatives. Yet the organization may have good reason to
change this setting: organizations with dedicated remote monitoring teams may prefer false
positives, while those who must rely only on frontline nurses managing a large caseload of
patients may not be able to tolerate them.
The challenge for post-deployment validation, in healthcare at least, is that small or rural
healthcare organizations often struggle to afford the necessary data science resources and
expertise to perform effective monitoring. Yet because AI performance will vary from
organization to organization, post-deployment validation is not something that can be
outsourced in practice. Without appropriate support for “local validation” activities, AI could
widen, rather than narrow, the digital divide in healthcare.
AI developers can help. In 2024, for example, Epic released an open-source tool called the AI
Trust and Assurance Suite that automates the most resource-intensive aspects of post-
deployment monitoring. The suite's real-time evaluations use the deployer's local data and
workflows and can monitor both Epic and third-party AI features, using the deployer's choice
of evaluation methodologies.

Providing Guidance for AI Validation
Even with the tools and resources needed to conduct local validation, healthcare organizations
may not know how to use them effectively. Today, there is little consensus on how to evaluate
traditional predictive models, and even less for generative AI. Gold-standard guidance such as
the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s AI Risk Management Framework asserts
the importance of ongoing monitoring but does not specify how to perform it. Vendors
providing evaluation services may use proprietary metrics or allow the user to select among
evaluation methodologies (as the AI Trust and Assurance Suite does); both limit comparability.
Regulators can add value by defining standard metrics and validation processes for AI
performance. Such standards need not be mandatory; guidance alone would increase
confidence among healthcare AI adopters and lower the barrier to widescale adoption. Clear,
shared methodologies would allow organizations to focus on performing validation rather than
designing it—especially if incorporated into existing tools.
Importantly, validation guidance should be specific to both the use case and the deployment
context, not one-size-fits-all. The risks, benefits, and appropriate metrics for documentation
assistance, clinical decision support, predictive modeling, and patient engagement tools differ
substantially. Tailored guidance for common classes of AI applications would allow
organizations to focus their efforts on meaningful evaluation rather than reinventing validation
frameworks from scratch.
Clear federal guidance would also accelerate adoption by lowering the perceived risk of
deployment. Many healthcare leaders delay or limit AI use not because they believe AI is
unsafe, but because they lack confidence that they are evaluating it correctly. By clarifying
expectations and establishing shared norms, the Department could help organizations move
from cautious experimentation to responsible scale.
Building AI Fluency
Technology adoption depends not only on technical readiness, but also on human
understanding and trust. Considerable attention has been paid to training clinicians on how to
use AI tools effectively, verify outputs, and avoid automation bias. While this training is
essential, a more foundational form of AI fluency is also needed to overcome barriers to
adoption—particularly among non-technical stakeholders such as clinical leadership, frontline
staff, and labor representatives.

In healthcare, AI is unlikely to displace workers. Persistent labor shortages across clinical roles,
combined with rising demand driven by an aging population, mean that AI is increasingly
necessary to sustain access to care. In practice, most healthcare AI use cases are designed to
reduce administrative burden, support clinical judgment, and allow clinicians to spend more
time with patients. Nonetheless, fears that AI will eliminate jobs remain widespread and
increasingly surface in labor negotiations.
These concerns often stem from a lack of shared understanding about what healthcare AI
does—and does not—do. Without credible, accessible education, AI adoption risks being
perceived as a cost-cutting measure rather than a capacity-multiplying one. This perception
can slow implementation, limit usage, or prompt organizations to constrain AI to narrow pilots
rather than deploy it broadly where it could deliver the most benefit.
The federal government can play an important role in addressing this gap by supporting
neutral, trusted education on AI’s role in healthcare. This could include developing materials
tailored to different stakeholder groups; supporting peer-to-peer learning networks among
healthcare organizations; or incorporating AI fluency into existing workforce development and
quality improvement programs. Such efforts would complement, rather than replace, vendor-
led training by focusing on system-level understanding rather than tool-specific instruction.
Ultimately, however, the most effective way to build AI fluency is through experience. When
clinicians and staff see AI reduce documentation burden, improve workflow efficiency, and
support—not override—clinical judgment, skepticism gives way to trust. Policies that enable
earlier, broader, and more responsible adoption of AI—such as those outlined in this
response—will therefore do more to build AI fluency than education alone. By helping
healthcare organizations move past prolonged pilots and into sustained use, the Department
can ensure that AI’s benefits are visible, tangible, and widely shared.
