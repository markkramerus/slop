Response to HHS RFI
Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care
Docket: HHS Health Sector AI RFI
Submitted by: Mosaic Life Tech
Date: [For submission by February 23, 2026]
Status: DRAFT v4.3 FOR FOUNDER REVIEW
Submitter Information
Mosaic Life Tech is a healthcare AI governance advisory firm. Our principals have over
50 years of combined experience in health information technology, including leadership
roles in federal health IT programs (ONC, CDC, ASTP), health information exchange
operations serving over four million patients, and clinical informatics. We advise
healthcare executives on establishing governance structures for AI-influenced clinical
decisions.
We submit these comments based on direct participation in federal health IT policy
development, industry governance forums, and review of recent survey data on AI
adoption and oversight in healthcare settings.
Response to Question 3
Question: For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may
raise novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and
accountability structures (e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security).
What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS
play to help address them?
The most significant governance challenge is not legal ambiguity at the federal level. It
is organizational ambiguity within health systems themselves.
Most health systems — and over 70% of acute care hospitals — now have AI tools
influencing clinical decisions, whether through EHR-embedded algorithms, operational
scheduling tools, or documentation assistants, though adoption drops sharply among
independent, rural, and critical access hospitals. Many of these tools fall outside FDA
device regulation. The question of "what federal rules apply" often has a clear answer.
The question of "who inside our organization is accountable for this AI-influenced
decision" frequently does not.
Recent survey data confirms a pervasive governance gap. The CHIME/Censinet AI
Adoption Survey (December 2025) found that only 59% of healthcare organizations
require formal approval before AI implementation, meaning over 40% lack even a basic

pre-implementation gate. Just 10% use automated monitoring to detect AI capabilities
within their existing software, while 51% rely on informal, ad hoc discovery. ECRI has
ranked "Insufficient Governance of AI in Healthcare" as the #1 health technology hazard
for 2025, warning that failure to develop systemwide governance increases both patient
safety and liability risk.
This pattern extends to federal healthcare settings. In January 2026, the VA Office of
Inspector General reported that VHA had deployed generative AI tools without
coordination with the National Center for Patient Safety, despite VHA Directive 1050.01
requiring such coordination to promote and safeguard patient safety. The OIG found no
formal process to track AI-related safety issues. This illustrates how even large,
sophisticated healthcare organizations can deploy AI without the governance structures
needed to manage risk.
The governance gap is widening. HTI-5's proposed removal of AI model card
transparency requirements at § 170.315(b)(11) and significant reduction in DSI
certification criteria effectively shifts the responsibility for evaluating AI safety, bias, and
fitness-for-purpose from federal certification requirements to deploying healthcare
organizations and the market. This shift is navigable for well-resourced health systems
with dedicated informatics leadership. For small and mid-market organizations,
community hospitals, and safety-net providers, the shift creates a governance burden
they lack capacity to meet.
The data confirms a "digital divide" in AI governance capacity. ASTP/ONC and AHA
report that 86% of system-affiliated hospitals use predictive AI versus just 37% of
independent facilities. HFMA survey data shows that among the 18% of health systems
with mature AI governance, three-quarters have over $1 billion in net patient revenue.
Dr. Brian Anderson, CEO of the Coalition for Health AI, has noted that the digital divide
is already being reinforced in the AI space, with rural communities lacking the IT staff to
deploy and configure AI tools.
Vendor contracts address liability and indemnification from the vendor's perspective. But
those contracts cannot resolve internal accountability when the organization hasn't
decided who is responsible for validating AI tools against their patient population,
monitoring for drift, or escalating concerns. A 2025 BMC Health Services Research
study of AI implementation experts identified a "responsibility vacuum" where
organizational structures obfuscate who should have accountability for AI monitoring.
Only 22% of hospital leaders report high confidence they could produce a complete AI
audit trail within 30 days if requested by a regulator.
HHS can help address this in several ways without adding regulatory burden:
First, HHS can support and amplify voluntary governance frameworks already emerging
in the field. The Joint Commission and the Coalition for Health AI published the
Guidance on the Responsible Use of AI in Healthcare on September 17, 2025. This
guidance provides a reasonable structure for organizational accountability across seven
elements: governance structures, patient privacy, data security, quality monitoring,
voluntary safety event reporting, risk and bias assessment, and education and training.

Because the Joint Commission holds CMS deeming authority under 42 CFR Part 488,
health systems are treating this guidance as a roadmap for aligning with likely future
accreditation expectations. HHS recognition of such frameworks, without mandating
them, would signal that establishing internal governance is expected and supported.
Second, HHS can consider technical assistance models that help under-resourced
organizations build governance capacity. The Regional Extension Center program
provides a clear precedent: it assisted approximately 134,000 primary care providers,
and ONC evaluations found that 68% of REC participants achieved Stage 1 Meaningful
Use versus just 12% of non-participants. A similar model for AI governance, potentially
leveraging existing infrastructure at HRSA (through FQHCs), SAMHSA, or other
agencies, could help smaller organizations develop oversight capabilities they currently
lack.
Third, HHS can fund or disseminate research on effective governance structures.
Health systems are experimenting with AI governance committees, chief AI officer roles,
and cross-functional oversight bodies. KLAS Research has noted that "stronger
organizational governance and ownership are needed to drive broader AI adoption" and
that when governance provides a structured way to vet and integrate AI, adoption is
more scalable and sustainable. Implementation science focused on AI governance,
potentially through NIH or AHRQ, would help the field learn faster than each system
discovering through trial and error.
The novel issue is not that AI creates new legal exposure. It is that AI creates decisions
that cross traditional departmental boundaries, and most organizations have not
adapted their governance structures to match. Federal clarity that organizational
governance is expected, combined with support for building that capacity, would do
more to accelerate responsible adoption than additional regulation.
Response to Question 5
Question: How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation,
certification, industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and
effective AI use in clinical care?
The private sector has already begun developing governance frameworks for AI in
clinical care. HHS can accelerate adoption by supporting these efforts rather than
duplicating them through regulation.
The Joint Commission, in collaboration with the Coalition for Health AI, published the
Guidance on the Responsible Use of AI in Healthcare on September 17, 2025. This
guidance addresses governance structure, validation, monitoring, training, and board
visibility through seven core elements. Because the Joint Commission holds CMS
deeming authority under 42 CFR Part 488 and accredits more than 80% of U.S.
hospitals, health systems are paying attention to this guidance even though it does not
create new accreditation standards. Legal analysts note that this guidance follows the
Joint Commission's established pattern: the National Patient Safety Goals began as

recommendations before becoming accreditation requirements, and health equity
guidance from 2023 became mandatory requirements effective January 2024.
Professional societies are also building AI-specific infrastructure. The American College
of Radiology has launched Assess-AI, described as the world's first AI quality registry
designed to monitor ongoing performance of imaging AI algorithms in real-world clinical
settings. ACR's Recognized Center for Healthcare-AI (ARCH-AI) program provides a
recognition framework for facilities meeting governance, model selection, acceptance
testing, and monitoring criteria. ACR is working toward a formal AI accreditation
program, with ACR Council approval anticipated for Spring 2027. The AMA's STEPS
Forward toolkit provides practical governance guidance for health system leaders
establishing AI oversight functions, including committee structure, decision rights, and
escalation pathways. These efforts represent the field building governance from within,
informed by clinical realities that federal regulation alone cannot capture.
HHS can support this work in several concrete ways:
HHS can publicly recognize voluntary frameworks as representing reasonable practice.
This does not require endorsement or mandate. A statement that organizations
following such guidance are taking appropriate steps toward responsible AI use would
reduce uncertainty and encourage adoption.
HHS can support accreditation bodies in developing AI governance competencies within
existing survey processes. Surveyors already assess governance, quality improvement,
and patient safety. Integrating AI oversight into these existing frameworks, rather than
creating separate AI-specific requirements, would reduce burden while improving
accountability.
HHS can fund professional organizations and associations in developing training and
credentialing for AI governance roles. Health systems are creating AI governance
committees and oversight functions, but the people filling these roles often lack formal
training in AI risk assessment or clinical validation methods. The AMA's STEPS Forward
toolkit on AI governance provides a starting point, but broader workforce development
would build capacity across the industry.
HHS can also support industry-driven testing and validation infrastructure. Health
systems struggle to validate vendor claims about AI performance on their specific
patient populations. Shared testing resources, benchmark datasets, or cooperative
validation arrangements could help smaller and mid-sized systems that lack internal
capacity for rigorous evaluation.
The risk of heavy federal regulation is that it freezes current practice into compliance
requirements before the field has learned what actually works. The opportunity in
supporting private sector frameworks is that governance can evolve with the technology
while still establishing reasonable expectations for oversight.
Response to Question 7

Question: Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care
organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are
the primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
No single role consistently owns AI adoption decisions across health systems. This
fragmentation is itself a primary hurdle.
Industry leadership patterns reflect a structural divide between technical authority and
clinical oversight. CHIME and AMDIS surveys document this dynamic: CIOs control
technology infrastructure and serve on 63% of AI governance committees, but are
typically not clinicians. CMIOs and CNIOs have clinical credibility but serve on only 45%
of AI governance committees; AMDIS/WittKieffer surveys show that only 19% of CMIOs
have a dedicated leadership budget, with 40% operating under the IT department’s
budget. Quality and patient safety leaders focus on outcomes but are rarely consulted
during AI acquisition. Risk management addresses liability but often learns about AI
deployments after the fact. Boards ask questions about AI oversight but receive
inconsistent answers about who is accountable. The CHIME/Censinet survey found that
33% of organizations cite "unclear ownership" between IT, clinical, and compliance
teams as a primary barrier to governance.
The CFO is often the decisive voice. HFMA's 2025 survey of 233 health system leaders
found that CFOs and CIOs are the leading decision-makers when weighing AI vendor
offerings, with cost reduction cited as the most frequent rationale for investment. Only
39% of CFOs believed AI investments would actually reduce overall costs, yet financial
justification remains the primary gate. HHS's own RFI on accelerating AI in clinical care
identifies reimbursement and payment policy as key levers, recognizing that AI adoption
decisions frequently come down to financial justification. Yet CFOs typically lack the
clinical or technical context to evaluate AI risk, creating a disconnect between the
person who approves the investment and the people who understand what is being
approved.
The organizations adopting AI most effectively have established clear governance
ownership, typically through cross-functional AI governance committees with explicit
decision rights, or through designated AI oversight roles that report to executive
leadership. These structures do not slow adoption. They accelerate it, provided they
include explicit decision rights and clear escalation pathways. Black Book Research
found that health systems with AI governance councils are twice as likely to achieve
ROI within 12 months, and programs with dashboards and clear ownership reach early
ROI in 7.5 months versus 13.5 months without them. KLAS Research has observed that
when governance provides a structured way to vet and integrate AI, adoption is more
scalable and sustainable.
The primary administrative hurdle is not technology cost or complexity. It is the absence
of a defined process for answering basic governance questions: Who approves the use
of this AI tool? Who validates it against our patient population? Who monitors it after
deployment? Who decides when to turn it off?

HHS could support adoption by funding research on effective governance structures
and disseminating findings. Health systems are solving these problems independently,
but there is limited sharing of what works across organizations. Implementation science
focused on AI governance would help the field learn faster.
Closing
We appreciate the Department’s focus on accelerating AI adoption while maintaining
appropriate oversight. Across the governance challenges identified in Question 3, the
private sector frameworks addressed in Question 5, and the organizational decision-
making dynamics explored in Question 7, the evidence points consistently in one
direction: governance is not a barrier to adoption but an enabler of it. Organizations with
clear AI governance ownership achieve ROI faster and scale more effectively than
those without. The most effective path forward supports organizational governance
capacity rather than prescribing specific requirements. Health systems are ready to act.
What they need from HHS is not more rules, but clear expectations and capacity
support that help them govern AI responsibly.
