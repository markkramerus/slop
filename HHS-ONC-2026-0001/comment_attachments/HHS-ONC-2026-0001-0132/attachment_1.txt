Comment on HHS Health Sector AI RFI
Docket: HHS-ONC-2026-0001
Submitted by:
Eli Talbert, PhD
Senior Data Scientist, United States Special Operations Command
The submitter works on operational AI governance in defense decision-support contexts, with a
focus on accountability, documentation, and escalation design; this comment applies those
governance principles to clinical AI adoption and oversight.
RFI Questions Addressed: Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4, Q7, Q8, Q10
Executive Summary: Four Immediate Policy
Actions
Regulatory uncertainty and liability ambiguity are among the most significant barriers to clinical
AI adoption. The actions below would accelerate deployment by establishing a common
accountability floor—reducing the duplicative institutional governance costs and defensive non-
adoption that currently slows innovation. To that end, HHS should:
1. Issue ONC interpretive guidance requiring minimum decision logging for high-
impact AI-enabled clinical decision support integrated into EHR workflows.
(Responds to RFI Questions 2, 3, 8)
2. Clarify accountability allocation expectations for non-device AI through joint ONC–
FDA guidance. (Responds to RFI Questions 2, 3)
3. Direct CMS to incorporate accountable AI documentation standards into
Conditions of Participation for hospitals deploying high-impact AI tools. (Responds
to RFI Questions 2, 7)
4. Fund NIH/AHRQ implementation science pilots testing post-deployment human–AI
interaction metrics, including override monitoring. (Responds to RFI Questions 4, 10)
These actions are likely to reduce liability ambiguity, strengthen interoperability, and create
measurable governance standards that accelerate responsible adoption, though empirical
evaluation of adoption and compliance effects should accompany implementation. This comment
does not propose new regulatory agencies, pre-market approval requirements for non-device AI,
or blanket restrictions on deployment. It proposes minimum documentation and logging
standards that reduce the regulatory uncertainty currently driving defensive non-adoption and
duplicative institutional compliance costs.
1

For purposes of this comment, “high-impact AI” refers to AI systems that materially influence
diagnostic, triage, medication, procedural, or discharge decisions, particularly in settings where
clinician workload, time pressure, or financial incentives may shape reliance patterns.
I. Close the Regulatory Gap for Non-Device
Clinical AI
[Responds to RFI Questions 1, 2, 3, 7]
Governance challenges in clinical AI mirror those in other high-stakes, time-pressured human-
algorithm decision environments: authority boundaries, escalation design, and documentation
standards determine whether hybrid systems remain accountable over time. In defense decision-
support systems, these are not optional governance add-ons but prerequisites for deployment
authorization. Clinical environments differ from military command structures in training
standardization, liability regimes, and autonomy structures, but both domains confront the same
core challenge: maintaining auditable authority boundaries in high-stakes human–algorithm
collaboration.
Existing HHS frameworks already address important components of this landscape, including:
 FDA regulation of Software as a Medical Device (SaMD)
 Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCP) governing planned device modifications
 ONC HTI-1 Decision Support Intervention (DSI) transparency requirements (45 CFR §
170.315)
 CMS documentation standards under the Conditions of Participation (42 CFR § 482.24)
For clarity, this comment distinguishes between (a) FDA-regulated device software pathways,
including SaMD submissions and PCCP-governed modifications, and (b) ONC-certified health
IT functionality and CMS documentation expectations applicable to EHR-integrated decision
support that may not meet device definitions. The governance gap identified here primarily
concerns workflow-integrated, non-device AI functionality embedded within certified health IT
environments.
Within this non-device space, a structural gap remains. Current frameworks emphasize model
transparency, performance reporting, and change management. They do not specify:
 How clinician override behavior must be logged
 How authority boundaries between model and clinician are operationally defined
 How escalation pathways are monitored over time
2

This creates ambiguity in accountability allocation and increases institutional hesitation to
deploy AI at scale.
HHS can close this gap through interpretive guidance clarifying documentation and
accountability expectations, using existing regulatory authority under 45 CFR § 170.315 (ONC
certification criteria) and 42 CFR § 482.24 (CMS Conditions of Participation for medical record
services).
II. Establish Minimum Decision Logging
Standards
[Responds to RFI Questions 2, 4, 8]
HHS should establish — through ONC guidance or certification updates under 45 CFR §
170.315 — a minimum logging requirement for high-impact AI-enabled CDS.
At minimum, systems should record:
1. Timestamp of model output
2. Model recommendation or risk score
3. Clinician action (accept / modify / override)
4. Override flag with standardized rationale category
5. Escalation pathway triggered (if any)
6. Linkage to downstream order or documented outcome
7. Patient notification flag (whether the patient was informed AI contributed to the decision)
Illustrative workflow: An EHR-integrated sepsis risk model generates a high-probability alert for
a patient in a medical-surgical unit. The attending clinician reviews the alert but determines that
the elevated score reflects a known chronic condition, and overrides the recommendation. Under
the proposed schema, the system logs the model output (Elements 1–2), the override action with
a standardized rationale code (Elements 3–4), and the absence of an escalation trigger (Element
5). Downstream, the patient’s outcome is linked to the decision (Element 6). This structured
record enables quality review to distinguish informed clinical judgment from unexamined
dismissal—without requiring real-time intervention or additional clinician workflow steps.
3

Critically, this schema imposes minimal marginal burden. Elements 1–3 build directly on
existing certified health IT audit logging capabilities required under 45 CFR § 170.315. Elements
4–7 require structured configuration within EHR workflows but not novel infrastructure or
hardware investment. The absence of a common standard imposes higher costs: each
deploying institution must independently design governance documentation, negotiate
liability allocation with vendors, and defend ad hoc logging practices—duplicative effort
that a federal floor would eliminate. Standardization reduces institutional compliance burden
while creating the interoperable evidence base that regulators, payers, and health systems need to
support scaled deployment.
4

Implementation Feasibility
Certified health IT must support audit logging of actions related to electronic health information.
However, AI-specific decision telemetry, including structured capture of model output, override
rationale, and escalation triggers, typically requires additional configuration or integration within
EHR workflows. Elements 1–3 build on existing audit logging capabilities, while Elements 4–7
generally require structured configuration but not novel infrastructure. The patient notification
flag would indicate whether AI involvement was disclosed in accordance with institutional
policy; this proposal does not prescribe the notification workflow itself. The schema should be
machine-readable and expressible via FHIR AuditEvent or equivalent interoperable standards,
consistent with USCDI expansion goals.
This aligns with:
 HTI-1 transparency requirements under 45 CFR § 170.315(b)(11)
 FDA Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) provisions under FD&C Act § 515C
(21 U.S.C. § 360e-4), which address planned modifications and when additional
premarket submissions are required; post-deployment monitoring expectations may arise
under device quality system and postmarket reporting frameworks (e.g., 21 CFR Parts
803 and 820), though PCCP provisions are primarily change-control focused.
 Regulatory concern regarding superficial review or rubber-stamping of AI
recommendations, consistent with HHS and OIG compliance principles on meaningful
clinician oversight
Such a schema would not inhibit innovation. It would reduce uncertainty and demonstrate
accountable deployment. Additionally, structured logging enables detection of disparate override
or recommendation patterns across protected characteristics, supporting existing
nondiscrimination obligations under federal civil rights law and strengthening patient trust in AI-
assisted care.
III. Ground Oversight in Empirical Evidence
[Responds to RFI Questions 4, 10]
Automation bias in clinical decision support has been documented in peer-reviewed literature
spanning over a decade, with recent findings confirming that the problem persists in production
AI deployments:
 Goddard et al., Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association (2012):
Systematic review establishing that automation bias is a consistent and measurable
phenomenon in clinical decision support, identifying frequency, effect mediators, and
mitigators.
 Lyell & Coiera, Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association (2017): A
PRISMA-compliant systematic review of 40 studies that found that automation bias
increases as verification complexity rises. This dynamic is particularly relevant to AI-
5

enabled clinical decision support, where clinicians often operate under high information
load and time pressure.
 Gaube et al., NPJ Digital Medicine (2021): In a randomized study of chest radiograph
interpretation, clinicians were influenced by both accurate and inaccurate advice.
Diagnostic accuracy declined substantially when participants received incorrect
recommendations, regardless of whether the advice was labeled as coming from an “AI
system” or a “human expert.” The source label affected perceived credibility ratings but
did not significantly change diagnostic performance.
 Sivaraman et al., Proceedings of the CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing
Systems (2023): Showed that clinician trust calibration in AI-based treatment
recommendations is difficult to achieve, and that reliance patterns go beyond binary
accept/reject decisions—reinforcing the need for structured override logging.
 Khera, Simon & Ross, JAMA (2023): Editorial accompanying Jabbour et al. empirical
research; warned that even in controlled settings without time pressure, clinicians favored
AI-based decision-making systems despite contradictory or clinically nonsensical
information, identifying automation bias as a direct risk of harm from AI-driven CDS.
 Dratsch et al., Radiology (2023): In a study of AI-assisted mammography (BI-RADS)
categorization, incorrect AI suggestions significantly impaired diagnostic performance
across all experience levels. While greater expertise reduced the magnitude of the effect,
even very experienced radiologists remained measurably susceptible to incorrect
recommendations.
These findings collectively establish that performance metrics alone do not capture behavioral
adaptation in hybrid human–AI systems. Over-reliance patterns emerge even among experienced
clinicians and are amplified under conditions of high information load and complexity. Existing
evidence suggests that transparency or labeling interventions alone do not reliably eliminate
these effects.
Structured override monitoring enables evidence-based oversight without imposing blanket
restrictions on deployment. Logging enables institutions to detect, quantify, and respond to
behavioral adaptation patterns.
IV. Clarify Accountability Allocation for Non-
Device AI
[Responds to RFI Questions 2, 3]
HHS should clarify expectations regarding:
 Designation of accountable organizational owner for AI deployment
 Documentation requirements for AI-assisted high-risk decisions
 Distinction between device-regulated AI and workflow-integrated non-device AI
This clarification could be implemented through:
6

 ONC interpretive guidance under HTI authority (45 CFR § 170.315)
 Joint FDA–ONC guidance on hybrid accountability allocation
 CMS Conditions of Participation updates referencing documentation standards (42 CFR §
482.24)
Clear guidance reduces defensive non-adoption driven by liability uncertainty. Such
clarification reduces uncertainty without altering tort standards; it establishes documentation
expectations that support, rather than supplant, existing malpractice and product liability
frameworks.
V. Fund Research on Human–AI Interaction in
Deployed Systems
[Responds to RFI Questions 4, 10]
HHS should fund applied research to:
1. Measure longitudinal override patterns in deployed systems.
2. Identify escalation pathway shifts following AI integration.
3. Develop standardized human–AI coordination metrics.
4. Pilot interoperable logging schemas across health systems.
Implementation science funding through NIH, AHRQ, or cooperative agreements (including
CRADAs) would generate the evidence base necessary for proportionate regulation. Contracts,
cooperative agreements, and prize competitions (as referenced in the RFI) are all viable
mechanisms; cooperative agreements may be particularly effective for multi-site logging schema
pilots that require cross-institutional coordination.
VI. Align Reimbursement Incentives with
Documentation Standards
[Responds to RFI Question 2]
This comment focuses primarily on regulation and research levers. However, the documentation
standards proposed above have natural connections to CMS payment policy that would reinforce
adoption:
 CMS could condition New Technology Add-on Payments (NTAPs) or Advanced APM
incentive payments on demonstrated AI governance documentation, including the
logging schema described in Section II.
 Quality measure reporting under the Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS)
could incorporate AI-assisted decision documentation as an Improvement Activity,
7

creating a positive incentive for adoption of accountable logging without mandating
specific technology choices.
 Aligning documentation standards across regulatory and reimbursement frameworks
would reduce the compliance burden for health systems deploying AI, rather than
creating parallel reporting obligations.
These reimbursement connections are noted here to signal their feasibility; detailed payment
policy design is beyond the scope of this comment but warrants dedicated analysis in future
rulemaking.
Conclusion
By clarifying documentation standards and encouraging minimal telemetry requirements, HHS
would create conditions likely to:
 Reduce liability ambiguity
 Support responsible innovation
 Strengthen patient trust
 Provide regulators with objective oversight tools
These measures are proportionate, technically feasible, and aligned with existing regulatory
architecture. Much of this could be pursued under existing authorities through interpretive
guidance, certification updates, and program conditions; however, binding requirements would
likely require notice-and-comment rulemaking or enforceable Conditions of Participation
consistent with Administrative Procedure Act constraints.
