Incentivizing Effective Use of AI in Clinical Care (Q. #2)
HHS can and should incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care. For example, HHS can
use the Quality Payment Program (QPP) and replicate the CEHRT/Promoting
Interoperability (PI) playbook by setting a clear, testable “floor” for what qualifies as
acceptable technology and use and then attaching meaningful scoring and payment
consequences.
Specifically, CMS should do the following:
1. Add a QPP definition for a certifiable class of clinical AI (e.g., “certified clinical AI
module”) by building on the existing CEHRT definitional structure in 42 CFR § 414.1305
and aligning certification mechanics to the ONC Health IT Certification Program authorities
in 45 CFR part 170, subpart E.
2. Update the PI performance category at 42 CFR § 414.1375 to create a scored
objective/measure set for “safe and effective clinical AI use,” limited to certified modules
and requiring auditable basics (e.g., version transparency/audit logs, clinician override,
performance monitoring). This will ensure CMS pays for verified capability rather than
marketing claims.
3. Add AI governance and post-deployment monitoring as high-weight Improvement Activities
via the IA inventory process at 42 CFR § 414.1355.
4. Prevent fragmented incentives by establishing one or more “AI-enabled care” MIPS Value
Pathways (MVPs) under 42 CFR § 414.1365 that bundle the AI expectations across
Quality/Cost/IA/PI instead of scattering optional attestations.
In addition, because the CEHRT approach also flowed into Advanced APM design, CMS should
extend the same logic to Advanced APM alignment by building on the existing Advanced APM
CEHRT criterion at 42 CFR § 414.1415—not by requiring “AI” generically, but by requiring
that any high-impact AI used in clinical decision support or automation within an Advanced
APM context meet certifiable, auditable, monitored standards. This is a direct analogue to how
“use CEHRT” is operationalized today. Together, these steps will allow HHS to incentivize
effective AI use in clinical care using an established program and proven mechanisms.
HHS can also use CMMI Innovation Models to incentivize AI use in clinical care settings. To do
so, HHS should prioritize programmatic design changes that (a) lower the upfront adoption
barrier the way CEHRT programs did, but (b) only “pay for AI” when it produces
validated value and remains safe in real-world use. The fastest way to do this would be to
hardwire an “AI capability track” into high-leverage models. LEAD is explicitly positioned to
launch after ACO REACH concludes at the end of 2026, making it the cleanest successor vehicle
for model-wide expectations (e.g., allowing infrastructure-style payments or enhanced care
management payments for certified AI deployment while gating shared savings/benchmarks on
monitored performance and equity checks).
Similarly, models with longitudinal care management or defined episodes can incorporate AI in
tightly measured ways. For example, Kidney Care Choices (KCC) has been extended through
2027, so CMS can add a standardized option to support certified AI-enabled risk

Novel Issues with AI in Non-Medical Devices (Q. #3)
It is true that use of AI in non-medical devices in clinical care settings may raise novel legal and
implementation issues. AI technologies are rapidly evolving, and gaps are emerging in existing
frameworks governing liability, privacy, safety, and accountability. However, HHS is
well-positioned—through the OneHHS approach articulated in its recent AI Strategy—to lead by
clarifying expectations, enabling innovation, and safeguarding patients. We recommend two
priority actions.
First, HHS should establish a voluntary certification framework for non-medical AI tools,
modeled on ONC’s Health IT Certification Program. This framework would accelerate the
responsible adoption AI tools while reducing the regulatory burden on innovators by providing a
means to evaluate and compare:
• Transparency and documentation.
• Safety monitoring and performance metrics.
• Bias and fairness assessments.
• Data protection and access safeguards.
Second, HHS should issue model contracting and procurement guidance. By building off
OMB Memorandum M-25-22 and the HHS AI Strategy’s procurement guidance, especially the
use of “Cross-Cutting Enablers,” HHS should provide health systems and vendors a consistent
baseline by publishing contractual expectations for:
• Model auditability
• Data rights and ownership
• Security and privacy protections
• Prohibitions on unauthorized model training
• Clear user responsibilities
By taking these steps, HHS can provide the necessary guardrails to enable effective and safe
innovation with AI in non-medical devices for clinical care settings.

Most Promising AI Evaluation Methods (Q. #4)
There are several promising approaches for evaluating AI in non-medical devices and we believe
HHS should support them, especially because the best methods for ensuring safe, reliable
operation with LLM-powered AI solutions are currently subject to research and debate. Further
work is required to devise the best approaches for understanding, testing, and evaluating these
technologies, especially in clinical care settings. HHS should take the lead by establishing
standards and supporting research and investment. Doing so will increase trust, improve
performance, and accelerate adoption and use, thereby bringing benefit to providers and patients.
NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework (RMF) is a useful lens for orienting this work, as its
four functions (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) provide a means of assessing LLM
performance and the risks of inadequate evaluation mechanisms. Another useful lens is the user.
We believe focusing on users will be the best way to ensure evaluation mechanisms align with
desired outcomes. Care providers are likely to be the greatest obstacle to broad adoption in
clinical care settings. Accordingly, HHS should use Human Centered Design (HCD) to position
care providers and patients at the center of evaluation method design and development. In
addition, when promoting adoption, HHS should encourage an incremental, evolutionary
approach that will create successes and build confidence. If done well, this will create a flywheel
effect that builds momentum. Initial successes will build confidence, leading to further adoption
and additional successes. Adoption and use will expand from there.
Promising Evaluation Methods
A variety of AI evaluation methods should be used to support increased adoption and use.
Currently, some of the most promising approaches include the following:
• Employing LLMs as judges.
• Adjusting LLM training approaches penalize hallucinations.
• Using dynamic evaluation methods to adjust benchmarks as LLM capabilities improve, so
that progress is consistent.
• Employing observability with tools like Langfuse.
• Red teaming to discover unanticipated LLM vulnerabilities.
Each of these approaches merits further research and support, especially in the field of clinical
care. We recommend integrating HCD with all these approaches, so that the behavior of AI
solutions is evaluated comprehensively and friction in the human-machine interface is
proactively identified and addressed.
Our concept follows the effective pattern of layering AI evaluations to provide a comprehensive
assessment of a solution’s effectiveness. These layers typically include evaluating LLM
responses in isolation, evaluating artifacts created from those responses by AI solutions (the
human-machine teaming that embeds the LLM), and evaluating the experiences that those
responses, artifacts, and solutions create. This layered approach is especially important in clinical
care. Both patients and care providers will interact with and experience an AI solution. If those
experiences are positive and valuable, then AI can drive improved outcomes for everyone.

Evaluation Metrics
There are several important metrics to consider for evaluation. Accuracy is crucial. Given the
nature of LLMs, accuracy is best broken into components, such as consistency, factual
correctness, and error count. Responses must also be relevant; LLMs should be evaluated on how
well they answered the question and whether they accessed the right data. This necessitates
subjectivity; user feedback on relevance, for example, is essential as people currently assess
relevancy far better than machines. This is one of the primary reasons why we contend it is so
important to employ HCD principles and approaches. Tone and clarity are also important factors
that require human assessment. Was the response easily understood or did it lead to confusion?
Was it at the right reading level? Finally, is the output fact-based and unbiased? As OMB Memo
M-26-04 states, it is important to ensure that AI solutions present truthful, ideological neutral
outputs. Accordingly, evaluation methods must account for these factors.
Supporting the Development of Improved Evaluation Methods
To support the development of improved evaluation mechanisms, several mechanisms are
available. First, HHS ought to develop and provide “gold standard” datasets for training and
evaluation of AI systems in clinical care settings. Those datasets could be created by HHS itself,
or HHS could sponsor their creation through grants, prize competitions, or contract work. Once
standardized data for training and evaluation is in place, progress with evaluation methods will
be more rapid and advance with greater confidence.
In addition, HHS should use SBIRs to encourage development of new evaluation methods and
tools. Focused grants and prize competitions should explore how best to use LLMs-as-judges in
specific clinical care domains. Grants and prizes should also be used to encourage the
development of additional models and datasets well-tuned to medical needs. Grants should be
issued for encouraging the development of automated mechanisms to dynamically keep those
models and datasets up to date as the field advances, a current research focus that HHS ought to
encourage. Grants should also be considered for developing effective red-teaming techniques for
clinical care scenarios and for identifying the most suitable metrics for evaluating AI solutions in
clinical care contexts.
HHS can also support and accelerate adoption by providing guidance and standards. How should
humans be centered in the evaluation process? How can vendors and partners leverage HCD with
confidence to integrate actual patients and clinicians into evaluations? HHS should encourage the
development of standards for these kinds of human-AI interactions in clinical care settings, so
that the industry can integrate human impacts into evaluation methods and practices. Standards
should also include acceptable levels of trust and bias, the use of LLMs-as-judges, and how AI
solutions should handle unanticipated failure modes when their use goes beyond the scope of
existing evaluations. By taking these steps, HHS can have the greatest impact on AI solutions
used for clinical care outside of medical devices.
HCD in Clinical Care AI
Human-Centered Design (HCD) builds reliability and trust in clinical AI by turning human
interactions, recovery from errors, and real-life use situations into clear evaluation goals.
Otherwise, they risk being left as untested assumptions. While model-level checks (like
accuracy, hallucination rates and robustness) show if an AI system can do a task, HCD looks at
whether the system works reliably when used in clinical workflows. For instance, usability

HCD complements other LLM evaluation methods by considering real-world context. While
techniques like LLMs-as-judges or dynamic benchmarking can give scores to outputs, HCD
methods check if those outputs are useful, easy to understand and fit for the specific clinical
decision. For example, two models might get similar scores for factual accuracy, but HCD
testing can reveal that one model offers explanations that are more likely to lead to
misunderstanding of risk among clinicians. Usability testing with scenarios, participatory design
sessions, and practice clinical walkthroughs let evaluators see how AI behaves in real situations
(like during handoffs, interruptions, or with missing information) and make sure safety
features—such as alerts, warnings, or escalation steps—work properly in real usage settings.
HCD also assists effective monitoring after deployment. Instead of just tracking technical results,
HCD-based monitoring looks at how trust in the system changes over time. For example, HCD can
catch whether clinicians start ignoring AI outputs, start trusting them too much, or changing their
work in unexpected ways. These patterns can be tracked with simple feedback tools—like pop-up
prompts in the software, regular surveys linked to specific AI uses, or clinician decisions to
override or correct AI suggestions—and used with technical checks to know when to review the
system. In this way, HCD acts as the final layer of a comprehensive evaluation strategy that helps
ensure AI stays safe, helpful, and trusted as clinical settings, users, and the technology change.
To strengthen responsible clinical AI implementation, HHS should support HCD approaches that
include intentional human‑to‑human engagement as a core component. Direct observation of
clinicians who are using AI tools, paired with one‑on‑one interviews and small-group listening
sessions, provides critical insight into how and why clinicians rely (or not) on these systems in real
workflows. These interactions uncover tacit knowledge, context-specific decision cues, and early
signs of misunderstanding or misuse that technical evaluations cannot detect. Just as importantly,
involving clinicians in this way builds trust and accelerates adoption: they see their concerns
reflected in design decisions, understand how their input influences implementation, and
experience the tools as being shaped with them rather than delivered to them. By supporting HCD
methods that prioritize these human touchpoints, HHS can help ensure clinical AI tools are
implemented responsibly, safely, and in ways that genuinely support the clinicians who use them.
HHS Support for Private Sector Activities (Q. #5)
HHS can support private sector activities like accreditation, certification, industry-driven testing,
and credentialing for clinical AI by adopting “tried-and-true” federal playbooks, such as:
1. Risk-based premarket review and lifecycle oversight (FDA).
2. Reusable third-party assurance that agencies can rely on (FedRAMP).
3. Scaled compliance pathways that mix self-attestation with independent certification for
higher-risk products (FCC).
4. Rapid adoption enabled by clear flexibilities, aligned payment, and temporary enforcement
discretion (COVID-era telehealth).

The common approach across these models is that government sets clear, testable expectations
and then recognizes independent private-sector assurance artifacts, allowing providers and
innovators to scale without every payer or program creating a unique and independent review.
HHS can leverage all four.
First, HHS can mirror the FDA approach by defining risk tiers and evidence expectations for
clinical AI and then formally recognizing qualified private sector entities that generate
standardized “assurance packages”—such as testing results, validation protocols, usability
evidence, and monitoring plans—that HHS programs can trust. FDA already operates multiple,
well-understood pathways for medical devices grounded in the FD&C Act and implemented
through established review practice. Examples include 510(k) clearance, De Novo classification
for novel low–moderate risk devices, and Premarket Approval (PMA) for Class III devices. HHS
could apply the same logic to clinical AI that sits outside traditional device boundaries by
publishing standardized submission templates that cover items such as intended use,
training/validation data provenance, performance limits, human oversight, post-deployment
monitoring, etc. and then recognizing third-party testing/certification programs that produce
evidence in those formats. This would create a predictable, scalable path for “trusted AI”
eligibility in Medicare and Medicaid program use cases.
Second, HHS can emulate FedRAMP by establishing an “assess once, reuse many times” model
for clinical AI assurance, where private assessors perform independent evaluations and HHS can
reuse the results across programs. FedRAMP was established by OMB in 2011 and later codified
in law through the FedRAMP provisions enacted in Public Law 117-263 (NDAA FY2023),
Section 5921. FedRAMP capitalizes on scalable private sector assurance through Third-Party
Assessment Organizations (3PAOs) that are independently assessed by the American
Association for Laboratory Accreditation (A2LA). HHS can take the same approach for clinical
AI by establishing a reusable authorization construct for AI used in clinical care that ensures
standards regarding security, privacy, auditability, monitoring, and performance are met. HHS
can then recognize accredited “clinical AI assessment organizations” and allow providers/payers
to rely on those reusable authorizations to encourage adoption and use at scale.
Third, HHS can borrow from the FCC’s equipment authorization framework by creating two
compliance tracks that support innovation while protecting patients: a lower-friction supplier
attestation path for low-risk tools and a higher-rigor independent certification path for higher-
impact clinical uses. FCC rules in 47 CFR part 2, subpart J, explicitly contemplate equipment
being authorized under Supplier’s Declaration of Conformity or receiving a grant of certification
(including via Telecommunication Certification Bodies), and these authorities are tied to statutory
foundations including 47 U.S.C. § 302a. HHS could employ a similar concept, permitting
streamlined attestation for limited-scope administrative AI (with audit rights and disclosures),
while requiring independent certification (and potentially accredited labs) for AI that materially
influences diagnosis, triage, treatment, or autonomous clinical actions. Adopting this approach
would encourage consistency in labels, logs, and change-management disclosures so that certified
tools could be regularly evaluated to ensure they remain trustworthy as they evolve.

Widening Opportunities through Interoperability (Q. #8)
Enhanced interoperability will widen market opportunities, advance clinical research, and
accelerate the development and employment of AI for clinical care. AI use in healthcare will
only be as effective as the consistency, quality, and portability of the data AI systems rely upon.
Today, clinical data remains fragmented across EHR platforms, public health systems, research
environments, claims systems, and social service data repositories, limiting the ability to create
scalable, generalizable AI solutions that can operate reliably across populations and care settings.
Additionally, data is often not “AI ready,” resulting unreliable, unsafe, biased, and non-portable
outputs that undermine clinical trust and regulatory readiness.
Data Types
The greatest impact from interoperability will come from improving the clinical and contextual
data used by AI systems and solutions. This includes EHR data (diagnosis, medications, lab
results, etc.), imaging and diagnostics (DICOM) data, claims and utilization data, public health
and surveillance data, genomic data, data from community resources, and data from personal
devices. Incorporating unstructured clinical narratives and administrative metadata into available
datasets will further strengthen AI’s ability to draw valuable inferences and support clinical care
delivery. These data sources should be longitudinally linked so that AI can support predictive
modeling, clinical decision support, assessments of population health, early interventions, and
research at scale while enabling multimodal benchmarking and reproducible evaluations across
representative cohorts.
Data Standards
Standards are essential for accelerating adoption and use of AI. We recommend consistent, broad
adoption of HL7, FHIR, USCDI, DICOM, LOINC, and SNOMED standards to allow AI
developers to build solutions that can operate across health systems and platforms without
extensive data transformation. API-driven architectures will further allow AI tools to be
embedded directly into clinical workflows rather than serving as standalone applications. These
standards allow AI solution providers to build solutions once and deploy them across multiple
EHRs, provider networks, research institutions, and public health agencies without costly data
transformation for each environment. Effective use of standards will also reduce the need for
custom integrations that would otherwise increase cost, limit competition, and slow innovation.

Market Impact
Because AI effectiveness, safety, and reliability depend directly on the quality and structure of
datasets used for solution development, validation, and deployment, interoperable data
environments enable clinicians and researchers to confidently use AI tools in diagnostics,
pediatric oncology, chronic disease research, and other high‑stakes areas. When data is poorly
organized, inconsistent, or incomplete, predictions become less accurate and clinical
decision‑making suffers. Interoperable systems directly address this by allowing normalization
and semantic alignment across data sources, thereby maintaining strong data lineage and
provenance and supporting transparent documentation. Interoperability also strengthens
governance by enabling privacy‑preserving de‑identification, bias detection across diverse
populations, and responsible data sharing. These elements streamline multi‑omic and clinical
data integration, support reproducible science, and ultimately expand opportunities for
cross‑institution research. Our recommended approach aligns with the HHS AI Strategy,
especially the idea of cross-cutting enablers, as it will encourage clinical deployment of AI in
ways that are aligned with federal strategies to reduce duplicative systems and strengthen
enterprise-wide coordination.
Benchmarking
Enhanced interoperability will enable the creation and use of “gold standard” benchmark datasets
that improve model validation across representative cohorts and care settings. We recommend
the government either work to build such benchmark datasets or sponsor their development by
establishing standards for development and evaluation of AI solutions in clinical care settings.
HHS can ensure these datasets promote interoperability by requiring standardized structures,
robust metadata, and privacy-preserving pipelines. This will ensure interoperability through by
reducing model fragmentation and will prevent AI systems from being trained on narrow
datasets that limit generalizability or introduce undesirable biases. By ensuring the use of
consistent and validated datasets, HHS can increase the market viability for AI tools, accelerate
evaluation and regulatory readiness, and strengthen confidence in real-world clinical use.
Specific Areas of AI Research to Prioritize (Q. #10)
AI’s true value will only be appreciated when it effectively augments human providers within
clinical workflows. Today, most healthcare AI research focuses on algorithm accuracy in

embracing HCD. Two specific research areas stand out.
