Detailed Responses to RFI Questions
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and
its adoption and use in clinical care?
• Despite progress through FHIR and related ONC initiatives, access to complete, high-quality,
standards-based data across EHRs, payer systems, and ancillary sources remains inconsistent.
AI systems rely on longitudinal, well-structured datasets, and fragmentation increases
development cost, limits generalizability, and slows deployment. Model Context Protocol (MCP)
integration can accelerate adoption by enabling secure access to clinical data across client
environments without requiring data migration or format standardization.
• Fee-for-service reimbursement models do not always incentivize technologies to
reduce utilization, shorten administrative cycle times, or lower the total cost of care. Therefore,
AI tools that improve efficiency or prevent unnecessary services may lack a direct
reimbursement pathway.
• Public concern regarding algorithmic bias, lack of transparency, and the risk of automation-
driven denials, particularly in prior authorization, creates skepticism and slows adoption even
where safeguards exist. One approach is to avoid training proprietary AI models
independently and instead use industry-standard foundation models (Anthropic Claude,
Google Gemini) and provide clinical context through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) via
MCP-connected data sources and unstructured document processing. This can reduce the
introduction of training data bias. The bias concerns can be limited to prompt injection
(malicious input manipulation), cross-tenant data access, decision confidence fairness, and RAG
context completeness (ensuring the right clinical evidence is retrieved for all patient types).
• Ambiguity in compliance frameworks, particularly around HIPAA and state-specific privacy
regulations, can hinder adoption. Implementing comprehensive HIPAA Technical Safeguards (45
CFR § 164.312) could streamline adoption. This should include: Access Control with 3-tier
capability token system (24-hour job tokens, 1-hour agent tokens with automatic expiration),
Audit Controls with 365-day CloudWatch retention and 7-year archive capability, Integrity
Controls with client-specific KMS encryption, Person/Entity Authentication via AWS Cognito
OAuth2, and Transmission Security with TLS 1.3. Our audit framework generates 9 report types
(daily/monthly/quarterly/annual/on demand), providing concrete evidence of compliance.

• Uncertainty regarding legal frameworks and governance, particularly relating to liability
allocation, indemnification, and the documentation of human oversight, delays contracting and
implementation processes, especially for AI applications within utilization management or
clinical decision support integrated into electronic health records (EHRs). It is critical to have
human-in-the-loop governance at multiple levels, including decision separation, confidence-
based routing, progressive automation with safeguards, and administrative controls.
Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care, and why? What HHS regulations,
policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical
care? Please provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
• HHS should prioritize payment models that reward AI-enabled reductions in administrative
burden, improvements in the timeliness of care, and measurable improvements in outcomes.
Introduce value-based incentives for AI-enabled solutions and documentation tools that
demonstrate reduced administrative burden, costs, and improved outcomes. Additionally, HHS
should simplify approval pathways for AI-driven utilization management tools, provided they
maintain clinical oversight by licensed professionals.
• HHS policy should clearly distinguish between AI that assists clinicians or reviewers and AI
that autonomously determines coverage or clinical actions. Existing CMS guidance stating that
AI may support but not replace individualized medical necessity determinations should be
codified.

Question 3: For non-medical devices, we understand that the use of AI in clinical care may raise
novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability
structures (including liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist, and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
• We recommend establishing predictable regulatory guidance that balances innovation with
patient safety. When AI informs but does not independently make clinical
or utilization management decisions, existing legal frameworks do not clearly address:
o Allocation of liability when AI recommendations are followed or overridden
o Responsibility for performance drift, bias, or misclassification
o Documentation standards demonstrating meaningful human review
• HHS could play a constructive role by:
o Issuing guidance on minimum governance expectations for clinical AI, including
human-in-the-loop workflows, audit trails, and documentation requirements.
o Providing optional model contract language addressing indemnification and liability
for AI-assisted clinical use cases.
o Clarifying permissible uses and disclosures under HIPAA for AI training, monitoring,
and safety auditing, including recommended de-identification practices.
Question 4: For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre-
and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered
evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so, which
mechanisms would be most impactful (e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or
prize competitions)?
• Promising evaluation methods and metrics include: bias and fairness testing using parity
metrics; robustness testing with documentation standards; post-deployment continuous
performance surveillance of FHIR loads and connections; and payer-linked outcomes
(appeal rates, overturn rates, adverse events) for AI in prior authorization and care
coordination.
• HHS should support funding public-private development, such as shared datasets
(clinical and claims), evaluation protocols, and independent measurement teams to
prioritize tools, ambient documentation, prior authorization, and health care navigation.
We recommend establishing grants for model registries and audit tools that use
standardized metric schemas and reporting. HHS can also support competitions that
incentivize reproducible processes that reduce bias and improve clinician trust, with
performance-based awards.

Question 5: How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification,
industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical
care?
• HHS should support a voluntary, risk-tiered assurance framework aligned with ONC
certification and TEFCA. HHS should also recognize the distinction between AI platforms
that train proprietary models and those that orchestrate industry foundation models.
• HHS should encourage third-party accreditation for prior authorization using AI and
clinical documentation using AI (scribes), with audit trails that demonstrate
human-in-the-loop governance, explainability, and outcomes (timeliness, overturn
rates, adverse events).
• Codify payer expectations and guardrails for AI use in coverage decisions and ensure
the architecture embeds key guardrails.
• Funding AI literacy and governance training for clinicians, medical directors,
and utilization management professionals. Training curricula could incorporate
anonymized examples to teach clinicians how to interpret AI confidence scores,
recognize performance drift, and exercise effective oversight.
Question 6: Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and
cost expectations, and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have
the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights into quality, and help
reduce costs?
• Ambient clinical documentation tools have met expectations by reducing clinician
burnout, preserving workforce capacity, and improving documentation quality.
• AI deployment often lags expectations due to integration complexity, governance
requirements, and validation burden.
• AI tools with High-Potential include prior authorization orchestration with real-time
data retrieval and human override, population-level risk navigation integrating social
drivers and benefits data, and explainable quality signal extraction beyond traditional
Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set (HEDIS) measures.
Question 7: Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care
organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the
primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
• Roles that are key influencers and decision makers among health plans include:
o Chief Medical Officer/Medical Directors (clinical sponsorship, guardrails).
o Chief Information Officers and Clinical Informatics (workflow integration, EHR
alignment).

o Utilization Management/Case Management Leaders for payer-side adoption in
prior authorization, concurrent review, and care coordination.
o Compliance/Privacy/Legal Officers.
• Primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI include
o Interoperability friction, such as fragmented EHRs, a lack of standardized APIs,
and mixed protocols.
o Payment uncertainty, including the fact that fee-for-service rarely rewards
deflationary technology, and coverage decisions could be slow.
o Site-specific performance drift and bias audits, which could lead to validation
and monitoring burdens.
o Workforce readiness and change management are impacted by clinician trust,
documentation standards, and governance.
Question 8: Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research,
and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data types, data
standards, and benchmarking tools.
• FHIR to enable plug and play across exchanges for clinical, claims, and prior
authorization datasets.
• Claims and clinical linkage for outcomes and total costs supported by AI tools (LOS,
readmissions, adverse events, equity impacts).
• Benchmarking tools such as national model registries with aligned metrics for pre- and
post-deployment data.
Question 9: What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see
addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and
caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
• Some of the challenges that patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the
adoption and use of AI include:
o Faster access to care by reducing prior authorization delays and having real-time
decisions, as well as transparent reasons for determinations.
o Fewer diagnostic delays because of AI triage and timely routing.
o Increasing clinician presence: AI would reduce time spent on charting and
increase time spent listening to ambient documentation.
• Some of the concerns that patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the
adoption and use of AI include:
o Patients express concern about algorithmic bias and unequal denials, particularly
in utilization management.
o There is significant concern about batch denials caused by automation.
o Sharing data over networks raises privacy and trust concerns.

Question 10: Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the
adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
a. Are there published findings about the impact of adopted AI tools and their use in
clinical care?
b. How does the literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI as part
of clinical care?
• The specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize include:
o Implementation science for AI-enabled prior authorization
o Real-world performance drift and monitoring methodologies
o Total cost of ownership and payment models for deflationary technologies
o Explainable quality measures linked to clinical outcomes
