RE: Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as
Part of Clinical Care
Dear Assistant Secretary Keane:
On behalf of the Heart Failure Society of America (HFSA), we appreciate the opportunity to provide
input in response to the request for information (RFI) on how the Department of Health and
Human Services (HHS) can help shape the development, evaluation, and deployment of artificial
intelligence (AI) in clinical care. HFSA is a multidisciplinary organization working to improve and
expand heart failure care through collaboration, education, research, innovation, and advocacy.
HFSA members include physicians, scientists, nurses, nurse practitioners, pharmacists, and
patients.
Through this RFI, HHS seeks feedback on how regulations, reimbursement policies, and research
and development efforts can be shaped to better support the rapid adoption and effective use of
AI in clinical care, while fostering public trust and aligning with federal priorities to enhance
productivity, reduce burden, lower health care costs, and improve health outcomes.
Heart failure offers a compelling use case for the responsible adoption of AI in clinical care. It is
a common, resource-intensive chronic condition that is managed across complex, longitudinal
care pathways and workflows. Approximately 6.7 million Americans over the age of 20 are
currently living with heart failure and the prevalence is expected to increase substantially over
the coming decades, rising to 11.4 million by 2050. The lifetime risk of developing heart failure
is 24%, meaning approximately one in four individuals will develop the condition. In 2022, heart
failure was a contributing cause in more than 425,000 deaths, accounting for 45% of all
cardiovascular deaths nationwide.1
Significant gaps, variation, and disparities persist in the use of guideline-recommended therapies
for heart failure. Fewer than one in four eligible patients with heart failure with reduced ejection
fraction receive comprehensive quadruple guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), despite
clear evidence of its benefit. Broader and more consistent implementation of these therapies
could have a profound impact on outcomes, with estimates suggesting that optimal uptake could
prevent approximately 1.19 million deaths globally each year.2
This creates clear opportunities for AI to improve operational efficiency, reduce clinician burden,
and increase reliability of delivering evidence-based care. This potential is reflected in a recent
research and development initiative offered through HHS’ Advanced Research Projects Agency for
Health (ARPA-H), which seeks to advance agentic AI technologies as “clinician extenders.” Through

At the same time, heart failure care is inherently high risk. Poorly governed or inadequately
evaluated AI tools could cause patient harm through preventable hospitalizations, medication-
related adverse events, or delayed escalation of care. Accordingly, the adoption of AI in clinical
care must be accompanied by robust governance, rigorous prospective evaluation, and
continuous post-deployment monitoring to ensure safety, effectiveness, and equity across patient
populations.
Recommended Principles
To ensure that AI is used appropriately in clinical care, HFSA recommends that HHS consider the
following principles when establishing policies to support the deployment of AI technologies:
• Establish risk-proportionate clinician governance and accountability for AI used in
clinical care. AI tools that inform diagnoses, triage, medication titration, escalation
decisions, or discharge planning should be deployed only with clear clinical ownership
and defined human oversight. HHS should promote governance expectations that are
proportionate to a tool’s clinical impact, including an explicit intended-use statement,
documented workflows for clinician override, and auditable logs of AI outputs and
clinician actions. These measures clarify accountability, preserve appropriate clinical
judgment, and support public trust while enabling innovation.
• AI-enabled care should be designed to address the full heart failure continuum. AI
initiatives in heart failure should be grounded in an understanding of heart failure as a
continuum. Approximately one-third of U.S. adults are at risk for heart failure (Stage A)
and nearly 24-34% have pre-heart failure (Stage B). While prevalence of stage C2D and
Stage D heart failure requires further examination, it has been estimated that 5-7% of
patients with symptomatic heart failure have advanced heart failure. Rising rates of
obesity diabetes, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease are accelerating progression
across this continuum. 3 Despite clear evidence-based therapies, substantial gaps,
variations, and disparities in use of guideline-recommended therapies persist, as noted
above. Effective AI must support surveillance, prevention, early detection, treatment
optimization, and advanced care across all stages of heart failure.
• Align reimbursement and federal incentives to support implementation, monitoring,
and value-based adoption of AI-enabled care. Many clinically meaningful AI tools require
substantial upfront investment in integration, workflow redesign, training, and ongoing
surveillance. These costs are often difficult for providers to absorb without a reliable
reimbursement pathway. HHS, largely through the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid
Services (CMS), should prioritize payment approaches that reward demonstrated clinical
utility and burden reduction, while avoiding per-alert or per-use incentives that may drive
volume without value. Time-limited implementation support and demonstration models
for AI-enabled care pathways would reduce adoption friction while promoting responsible
scale. CMS should look to offer incentives to providers that reward safe deployment of AI.
• Shift evaluation expectations from retrospective accuracy to prospective clinical
utility, safety, and equity in real workflows. HHS should invest in research evaluating
whether and how AI improves outcomes and reduces clinician burden without introducing
new harms in the real world. Retrospective performance metrics alone are insufficient to
justify clinical deployment. Evaluation frameworks should incorporate human factors

clinically meaningful safety endpoints. HHS can accelerate responsible adoption by
supporting pragmatic, multi-site randomized trials and post-deployment monitoring that

• Establish comprehensive lifecycle evaluation framework for AI tools. Appropriate HHS
authorities should oversee pre-deployment evaluation and lifecycle regulation of AI tools
that meet criteria for Software as a Medical Device, including requirements for clinical
validation, transparency, and post-market performance monitoring to address model drift
and safety risks in heart failure populations. In particular, the Assistant Secretary for
Technology Policy/Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology
(ASTP/ONC) should establish interoperability and auditability standards to ensure AI tools
integrate reliably with electronic health records and support clinician interpretability and
governance. CMS should also align reimbursement and quality measurement approaches
with broader CMS initiatives, such as the Ambulatory Specialty Model and the Advancing
Chronic Care with Effective, Scalable Solutions Model, to support coordinated adoption of
high-value AI-enabled care.

responsibly accelerate adoption of AI in clinical care. We encourage HHS to maintain ongoing
engagement with representative clinical experts and patient populations in the development of
AI-related policies to ensure that resulting recommendations are accurate, precise, and grounded
in real-world clinical practice. If you have questions or require additional information, please
contact Sue Ramthun at sramthun@hfsa.org.
