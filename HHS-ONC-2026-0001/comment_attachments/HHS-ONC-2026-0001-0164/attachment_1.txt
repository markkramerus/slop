Dear Dr. Keane,
On behalf of the HIMSS Electronic Health Record (EHR) Association, we value the opportunity to submit
comments in response to the Request for Information (RFI) on actions the U.S. Department of Health
and Human Services (HHS) can take to accelerate the adoption and use of artificial intelligence (AI) in
clinical care.
Established in 2004, the EHR Association is comprised of nearly 30 member companies that supply the
vast majority of EHRs used by hospitals, physician practices, and post-acute care environments across
the United States. This gives us a comprehensive view of how AI-enabled tools are developing and being
integrated into real-world clinical workflows, and this is the lens through which we respond to the RFI’s
questions below.
We remain committed to supporting safe healthcare delivery, fostering continued innovation, and
operating with high integrity for EHR users and their patients. Our members are enthusiastic about the
potential for AI in healthcare technology, with reasonable guardrails in place to protect patients and
create a federal baseline for AI tools in certified health IT. We appreciate the chance this RFI presents to
harmonize policies across HHS, avoiding conflicting obligations and reducing implementation burden.
We also support efforts to modernize reimbursement models to reward clinically valuable AI that
improves outcomes, reduces burden, and lowers the total cost of care.
We have developed the following core guiding principles for EHR Association member organizations to
guide the design, development, and deployment of AI solutions; these inform our comments to the
specific questions raised in the RFI:

● Patient Safety and Clinical Efficacy: We support the adoption of a national, risk-based
governance framework that scales requirements (e.g., committees, processes) to
organizational size and AI risk profile. AI must meet standards of safety, reliability, and
clinical effectiveness commensurate with risk.
● Clinician Oversight and Accountability: AI should be designed and incorporated within EHRs
and other health IT to augment informed clinical judgment, not be a substitute for a licensed
clinical professional exercising judgment in a particular situation. Clinicians must be able to
contextualize decisions or override outputs as appropriate. Regulations should affirm
clinician primacy and permit context-driven overrides without mandating one-size-fits-all
technical override functions for all AI modalities.
● Transparency and Explainability: Clear information about intended use, data provenance,
performance characteristics, limitations, and monitoring responsibilities should be available
for end users.
● Privacy and Security: Protection of identifiable health information compliant with not only
HIPAA but also best practices for cybersecurity and data minimization.
● Interoperability and Data Quality: Adoption of standards-based data formats,
provenance/traceability, and benchmarking to support safe, scalable deployment.
● Human-Centered Design and Usability: Workflow-aware design that reduces burden and
supports safe use in context.
● Lifecycle Risk Management: Pre- and post-deployment evaluation, performance
monitoring, and continuous improvement.
Responses to Specific RFI Questions
RFI Question 1 — Biggest barriers to private-sector AI innovation and adoption in clinical care
From the vantage point of EHR developers serving hospitals, ambulatory practices, and post-acute
providers nationwide, the most significant barriers to AI innovation and adoption fall into five
interconnected areas: regulatory fragmentation and uncertainty, data quality and access constraints,
governance burdens that are not scaled to risk, misaligned payment incentives and unclear ROI, and
workforce and workflow readiness.
First, providers and developers face ambiguity about how existing federal frameworks apply to non-
device clinical AI versus FDA-regulated Software-as-a-Medical Device (SaMD), creating hesitation and
elongated timelines for responsible deployment. A forward-leaning, harmonized HHS posture,
coordinated across ASTP/ONC, FDA, CMS, and related offices, would materially reduce uncertainty,
accelerate implementation, and promote trust, consistent with the RFI’s objective of establishing a
predictable, proportionate regulatory approach that both protects patients and enables innovation. The
FDA guidance issued on January 6, 2026, on Clinical Decision Support Software is a helpful step toward
greater certainty in this area, but we need to revisit it as AI technologies mature.
2

Second, even when use cases are clinically compelling, the foundation of data elements needed to safely
build, validate, and monitor AI can sometimes be inconsistent, incomplete, or insufficiently
representative of both diverse populations and care settings. The RFI’s call to align federal levers so that
AI is deployed in ways that enhance productivity, reduce burden, lower costs, and improve outcomes
will depend on trustworthy, interoperable health data, ongoing oversight and retraining, and a regular
cadence of standards enhancement and enforcement. To help remedy these issues, HHS could explore
options for supplying rich, purpose-specific (and de-identified) data sets that the industry could rely on
for high quality training and evaluation.
Third, many smaller organizations and rural providers face governance expectations in proposed
legislation/frameworks that risk being overly burdensome. The EHR Association supports multi-
disciplinary oversight but has cautioned that requiring rigid governance structures that do not reflect the
specific needs of a given organization, costly external validation, and any too-specific timeline could
impose disproportionate burdens on resource-constrained settings, as well as lower-risk AI functions
that do not require the same scrutiny. We recommend establishing a tiered governance model that is
scaled to the organization's type, the tool’s intended use, and its risk profile, so that safety, fairness, and
transparency are upheld without creating barriers that inadvertently discourage the adoption of
beneficial technologies. A digital divide already exists specific to health IT between larger, more
resource-rich healthcare systems and providers working in smaller ambulatory practices. We believe
these smaller practices can measurably benefit from these technologies; supporting their adoption of
these tools should be a priority.
Fourth, increased AI adoption would be best supported through aligned payment policies that reward
clinical value, avoid burden imposition, and track total cost-of-care improvements. The RFI recognizes
that legacy fee-for-service regimes frequently do not include mechanisms that incite adoption of
innovative technologies, therefore slowing responsible uptake; modernized payment mechanisms that
reward validated outcomes would catalyze provider demand and responsible scale.
Finally, workforce readiness remains a practical factor for consideration, as it does for any innovative
but disruptive tool. For adoption of such a technology to be successful, clinicians and their staff need
tools that smoothly fit within EHR-enabled workflows, and they also rely on both training offerings and
organizational governance policies that allow them to confidently assume any associated risk. Provider
organizations and our collective clients have made it clear that there is a critical need to preserve
independent clinical judgment, including the ability to contextualize or override AI outputs. This aligns
with positions put forward by the EHR Association, which state that AI should augment, not replace,
clinician expertise. These conditions are essential to building trust, ensuring safety, and supporting
continuous improvement at the point of care.
RFI Question 2: Priority Regulatory, Payment, and Programmatic Changes
From the perspective of the EHR Association, the most impactful actions HHS can take to accelerate
responsible AI adoption involve establishing a clear, harmonized, and risk-based federal framework that
allows developers and providers to innovate with confidence while ensuring that patient safety, clinical
judgment, and system transparency remain foundational. The RFI underscores HHS’s intent to create a
regulatory posture that is predictable, proportionate to risk, and aligned with broader federal strategies
to accelerate AI adoption in healthcare, reduce uncertainty, and promote trust.
3

In this context, we believe a formal federal framework specifically tailored to non-device clinical AI is
urgently needed. Such a framework should articulate expectations for documentation, transparency,
governance, and lifecycle monitoring while explicitly scaling requirements to the risk profile of the AI
tool and the size and capacity of the implementing organization. Overly prescriptive governance
structures can inadvertently burden smaller providers, and low-risk AI-supported tools, such as those
focused on simplifying administrative tasks or reducing the burden on support staff, should be right-
sized to support safe, equitable adoption. Given that many organizations have expressed a strong
interest in adopting those low-risk models, any steps HHS can take within its regulatory framework to
clearly differentiate between high- and low-risk AI implementations, with corresponding tiers of
regulatory requirements, would be welcome.
A critical priority is aligning and harmonizing federal oversight across ASTP/ONC, FDA, CMS, and OCR, as
well as attempting to harmonize the expanding patchwork of state laws regulating AI development and
use. The HHS RFI itself acknowledges the need for a “forward-leaning, industry-supportive approach”
that leverages regulation, reimbursement, and research to create clarity and accelerate adoption. Such
an approach would go a long way toward alleviating the uncertainty that, in some cases, is creating
hesitancy among both software developers and users.
The EHR Association strongly supports establishing shared definitions, common documentation artifacts,
and coordinated expectations across agencies, particularly for intended-use statements, required
performance information, and post-deployment monitoring responsibilities. Harmonization will
significantly reduce duplicative compliance activities and help align clinical workflow integration, safety
oversight, and privacy protections under a coherent national policy framework.
Payment modernization is also essential to ensuring that the value created by clinically validated AI tools
is recognized and rewarded. The EHR Association encourages CMS to explore targeted add-on
payments, AI-specific quality program credits, and pathways within alternative payment models (APMs)
to support deployment of AI tools that improve clinical outcomes, reduce administrative burden, or
enhance operational efficiency. Without clear, predictable financial signals, even highly effective tools
may struggle to gain traction among financially constrained or operationally stretched providers.
Finally, the EHR Association recommends that HHS increase federal investment in benchmarking
datasets, reference implementations, and safety testbeds to improve model transparency and external
validation. Investments in shared safety testbeds and common reporting templates would further
support trusted implementation and post-deployment monitoring across the industry.
Taken together, these regulatory, payment, and programmatic actions would significantly advance the
responsible adoption of AI in clinical care while protecting patient safety, ensuring equitable application
of data benefits to all patients, and rewarding clinician adoption of innovative technologies.
RFI Question 3: Novel Legal and Implementation Issues for Non-Medical-Device AI and HHS’s
Role
From the EHR Association’s perspective, the rapid expansion of AI tools that influence clinical care (but
do not fall under FDA regulation as medical devices) introduces a series of novel legal and operational
challenges that require thoughtful federal guidance. A central issue is the allocation of liability among
4

developers, implementers, and clinicians when AI-generated recommendations inform care decisions.
Because these tools usually operate in advisory or assistive roles and do not constitute regulated
medical devices, the boundaries of responsibility can be ambiguous. To support safe deployment and
reduce legal uncertainty, the industry would benefit from model “duty of care” frameworks and safe-
harbor practices for documenting AI-informed clinical decisions, recognizing that the ultimate
responsibility for patient care ultimately rests with the authorizing clinician. Such structures would help
ensure that accountability aligns with real-world roles and capabilities while preserving clinicians’
independent judgment.
Another challenge arises in the contracting and indemnification frameworks needed for adaptive or
continuously learning AI systems. Traditional contracting models do not adequately address issues such
as performance warranties, update obligations, or the allocation of risks associated with model drift and
re-training. There is work necessary for the industry to collectively identify best practices that lay out
reasonable expectations for developers, implementers, and users, including shared responsibilities for
updates, logic and data transparency, ongoing training and performance monitoring.
Non-device AI systems also pose novel privacy and security complexities and challenges for
implementers to consider. While the applicability of and obligations under HIPAA Privacy and Security
Rules are clear and consistent with traditional software, the strategies implementers should deploy to
ensure compliance with those obligations are not always so clear. The industry as a whole could benefit
greatly from thorough guidance on best practices for privacy and security considerations and strategies
coming out of HHS in collaboration with federal partners, such as NIST.
Finally, the rise of AI-augmented clinical workflows raises workforce, credentialing, and scope-of-
practice considerations. As clinicians integrate AI outputs into care decisions, organizations need to
implement policies that clarify training, competency, and oversight expectations (and should make
those expectations clear to their software partners as well). These issues also intersect with CMS
Conditions of Participation, clinical quality measurement programs, and HRSA workforce development
initiatives, where HHS can play a direct role in ensuring both resources are sufficient and expectations
are clear. We also recommend that HHS consider investments in workforce training programs specific to
AI safety and efficiency.
Taken together, these legal and operational complexities underscore the importance of coordinated
federal leadership. By offering clear, consistent guidance on liability, contracting, privacy, and workforce
readiness, HHS can significantly reduce uncertainty, support safe and equitable adoption, and build the
foundation for responsible use of non-device AI tools across the healthcare ecosystem.
RFI Question 4: Promising evaluation methods for non-medical device AI and how HHS can
support them
Non-medical device AI tools benefit from evaluation methods that combine traditional validation with
real-world clinical usability and workflow safety assessments. While the industry is nascent with best
practices still evolving, it is logical that structured intended-use analysis, external validation across
representative patient populations, and human-factors testing in live or simulated EHR workflows are all
steps to consider as stakeholders determine their optimal processes. Post-deployment, ongoing
5

monitoring for calibration, drift, fairness impacts, alert fatigue, and patient outcomes are all beneficial in
ensuring a safe deployment of health-related AI.
Because these evaluation practices vary in maturity across the industry, HHS could add value by funding
the development of common benchmarks, publishing reporting templates for industry consideration,
and supporting shared voluntary testbeds that would enable transparent, replicable assessment across
organizations and care settings.
RFI Question 5: Supporting private-sector accreditation, certification, and testing
Because there is no federal framework for AI development and use currently in place, many private-
sector EHR developers have already taken substantial steps to establish internal governance, testing,
and safety protocols. In some cases, our customers have communicated that they also require
safeguards and data testing before proceeding with adopting AI tools and functionality.
We believe the private sector will play an active role in the conversation specific to ideas about
voluntary accreditation, certification, and testing mechanisms for clinical AI. However, standardizing
expectations for model audit trails will help ensure that AI-supported decisions are consistent in key
ways across the industry, whether applied by EHR developers or independent AI model developers
without certified EHRs on the market. Such consistency would likely lead to increased confidence in AI
solutions and a more consistent framework for comparing different AI deployments across the
healthcare industry for key metrics of safety, accuracy, and efficiency.
RFI Question 6: Where AI has met or fallen short, and high-impact opportunities
We believe it is possible to accelerate provider adoption of AI innovations if the capabilities are paired
with payment models that reward validated clinical benefit.
RFI Question 7: Roles influencing adoption and primary administrative hurdles
Decisions about whether and how to adopt AI tools are influenced by a complex array of stakeholders
within healthcare organizations, including clinical leadership, IT and EHR teams, quality and safety
officers, compliance, and privacy teams, and financial or operational executives. Conversely, in smaller
organizations, the decision-making authority may be vested in a single individual or a small cohort of
clinicians. Despite interest, adoption is sometimes slowed by administrative hurdles, such as lengthy
procurement processes, security reviews, and uncertainty about what documentation would be
required of governance structures must be established.
RFI Question 8: Interoperability enhancements to widen opportunities
The EHR Association emphasizes that progress in AI adoption is directly linked to the quality and
consistency of interoperability across the health IT ecosystem. AI tools depend on accessibility to
structured, standards-based data to generate reliable and equitable predictions, but that must be
coupled with data provenance transparency and audit trails that capture data use and exchange for
purposes of ensuring sufficient monitoring capability. This would contribute to the expansion of
opportunities for innovation and improve the reliability of external validation efforts. Investments in
6

national benchmarking datasets and standardized evaluation frameworks would further aid developers,
researchers, and providers in assessing model performance across diverse populations and
environments.
RFI Question 9: Patient and caregiver priorities and concerns
Patients and caregivers increasingly expect AI to be part of their care process, but they also sometimes
raise concerns about privacy, bias, and accountability when AI-related errors occur. Additionally, some
inquire about opting out of AI use in their care, and all are questions that healthcare providers must be
prepared to address to maintain public trust. Providers should provide clear, accessible communication
materials that help patients understand when and how AI is used in their care and what options they
have to ask questions, seek clarification, or raise concerns. HHS could also help by providing guidance on
plain-language communications about AI use for providers to help inform patients about AI use, support
opt-in/opt-out choices where feasible, and provide clear pathways for questions, appeals, and
remediation.
RFI Question 10: Priority areas for AI research and the literature on costs/benefits
The EHR Association encourages HHS to prioritize research efforts that address the elements most
critical to safe and equitable AI adoption: data and logic transparency, human-centered usability, real-
world safety evaluation, and performance monitoring. We also support investment in pragmatic trial
designs and implementation science approaches that measure AI’s true impact on outcomes, clinician
workload, equitable application of the technology, and total cost of care. Because cost-benefit analyses
and burden-reduction measures are still emerging fields in AI evaluation, federally supported research in
this area could help inform payment reform specific to AI use.
Conclusion
We appreciate HHS’s leadership in evaluating the best path to a forward-leaning, industry-supportive
approach to regulating healthcare AI. By affirming clinician primacy, adopting scalable risk-based
governance, enabling legitimate quality and safety uses of data, and harmonizing expectations across
federal programs, HHS can accelerate responsible AI adoption that improves outcomes, reduces clinician
and staff burden, and helps constrain systemic costs.
The EHR Association stands ready to collaborate on and support the safe, effective, and equitable
deployment of AI in health information technologies and across the healthcare system.
