Dear Mr. Posnack and the ASTP/ONC Review Team,
Thank you for the opportunity to provide feedback on the Department's approach to
accelerating the safe adoption of AI in clinical care. I am commenting on the use of AI as an
active agent in physical care—such as in autonomous surgical robotics, closed-loop
infusion pumps, and automated bio-synthesis—I want to offer a perspective on the
physical safety limits of software-only guardrails.
My primary feedback regarding the deployment of AI in physical clinical care is that
software safety models, while necessary, are inherently probabilistic and subject to
execution latency, kernel panics, and model deception. In high-consequence clinical
environments, I recommend that HHS establish safety expectations rooted in physical
hardware, rather than relying solely on software alignment.
Regarding Question 3, which touches on the novel legal and implementation issues relating
to liability and accountability, the introduction of agentic AI creates a unique forensic
challenge. Traditional medical liability relies on being able to audit what went wrong.
However, if an AI-driven medical device acts unpredictably, the software logs it generates
can be unreliable or spoofed if the system's root environment is compromised.
To resolve this accountability gap, HHS should encourage the adoption of hardware-
resident event logging. If a device experiences a safety violation that results in a shutdown
or a severed power connection, that event should generate a cryptographically signed
record at the hardware level—completely independent of the AI's operating system. This
acts as a true, unalterable "black box" for malpractice adjusters, hospital compliance
boards, and the FDA, proving the physical state of the device regardless of what the
software logs claim.
In response to Question 4 concerning AI evaluation methods and metrics, I suggest the
Department rethink how it measures safety responsiveness. Currently, many robustness
metrics focus on software response times. In physical care, such as robotic surgery, a
software loop taking 20 to 50 milliseconds to recognize an error and issue a stop command
can result in unintended tissue damage. HHS evaluation methods for physical AI tools
should transition toward measuring "deterministic arrest windows." For instance, systems

should be evaluated on their ability to physically guarantee a zero-energy state at the
actuator in under one or two milliseconds. Achieving this practically requires physical
hardware interlocks (like a series interrupter on the power line) rather than just software-
based stops.
Finally, regarding Question 5 and how HHS can best support private sector accreditation
and certification, I believe the greatest impact would come from establishing baseline
hardware states for clinical AI. One of the most effective ways to mitigate risk is to require
that devices capable of causing physical harm default to a de-energized, normally-dark
state. For example, power to drug-infusion pumps or surgical drives should be physically
isolated until an independent hardware gate verifies the integrity of the clinical instruction
set.
This concept extends to a variety of clinical applications. In automated molecular
synthesis labs, reagent pumps should remain physically disconnected from power until the
instruction sequence is verified to be free of prohibited pathogen sequences. Similarly,
wearable or implantable AI controllers should have hardware disconnects that trigger if the
device falls out of sync with the patient's natural biological rhythms.
Software recommendations will constantly evolve as AI models change, but the laws of
physics do not. By standardizing the expectation that safety-critical interventions must
happen at the physical energy-delivery layer, HHS can ensure that human clinicians—and
not probabilistic software models—always retain the ultimate authority over patient care.
Thank you for your time and for the work your office is doing to secure the future of
healthcare technology.
