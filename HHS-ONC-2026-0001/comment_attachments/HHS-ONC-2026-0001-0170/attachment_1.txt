II. Responses to Specific Questions
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for healthcare and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
The single greatest barrier to AI innovation in healthcare is the fragmentation of patient data across
incompatible systems and modalities. Patient information today is scattered across EHRs, laboratory
information systems, genomic databases, imaging archives, pharmacy records, and wearable devices —
each with its own format, access model, and update cycle. No common data representation exists across
these modalities, meaning each requires specialized parsing and interpretation.
This fragmentation has a cascading effect on both clinical care and AI development. Clinicians are
forced to serve as the manual "integration layer," synthesizing numerous data sources per patient
encounter — an inefficient process that leads to missed connections and diagnostic delays. For AI
developers, the lack of standardized, interoperable data makes it extraordinarily difficult and expensive
to build models that can reason holistically about a patient's health over time. Even the most advanced
large language models are not natively built to route each dataset to the right analytical tool or to

maintain a longitudinal patient state, and as a result, their clinical reasoning degrades in multimodal
contexts.
Beyond data fragmentation, additional barriers include regulatory uncertainty around the classification
and oversight of non-medical-device AI tools, the absence of reimbursement pathways for AI-enabled
clinical services, and the difficulty of integrating new AI tools into existing clinical workflows without
disrupting care delivery. The reimbursement landscape is particularly challenging: as of January 2026,
only three AI solutions have received Category I CPT codes, and most AI tools are billed under
temporary Category III codes with no guaranteed payment. This uncertainty limits the financial viability
of clinical AI solutions and incentivizes developers to prioritize administrative AI—where revenue
models are more predictable—over clinical AI with greater patient impact.
Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why?
HHS should prioritize a multi-pronged approach that combines regulatory clarity, payment innovation,
and programmatic support. We propose several frameworks that we believe could be transformative.
Regulatory and Programmatic Priorities:
Government-Sponsored Clinical AI Agent Marketplace. HHS should establish a curated,
government-endorsed platform where validated clinical AI agents can be discovered, evaluated, and
deployed by health systems. Health systems today face an overwhelming and opaque landscape of AI
tools with no standardized way to compare them. A government-endorsed marketplace, with clear
validation requirements and transparent performance data, would dramatically lower the barrier to
adoption. Agents could be organized by clinical specialty, condition (e.g., cardiology agents, chronic
care agents, oncology agents), and risk level, allowing health systems to find the right tool for their
needs. The ARPA-H ADVOCATE program, which is funding the development of an FDA-authorized AI
agent for cardiovascular disease, demonstrates that the government is already investing in
condition-specific AI agents; the marketplace concept would scale this approach across the full spectrum
of clinical care.
NPI-Equivalent Identifiers for AI Agents. Just as every clinician in the United States has a unique
National Provider Identifier, validated AI agents should be assigned unique identifiers that allow them to
be tracked, credentialed, audited, and billed against within the healthcare system. This would enable
healthcare organizations to establish AI Agent Credentialing Committees — formal governance
bodies, similar to existing medical staff committees, that review, approve, and continuously monitor the
AI agents deployed in their clinical environments. This framework would bring the same rigor and
accountability to AI deployment that already exists for human clinicians, building trust among providers
and patients alike.
Expanded ARPA-H Funding for Disease-Specific Agents. The ADVOCATE model — which funds the
development of autonomous AI agents with a clear FDA authorization pathway and a defined timeline
— should be replicated for other high-burden conditions such as diabetes, COPD, and cancer. This
would accelerate the creation of a library of validated, condition-specific AI agents that could populate
the proposed marketplace.
Payment Policy Priorities:

Expand the CMS ACCESS Model. The CMS ACCESS (Advancing Chronic Care with Effective,
Scalable Solutions) model is the right foundation — it already tests outcome-aligned payment in
Original Medicare and explicitly promotes technology-enabled care. HHS should make ACCESS the
rule, not the exception, by rapidly expanding the model beyond its current scope and using it as the
template for how AI-enabled care is reimbursed across Medicare. Specifically, ACCESS should be
extended to cover a broader range of AI-driven interventions — not just chronic care management, but
also AI-powered diagnostics, care gap detection, and preventive screening — so that any AI tool
demonstrating measurable improvements in patient outcomes (reduced readmissions, earlier detection,
lower total cost of care) has a clear path to reimbursement.
Accelerate Development of CMAA Codes. Beyond expanding ACCESS, HHS should work with the
AMA to accelerate the development of the proposed "Clinically Meaningful Algorithmic Analyses"
(CMAA) codes — a new coding classification within the CPT code set specifically designed for
algorithm-based services. Today, most clinical AI tools are limited to temporary Category III codes with
no guaranteed payment and wide geographic variation in pricing set by regional MACs. CMAA codes
would create a dedicated, nationally consistent billing pathway for clinical AI, giving developers the
reimbursement certainty they need to invest in clinical — not just administrative — AI.
AI-Specific Value-Based Purchasing Incentives. Finally, HHS should explore AI-specific value-based
purchasing incentives that go beyond ACCESS. For example, Medicare Advantage Star Ratings could
be updated to reward plans that deploy validated AI agents for proactive care management, and shared
savings programs could include explicit bonuses for AI-driven cost reductions that maintain or improve
quality.
Question 3: For non-medical devices, what novel legal and implementation issues exist... and what
role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
The deployment of non-medical-device AI in clinical care raises several novel legal and implementation
challenges that existing governance structures are not fully equipped to address.
Liability and accountability are the most pressing concerns. When an AI agent contributes to a clinical
decision — for example, by flagging a care gap, interpreting a lab result, or synthesizing a patient's
medical history — the question of who bears responsibility for errors is often unclear. Is it the AI
developer, the healthcare provider who acted on the recommendation, or the institution that deployed the
tool? HHS can play a constructive role by developing model frameworks for shared responsibility
that encourage transparency, accountability, and the use of validated AI tools, without imposing liability
structures so burdensome that they stifle innovation. The AI Agent Credentialing Committees proposed
in our response to Question 2 would further clarify lines of accountability by creating a formal
institutional process for evaluating and approving AI agents before deployment.
Privacy and security concerns are amplified by the volume and sensitivity of data that clinical AI
systems process. While HIPAA provides a critical baseline, the emergence of AI tools that synthesize
data across multiple modalities and maintain longitudinal patient states requires a renewed focus on data
governance. HHS should promote the adoption of privacy-enhancing technologies such as federated
learning and differential privacy, and provide explicit guidance on how existing HIPAA provisions apply
to AI-specific data processing activities, including the generation and storage of AI-derived patient
representations.
Implementation challenges include the need for robust change management within healthcare
organizations, the difficulty of integrating AI tools with legacy IT systems, and the absence of
standardized credentialing or certification processes for clinical AI. The NPI-equivalent identifier and

credentialing committee frameworks proposed in Question 2 would directly address the credentialing
gap and give health systems a structured pathway for governing AI deployment.
Question 4: For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods... and
should HHS further support these processes?
The most promising evaluation methods are those that assess AI performance in realistic clinical
contexts using clinician-led, blinded, randomized protocols. Standard NLP benchmarks often fail to
capture the nuances of clinical decision-making. Evaluations should test models on their ability to
perform specific, clinically relevant tasks — such as synthesizing multimodal patient data, identifying
care gaps, or generating accurate differential diagnoses — as judged by practicing physicians.
However, expert physician evaluation does not scale. To solve this, HHS should fund the creation of a
National Repository of Clinician-Backed Grading Rubrics — a structured, open-access library of
evaluation criteria authored and validated by hundreds or thousands of expert physicians across every
major specialty. These rubrics would codify what "good" clinical reasoning looks like for specific tasks
(e.g., interpreting a complex lab panel, triaging disease presentation, recommending a treatment pathway
for a comorbid patient). Once established, these rubrics could be used to power LLM-as-a-Judge
evaluation systems — where a secondary AI model, guided by the physician-authored rubrics, can grade
the outputs of clinical AI tools at scale with a level of rigor that adheres to strict guidelines set by the
expert community. This approach would make high-quality, clinician-aligned evaluation continuous,
scalable, and affordable, rather than limited to expensive, one-off human review studies.
For post-deployment evaluation, continuous monitoring frameworks that track model performance, drift,
and safety in real-world clinical settings are essential. The ARPA-H ADVOCATE program's plan to
develop a disease-agnostic supervisory agent for monitoring AI systems is a promising approach that
could be generalized across the industry.
HHS should further support these processes through several mechanisms:
Federated Evaluation Networks. HHS should fund the creation of government-supported
infrastructure that allows AI models to be tested across multiple institutions on diverse patient
populations without sharing raw patient data. This infrastructure would preserve privacy while enabling
robust, multi-site validation that produces generalizable results.
Open-Source Clinical AI Benchmarks. HHS should fund the creation of publicly available,
high-fidelity synthetic patient datasets and standardized evaluation toolkits for a range of clinical
specialties. This would level the playing field for all developers — including smaller, innovative
companies — and ensure that models are benchmarked against common, clinically meaningful standards
rather than proprietary datasets.
Prize Competitions. HHS should sponsor prize competitions that benchmark AI models on specific,
high-impact clinical challenges, such as care gap detection and treatment pathway optimization. These
would stimulate innovation, establish public performance baselines, and generate valuable data on the
state of the art.
Question 5: How can HHS best support private sector activities... to promote innovative and
effective AI use in clinical care?
HHS can most effectively support the private sector by acting as a convener, endorser, and data
provider.

As a convener, HHS should bring together multi-stakeholder working groups — including AI
developers, healthcare providers, payers, patient advocates, and academic researchers — to develop
consensus-based standards for clinical AI evaluation, deployment, and monitoring. These standards
should be flexible enough to accommodate the rapid pace of AI innovation while rigorous enough to
ensure patient safety.
As a data provider, HHS should make more de-identified government datasets available for AI model
training and evaluation. The NIH, CMS, and VA hold vast repositories of clinical data that, if made
accessible through secure, privacy-preserving mechanisms such as federated learning infrastructure,
could dramatically accelerate the development of high-quality clinical AI models. The transformative
potential of accessible research data for training multimodal clinical models cannot be overstated.
As an endorser, HHS should lend its credibility to industry-led certification and testing initiatives that
meet rigorous standards. The CMS Health Tech Ecosystem pledge program for conversational AI
assistants, which already includes early adopters like major technology companies and health systems, is
an excellent model for how government can catalyze AI adoption through voluntary industry
commitments without heavy-handed regulation.
Question 6: Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the
greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help reduce
costs?
Where AI has met or exceeded expectations:
AI tools have demonstrated exceptional performance in clinical documentation and EHR analysis,
significantly reducing the time clinicians spend on administrative tasks and directly addressing provider
burnout. AI-driven care gap detection has also shown promising results, with intelligent agents
scanning patient records against clinical guidelines to flag missed screenings, lapsed medications, and
overdue follow-ups — enabling proactive intervention at scale. In the payer space, AI is increasingly
being used for prior authorization automation, with some organizations targeting near-real-time
decision-making.
Where AI has fallen short:
AI tools have underperformed when deployed as generic, one-size-fits-all solutions without
domain-specific training or clinical validation. General-purpose LLMs, while impressive in breadth,
often lack the depth of clinical reasoning required for safe and effective use in healthcare. They can
hallucinate, miss critical context, and fail to account for the multimodal, longitudinal nature of patient
data. The industry has also seen troubling examples of AI being used to automate insurance claim
denials without adequate clinical oversight, eroding public trust and underscoring the need for rigorous
governance.
Novel AI tools with the greatest potential:
The tools with the greatest transformative potential are those that enable dynamic, computable models of
individual human physiology — what we term "World Simulations of Humans." This approach
represents a paradigm shift beyond static data aggregation towards creating a dynamic, computable
model of an individual’s entire physiology. Such a simulation would serve as the standard platform for

integrating all multimodal data — from genomics and proteomics to imaging, EHR data, and
real-time wearable streams. By fusing these sources into a unified, dynamic model, these simulations
would enable predictive modeling of disease progression, in silico simulation of individual treatment
responses, and truly personalized care at a biological level. HHS should consider direct investment in
the foundational research and infrastructure for these models, as they represent the ultimate platform for
preventive medicine and long-term cost reduction.
Additionally, condition-specific autonomous AI agents — like the cardiovascular agent being
developed through ARPA-H's ADVOCATE program — represent a paradigm shift from
decision-support tools to active participants in care delivery. Expanding this model to other disease areas
would be particularly impactful.
Question 7: Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations
have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary
administrative hurdles?
The most influential decision-makers for AI adoption are Chief Medical Officers (CMOs), Heads of
Innovation, and increasingly, dedicated AI leads within healthcare organizations. These leaders are
closest to the clinical pain points and are often the ones pushing to accelerate beyond what their
organizations can build in-house. They recognize that the pace of AI development in the broader market
far exceeds what any single health system's internal team can match, and they are actively seeking
external partners and tools. C-suite executives (CEO, CFO, CIO) make the final investment decisions,
primarily motivated by ROI, regulatory compliance, and competitive positioning, while clinical
department chairs and physician leaders have outsized influence on whether frontline clinicians will
actually adopt and trust a new tool in practice.
The primary administrative hurdles are evolving. Most healthcare organizations have focused initial AI
efforts on automating the operational basics: scheduling, billing, claims processing, and documentation.
These segments are important, but they represent the floor, not the ceiling. The larger challenge — and
the larger opportunity — is shifting AI adoption toward higher-value clinical applications: identifying
incorrect billing codes before they cause downstream problems, detecting care gaps where patients
should be receiving screenings or treatments they are not, reducing misdiagnosis through AI-assisted
clinical reasoning, moving toward preventive health by identifying at-risk patients before acute events
occur, improving patient education and engagement through personalized AI-driven outreach, enabling
payers to perform real-time risk tracking and simulation across their populations, and dramatically
compressing the time it takes to assemble care plans for complex disease patients — including finding
the right specialists, trials, and resources nationwide.
The AI Agent Credentialing Committees proposed elsewhere in this response would help organizations
navigate this transition by providing a formal, standardized governance framework for evaluating,
approving, and monitoring AI agents across both operational and clinical use cases — giving innovation
leaders and CMOs a clear process to move faster with confidence.
Question 8: Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research,
and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care?
Enhanced interoperability is the single most important technical enabler of clinical AI at scale. The
universal adoption and enforcement of FHIR-based APIs would transform the healthcare AI landscape.
Market Opportunities

Universal adoption and enforcement of FHIR-based APIs would create a true plug-and-play ecosystem
for clinical AI tools. Today, a significant portion of development time and cost is consumed by custom
integrations with each client's unique data infrastructure. If all EHRs, lab systems, and payer platforms
adhered to common data standards, AI developers could build once and deploy everywhere —
dramatically lowering costs, accelerating time-to-market, and enabling smaller, innovative companies to
compete with entrenched incumbents.
This would be particularly impactful for AI-driven prior authorization automation. With CMS rules
already mandating electronic prior authorization and faster decision timelines between 2026 and 2027, a
fully interoperable system would allow AI agents to securely compile necessary clinical documentation
and submit it to payers in real time, drastically reducing administrative burden and care delays. The
same interoperability would enable AI agents to seamlessly access and synthesize data across providers,
creating the unified patient view that is essential for high-quality clinical reasoning.
Research and AI Development
Interoperability alone is necessary but not sufficient. Even with standardized data formats, it remains
extraordinarily difficult for AI developers and researchers to access large-scale, longitudinal datasets —
spanning EHRs, lab results, imaging, and genomics — and train models on them at scale. Privacy
concerns, institutional data-sharing restrictions, and the sheer cost of assembling and de-identifying
these datasets create a bottleneck that interoperability standards alone cannot solve.
HHS should address this directly through two complementary approaches. First, HHS should establish a
National Clinical AI Training Repository — a secure, centralized (or federated) data environment with
the necessary compute resources where vetted researchers and developers can train models on
large-scale, longitudinal, multimodal clinical data under strict privacy and governance controls. Second,
HHS should invest in the generation and public release of high-fidelity synthetic clinical datasets built
on top of real-world data. Synthetic data that faithfully preserves the statistical properties, clinical
patterns, and longitudinal complexity of real patient records — without exposing any individual's
information — would democratize access to training data and dramatically accelerate the development
of clinical AI across the entire ecosystem.
Without one or both of these interventions, only a handful of organizations with privileged data access
will be able to build frontier clinical AI models — limiting competition, innovation, and ultimately
patient benefit.
The areas where enhanced interoperability would have the greatest impact include genomic data,
wearable and remote monitoring data (which currently lacks any standardized format), lab results, and
imaging — all linked to longitudinal EHR records.
Question 9: What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed
by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers
have?
Challenges patients and caregivers want AI to address:
The most fundamental challenge is the gap between the pace of scientific discovery and the
personalization of care. Millions of peer-reviewed papers are published each year, but the vast majority
of this knowledge never reaches the individual patient in a meaningful way. Patients want personalized,
science-grounded health guidance — not generic recommendations, but insights tailored to their
unique biology, medical history, and circumstances.

Patients and caregivers also want help navigating complex medical journeys, particularly for cancer
and chronic conditions. AI agents that can synthesize multi-specialist findings, connect patients to
relevant clinical trials, and provide a unified view of a complex medical history would be
transformative.
Finally, patients want proactive rather than reactive care. They want to understand health risks before
they become emergencies, and they want their healthcare providers equipped with tools that enable early
identification and intervention.
Concerns patients and caregivers have:
The primary concerns are around data privacy and security — patients want to know that their most
sensitive health information is protected and used only with their consent. There are also concerns about
algorithmic bias and the risk that AI models trained on non-representative datasets could perpetuate or
amplify existing health disparities. Finally, patients value the human relationship in healthcare and
want assurance that AI will augment, not replace, the clinician-patient bond.
The CMS Health Tech Ecosystem pledge program for conversational AI assistants provides a useful
model for addressing these concerns. By having companies publicly commit to building tools that are
secure, transparent, and patient-centered, the program creates accountability and begins to build the
public trust that widespread adoption will require.
Question 10: Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the
adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
HHS should prioritize research in the following areas:
Multimodal AI for Patient Representation and Digital Twins. The ability to create a unified,
continuously updated model of a patient's health — integrating data from EHRs, genomics, proteomics,
metabolomics, imaging, and wearables — is the foundation of next-generation clinical AI. Research that
advances the science of Patient Digital Twins, including methods for fusing heterogeneous data types
and generating rich molecular profiles from routine clinical data, should be a top priority.
Autonomous and Semi-Autonomous Clinical AI Agents. Building on the ARPA-H ADVOCATE
model, HHS should fund research into the design, safety, and governance of AI agents that can
autonomously perform clinical tasks — from care coordination and medication management to patient
triage and monitoring. This funding would encompass research on supervisory AI systems that can
monitor and ensure the safety of other AI agents in real time.
Implementation Science for Clinical AI. Understanding how to effectively integrate AI into clinical
workflows — including the organizational, behavioral, and technical factors that determine success or
failure — is as important as the AI technology itself. HHS should fund research on best practices for AI
deployment, clinician training, change management, and the design of AI Agent Credentialing
Committees.
AI Safety, Fairness, and Explainability. Research on methods to detect and mitigate bias in clinical AI
models, to ensure equitable performance across diverse patient populations, and to make AI reasoning
transparent and interpretable to clinicians and patients is essential for building and maintaining public
trust.
