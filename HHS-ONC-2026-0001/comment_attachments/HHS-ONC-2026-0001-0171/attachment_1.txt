Executive Summary
xCures operates an AI-assisted healthcare data platform that extracts and structures clinical
information from medical records gathered across multiple sources, including from health information
networks like TEFCA and Carequality. This platform serves as a semantic layer for healthcare data,
delivering decision-ready intelligence that empowers professionals to act with confidence and clarity.
We appreciate the opportunity to comment on how HHS can accelerate AI adoption in clinical
care. Our experience processing millions of patient records across the U.S. healthcare system suggests
that the primary barrier to AI efficacy is not algorithm availability, but data readiness—specifically, the
inability of standard systems to process unstructured clinical evidence (PDFs, faxes, and images) where
the most critical medical insights reside.
We respectfully submit the following responses to specific questions in the RFI, advocating for a
regulatory framework that prioritizes Source Verifiability (audit trails), Universal Participation, and
Semantic Interoperability.
Responses to Specific Questions
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care?
Barrier A: The "Unstructured Data" Cliff
The greatest technical barrier is the industry’s over-reliance on structured data feeds (HL7/FHIR) which
often fail to capture the complete clinical picture.
● The Reality: While a majority of health data transmission may technically be structured
(XML/JSON), in reality the high-value clinical indicators—pathology reports, genomic staging, and
discharge summaries—predominantly reside in unstructured formats like PDFs, faxes, and
scanned images.
● The Barrier: Current "standard" interoperability often dumps these files as raw blobs that generic
AI models struggle to interpret accurately. Without an infrastructure layer capable of normalizing
this unstructured multimedia data into compute-ready formats, clinical AI will remain limited to
basic administrative tasks rather than deep clinical decision support.
Barrier B: Data Access and Network Participation
Even when data is digital, another barrier is just getting access to all the data. Not all holders of
patient's health data are participating in TEFCA.

● The Barrier: This non-universal participation in health information networks means AI
models cannot build a complete, longitudinal view of the patient, leading to
"hallucinations" born of missing context.
● The Solution: Innovation requires a federal requirement or penalty for not participating
in TEFCA's treatment and individual access services pathway, which are critical for
healthcare providers and patients to have access to their longitudinal data in order to
use that for AI. Streamlining access through mandatory participation and seamless
access would allow healthcare providers and patients to rapidly aggregate the necessary
data to power clinical AI, reducing the cost and time delays currently imposed by
fragmented retrieval processes.
2. What regulatory or payment policy changes should HHS prioritize?
Incentivize "Data Completeness" and Administrative Reduction:
HHS should prioritize payment models that reimburse for the completeness of clinical documentation,
not just the volume of care.
● Reimbursement Leakage: Healthcare organizations currently face significant revenue loss due to
missing or incomplete documentation. This "administrative friction" delays patient care (e.g.,
prior authorization bottlenecks).
● Recommendation: CMS should allow AI-verified data (with direct links to source documents) to
automatically satisfy documentation requirements for Prior Authorization and Quality Reporting
(e.g., NCQA ECDS). This would incentivize the adoption of AI tools that solve the "missing data"
problem, directly reducing administrative burden.
4. & 5. What are the most promising AI evaluation methods? How can HHS support
certification?
The "Trust but Verify" Standard:
HHS must distinguish between "Black Box" AI (which summarizes without proof) and Source-Verified AI.
● Safety Risk: Generative AI that summarizes patient history without an audit trail poses a
significant safety risk (hallucinations).
● Proposed Standard: We advocate that any AI tool used for clinical decision-making or
reimbursement must meet a "Source Linking" standard. Every extracted clinical fact (e.g.,
"Patient has Stage 4 Breast Cancer") must be digitally linked to the specific page, document, and
timestamp of the source record.
● Validation: Validation metrics should not just measure "accuracy" against a gold standard, but
the speed and ability of a human reviewer to verify the AI's claim via these source links. This
"human-in-the-loop" architecture is the only safe path for clinical AI.
● Ongoing Quality Control: Initial validation is insufficient given the rapid evolution of AI
technology. We recommend regulatory frameworks mandate ongoing quality controls—such as

continuous monitoring and regression testing—to ensure that AI tools remain trustworthy and
stable as models and data sources evolve.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities?
A. Mandate Universal Network Participation
To widen market opportunities for AI, interoperability must be universal, not optional.
● The Problem: Currently, participation in networks like TEFCA is voluntary, leaving significant gaps
in coverage—particularly among smaller practices, rural clinics, and specialists who may still rely
on fax. AI models trained on or operating with "Swiss cheese" data (data with holes) are
inherently unsafe and biased.
● Recommendation: HHS should prioritize regulatory levers that move towards mandatory
participation in Health Information Networks for all providers. Universal participation ensures
that AI agents have access to the entire patient journey, not just the episodes that occurred in
large, tech-enabled health systems.
B. From "Transport" to "Semantic" Interoperability
Current interoperability frameworks (TEFCA, QHINs) excel at transporting files but fail at semantic
understanding.
● The Gap: Moving a PDF from Hospital A to Hospital B is "interoperability," but it does not help an
AI system understand the patient's history.
● Evidence: In representative analyses of patients with complex conditions (e.g., advanced
oncology), we found that relying solely on standard structured data feeds resulted in missing
approximately 88% of relevant Hierarchical Condition Category (HCC) coding opportunities
compared to AI-enhanced extraction from unstructured notes.
● Recommendation: HHS should broaden the definition of "interoperability" to include the capacity
to parse, normalize, and structure unstructured data. True interoperability is not just moving the
file; it is making the content of the file machine-readable for downstream AI applications.
