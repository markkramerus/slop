On behalf of Clearstep Inc. (Clearstep), thank you for the opportunity to provide comments in
response to the Department of Health and Human Services’ (HHS) Request for Information on
accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence (AI) as part of clinical care. Clearstep
supports HHS’s “OneHHS” efforts to align regulatory, reimbursement, and research and
development strategies across the Department to promote safe, effective, and equitable AI use in
clinical care.
In summary, Clearstep believes that an HHS-wide AI framework that explicitly integrates both
expert-encoded systems and statistical methods is essential to maximizing patient safety,
ensuring explainability and transparency, and fostering responsible innovation. Our
recommendations emphasize: (1) a proportionate, risk-based regulatory posture that
differentiates opaque “black-box” models from expert-encoded and hybrid systems; (2)
modernized reimbursement that rewards validated AI tools for delivering measurable value; and
(3) targeted research, implementation science, and public–private partnerships that bridge the gap
between promising AI capabilities and safe, scalable use in clinical care.

I. Regulation
Clearstep supports HHS’s objective to establish a regulatory posture on AI that is well
understood, predictable, and proportionate to risk, enabling rapid innovation while protecting
patients and maintaining public trust. In our view, there is a particular opportunity for HHS to
clarify expectations for non-medical-device AI tools that influence access, routing, and
workflow, including digital triage, navigation, voice automation, and scheduling decision
support.
A. Proportionate, risk-based oversight and transparency
We encourage HHS to explicitly distinguish between:
● Opaque “black-box” statistical models and foundation models, and
● Expert-encoded and hybrid systems, whose decision pathways are traceable to
established clinical guidelines and curated content.
Regulatory expectations including documentation, transparency, and monitoring obligations
should be calibrated accordingly. For AI tools used in clinical navigation and decision support,
HHS can build on ONC’s HTI-1 rule and Decision Support Intervention (DSI) transparency
elements in 45 CFR Part 170 to:
● Require that systems be explainable, interpretable, and tunable, especially where they are
patient-facing or influence access, prioritization, and/or care delivery.
● Require developers to document intended use, target population, clinical context, and
known limitations, including situations where human override is expected.
● Ensure that key outputs (e.g., triage dispositions, routing recommendations) are traceable
back to clinical best practice guidelines and content sources, enabling independent review
by licensed clinicians and local governance bodies.
These expectations should apply both to certified health IT and to non-device AI systems
integrated via APIs, with proportional tailoring based on the potential clinical impact of the tool.
B. Validation, certification, and post-market surveillance

Clearstep supports formalizing expectations for pre-deployment validation and post-deployment
monitoring of AI tools that are used in clinical workflows. We recommend that HHS:
● Define baseline validation expectations for non-device clinical AI (e.g., triage,
navigation, scheduling decision support), including stress-testing on edge cases,
representative subpopulations, and incomplete or noisy data.
● Encourage documentation of performance metrics such as hallucination rates, data drift,
under- and over-triage, and first-contact resolution for navigation and voice tools.
● Coordinate with ONC to extend certification concepts in 45 CFR Part 170 to AI-driven
clinical navigation and workflow tools, so that health systems have a consistent HHS
signal of quality and safety when procuring such solutions.
Post-market surveillance should mirror pharmacovigilance principles, including:
● Continuous monitoring for performance degradation, bias, and drift across populations
and sites.
● Defined thresholds and processes for remediation or rollback when safety or performance
concerns emerge.
● Structured mechanisms for reporting and addressing significant performance or safety
incidents.
C. Privacy, de-identification, and data use for AI
Clearstep strongly supports the HIPAA de-identification standards under 45 CFR 164.514 for AI
training and evaluation. To accelerate innovation, we encourage HHS to provide AI-specific
guidance on:
● Expert Determination (45 CFR 164.514(b)(1)): Clarifying how Covered Entities and
Business Associates can most efficiently apply this method to validate datasets for AI
development.
● Lifecycle Governance: Establishing clear expectations for documenting de-identification
methodologies and managing the long-term safety of datasets used in AI model
evolution.
These clarifications would reduce the administrative burden on developers while ensuring that
model growth does not compromise privacy or security.
II. Reimbursement
HHS payment policies and programs are a primary determinant of whether high-value AI tools
can be deployed and sustained in clinical care. In our experience, current reimbursement
structures often under-recognize AI’s contributions to access, safety, and operational efficiency.
A. Rewarding AI-enabled value, not volume

Legacy fee-for-service payment regimes rarely recognize the cost savings and safety
improvements created by validated AI tools, particularly when those benefits arise from avoided
visits, optimized site-of-care selection, and administrative efficiency. Clearstep recommends that
HHS:
● Expand value-based care and alternative payment models that explicitly recognize AI’s
role in reducing avoidable ED visits, improving triage and routing, and increasing
completion of preventive and chronic care services.
● Allow providers to share in savings that are attributable to AI-enabled improvements in
access, navigation, and care coordination, provided tools meet HHS-defined transparency
and safety standards.
B. Liability and reimbursement clarity
Uncertainty around liability for AI-supported decisions is a significant barrier to adoption.
Clearstep manages this through comprehensive insurance and indemnification protections and by
ensuring that our systems are used as decision support rooted in clinical best practice. To support
broader adoption across HHS programs, we recommend that HHS:
● Clarify expectations for allocation of responsibility between AI developers and providers
when tools are used within labeled indications, with appropriate human oversight and
documented governance.
● Consider establishing safe harbor approaches in which providers using AI tools that
satisfy HHS-defined criteria for transparency, monitoring, and validation receive
specified protections related to AI-driven decision support.
● Align reimbursement rules so that providers are not penalized for using robust,
safety-forward AI tools that demonstrably improve outcomes and operational
performance.
C. Incentivizing safety-focused, expert-encoded safeguards
Reimbursement policies should explicitly recognize the added safeguards provided by systems
that integrate expert-encoded knowledge with statistical models. We recommend that HHS:
● Differentiate, in payment and program design, between “black-box-only” approaches and
systems that provide traceable, clinician-reviewable logic anchored in validated clinical
guidelines.
● Encourage payment models that reward the use of AI tools with clear safety guardrails,
conservative fail-safes, and mechanisms for continuous oversight and tuning.

HHS is uniquely positioned to catalyze research, implementation science, and public–private
partnerships that accelerate responsible AI adoption in clinical care.

A. Implementation science
Clearstep encourages HHS to prioritize implementation science that studies:
● How AI tools integrate into diverse clinical workflows (e.g., digital front doors, nurse
advice lines, call centers, ambulatory and specialty scheduling).
● How governance, credentialing, and privileging models can incorporate AI-supported
workflows without duplicative administrative burden.
● How AI affects patient experience, equity, clinician and staff workload, and overall
system efficiency.
B. Public–private partnerships and CRADAs
Building on our work within the Defense Health Agency environment, we recommend that HHS:
● Use Cooperative Research and Development Agreements (CRADAs) and related
arrangements to test AI triage, navigation, and scheduling tools across heterogeneous
delivery systems, including those serving Medicare, Medicaid, and other priority
populations.
C. Priority focus areas
HHS research and development investments should prioritize:
● AI Voice Automation for nurse advice lines and call centers improving first-contact
resolution, narrowing access gaps, and alleviating workforce strain. Particularly
distinguishing those solutions that use trusted, gold standard clinical algorithms as
opposed to those that use black box statistical methods that may hallucinate, drift,
provide inconsistent responses, and the basis for which recommendations cannot be
evaluated.
● AI Optimized Scheduling and intake that match patients to appropriate appointments and
care settings across the full range of EHR and scheduling maturity.
● Methods and tools to measure and mitigate hallucinations, bias, and data drift over time,
and to empower health systems to perform ongoing AI monitoring without requiring deep
in-house AI expertise.
Responses to Selected RFI Questions
1. Biggest barriers to private sector innovation and adoption
Clearstep observes several key barriers to AI innovation and adoption in clinical care:
● Legal and governance uncertainty regarding liability for AI-supported decisions in mixed
human–AI workflows.
● Significant variance in EHR and scheduling maturity, limiting the ability to deploy
real-time booking and advanced orchestration consistently across provider organizations.

● Limited liquidity and standardized availability of administrative and resource data, which
complicates the seamless facilitation of scheduling and care navigation across the

● Administrative hurdles, including inconsistent credentialing and privileging requirements
and fragmented internal AI review processes that slow replication and scaling of proven

2. Regulatory, payment, and programmatic priorities
We recommend that HHS prioritize:
● Standardizing referral qualification and documentation in ways that support, rather than
replace, local credentialing and privileging; Clearstep’s tools, for example, standardize
referral criteria and documentation while leaving credentialing authority with local
governance.
● Revisiting 45 CFR Part 170 to incorporate clear certification criteria for AI-driven
clinical navigation and decision support, aligned with ONC’s DSI transparency and
algorithmic accountability requirements.
● Recognizing and incentivizing “safe handoff” patterns structured, context-rich handoffs
from AI to human coordinators where real-time booking is not authorized or technically
feasible, given EHR maturity variance.
3 & 4. Non-medical devices: legal, implementation, and evaluation issues
For non-medical-device AI tools used in clinical care, Clearstep recommends that HHS:
● Establish safe harbor concepts for providers that use AI tools meeting HHS-established
transparency, safety, and monitoring criteria, with clear expectations for human oversight
and scope-of-use.
● Support evaluation frameworks that emphasize hallucination rates, data drift, under- and
over-triage, and first-contact resolution, alongside traditional accuracy measures,
particularly for navigation and voice automation tools.
● Use contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and prize competitions to fund robust
stress-testing of AI tools across diverse clinical scenarios and populations and to develop
open benchmarking frameworks and monitoring toolkits.
7. Key decision-makers and administrative hurdles
Within health systems, Clearstep typically engages CIOs, CMOs, digital leaders, access and call
center leaders, and legal/compliance stakeholders. The primary administrative hurdles to AI
adoption include:
● EHR and scheduling maturity variance, which constrains the ability to implement
real-time booking and advanced automation uniformly.

● Fragmented governance and review processes, where multiple committees evaluate AI in

In practice, AI tools must be designed to function across high-maturity environments (real-time

8. Interoperability, data standards, and benchmarking
Enhanced interoperability through consistent use of FHIR-based standards would substantially
widen opportunities for AI tools and accelerate innovation. Clearstep recommends that HHS:
● Promote consistent implementation of FHIR resources for appointments, scheduling, and
referrals across major EHR platforms to support AI-driven triage, navigation, and
real-time booking.
● Prioritize benchmarking tools and shared test scenarios for AI-driven scheduling and
intake, including common metrics and test cases that link patient-facing AI interactions to
provider-facing workflows.
● Ensure that administrative data necessary to facilitate scheduling across all health system
service lines be made interoperable for solutions that health systems desire to put in place
to provide benefit to the end patient.

AI that balances innovation with safety, privacy, civil rights, and public trust.
