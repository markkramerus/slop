Dear Mr. Posnack:
The Health Data Standards division of the Regenstrief Institute appreciates the opportunity to
respond to the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy’s Request for Information on
accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence in clinical care. As a research
organization with deep expertise in biomedical informatics, learning health systems, and
national health data standards stewardship, Regenstrief approaches AI not as a standalone
technology, but as a capability that depends on strong data foundations, real-world evidence,
and sustained governance.
Our responses focus on areas where federal leadership can most effectively translate AI
innovation into durable clinical impact. In particular, we emphasize the importance of semantic
and technical interoperability, standards-based data infrastructure, pragmatic evaluation in real-
world settings, and post-deployment monitoring to support safe, trustworthy, and scalable use of
AI in care delivery.
The enclosed responses address three priority areas:
• Key barriers to private sector innovation and adoption of AI in clinical care
• The role of enhanced interoperability in widening markets, fueling research, and
enabling generalizable AI evidence
• Patient and caregiver priorities and concerns related to AI use in clinical settings
Across these areas, our comments reflect findings from the published literature and practical
experience supporting national health data standards, multi-site research, and learning health
system infrastructure. We believe sustained progress in clinical AI adoption will depend less on
algorithmic advances alone and more on coordinated investment in data standards,

Specific Questions
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care
and its adoption and use in clinical care?
Comment: At their core, many barriers to AI adoption stem from inconsistent, non-
standardized clinical data and limited semantic interoperability, which undermine technical
performance and trust. AI systems depend on computable, comparable data across settings,
yet uneven adoption and alignment of foundational standards—such as LOINC®, UCUM,
SNOMED CT®, and others—continue to require costly data normalization and limit the ability
to validate, scale, and monitor AI tools in real-world clinical environments. These gaps
contribute to clinician skepticism and limit the ability to generate transferable, real-world
evidence across institutions. Strengthening national adoption and coordinated use of core
clinical data standards would lower development and implementation costs, improve model
reliability and transparency, and support more robust evaluation of safety and effectiveness.
Addressing these data foundations, alongside regulatory clarity and payment alignment, is
essential to building confidence in AI and unlocking its full potential in clinical care.
Question 8: Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel
research, and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific
data types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
Comment: Enhanced interoperability would most effectively widen market opportunities and
accelerate AI development where it enables semantically consistent, standards-based, and
longitudinal clinical data that can be reused across organizations, vendors, and care settings.
1. Foundational Clinical Data Prioritized in USCDI
AI development and deployment depend on consistent representation of core clinical
data prioritized within USCDI, including laboratory results, vital signs, clinical
observations, diagnoses, procedures, medications, and outcomes. Stronger
interoperability across these data types—through coordinated use of nationally
recognized data, terminology, and units of measure standards—would reduce data
normalization costs, improve model generalizability, and expand market access for AI
developers.
2. Semantic and Technical Interoperability Across the Standards Ecosystem
True interoperability requires shared clinical meaning and reliable technical exchange.
Uneven adoption and misalignment across clinical terminologies, information models,

exchange specifications, and implementation guides continue to limit AI development,
evaluation, and deployment. Greater coordination across the standards ecosystem
would enable AI models trained in one environment to be more readily validated,
deployed, and monitored in others without extensive reengineering.
3. Longitudinal and Cross-Setting Data
AI tools that support prediction, risk stratification, quality measurement, and outcomes
analysis require longitudinal data spanning inpatient, outpatient, laboratory, public
health, and community-based care settings. Interoperability that supports consistent
use of standards-aligned data over time and across settings would fuel research and
strengthen real-world evidence generation.
4. Benchmarking, Evaluation, and Real-World Evidence Infrastructure
Enhanced interoperability is essential for shared benchmarking tools, reference
datasets, and evaluation frameworks. When AI inputs and outputs are grounded in
common standards, performance metrics can be compared meaningfully across
vendors and institutions, supporting post-deployment monitoring and adoption.
5. Supporting Evolution and Innovation in Standards
Interoperability frameworks should support responsible integration of emerging data
types and evolving standards while maintaining backward compatibility and alignment
with USCDI.
Question 9: What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see
addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients
and caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
Comment: Research consistently shows that patients and caregivers support AI adoption
when it improves access, coordination, safety, and personalization of care while preserving
trust and the clinician–patient relationship. Patients value AI applications that improve
timeliness of care, reduce diagnostic delays, support care coordination for complex
conditions, reduce documentation burden, and enable more meaningful patient–clinician
interaction.
At the same time, patients express concerns related to loss of human connection, lack of
transparency or explainability, inconsistent performance across care settings, privacy and
secondary use of health data, and accountability when AI contributes to clinical decisions.
Evidence suggests patient trust increases when AI tools are transparent, grounded in high-
quality clinical data, evaluated in real-world settings, and deployed as decision support rather
than decision-makers.
