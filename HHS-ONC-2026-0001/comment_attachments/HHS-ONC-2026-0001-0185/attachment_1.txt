Dear Deputy Secretary and ASTP/ONC Leadership,

Thank you for the opportunity to provide input on the integration of artificial intelligence into
clinical care. As a physical therapist, educator, and academic leader responsible for curriculum
development and professional formation, | support the continued acceleration of Al adoption in
healthcare. However, sustained success will depend on thoughtful governance, high-quality data
infrastructure, and deliberate investment in workforce preparation.

agree that data fragmentation remains a central structural barrier. Al tools are only as strong as
the datasets on which they are trained. If health data remain siloed across systems, specialties,
and populations, we risk perpetuating bias and widening disparities rather than reducing them.
Responsible interoperability standards, data quality requirements, and safeguards for privacy
rust remain foundational. Rapid adoption without rigorous data standards may yield short-term
innovation but long-term erosion of trust. This loss of trust is of great concer to me.

Clear accountability structures are equally essential. Where clinicians retain decision-making
authority, responsibility should align with professional judgment, consistent with existing
standards of practice. However, as Al tools move toward more autonomous workflows,
accountability must be shared between deploying organizations and product developers. Aligning
accountability with decision-making authority will protect both patients and practitioners while
maintaining clarity in governance. The human mind must be included in the decision making
algorithin.

From an educational perspective, 1 would urge HHS to recognize that Al integration in clinical
care necessitates parallel investment in healthcare education. Al literacy should not be treated as
an optional technical competency; it is rapidly becoming a core professional skill. Healthcare
students must be trained to critically evaluate Al outputs, understand algorithmic limitations,
recognize automation bias, and apply tools within scope-of-practice boundaries. Without this
foundation, even well-designed tools may be misapplied.

Federal support for Al workforce readiness could take several forms:

Competitive grants to healthcare education programs to develop Al-integrated curricula
Interprofessional pilot programs that study Al implementation in clinical training environments
Scholarships or loan forgiveness programs for students pursuing advanced training in health
informatics, clinical data science, or Al governance

Faculty development funding to ensure educators themselves are prepared to teach Al
competency responsibly

Such investments would help ensure that Al adoption enhances rather than outpaces clinical
education and professional standards.

Finally, reducing clinician administrative burden through validated Al tools (such as ambient
documentation systems) represents a high-value opportunity. Burnout is not merely a workforce
concem; it is directly tied to patient safety and quality of care. Supporting the adoption of
evidence-based administrative Al tools, particularly in resource-constrained and rural settings,
could meaningfully improve care delivery capacity while preserving clinician engagement.
