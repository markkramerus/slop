0. Introduction
A report published by the IBM Institute for Business Value (IBV) in August 2025 found that 77%
of healthcare executives report AI is delivering clear and measurable competitive advantage, and
69% expect it to contribute significantly to financial performance within the next three years. AI
in clinical care has moved beyond proof-of-concept experimentation and now presents an
implementation challenge. The central barriers to adoption are no longer model capability, but
structural conditions: payment alignment, regulatory clarity, interoperable data infrastructure, and
mature governance frameworks. HHS is uniquely positioned to address these systemic constraints.
By modernizing reimbursement policy, establishing adaptive regulatory pathways, and enforcing
standards-based interoperability, HHS can accelerate safe and scalable AI deployment in clinical
care while reinforcing public trust.
IBM Consulting, together with IBM’s Technology and Research divisions, brings decades of
experience supporting federal health agencies, academic medical centers, integrated delivery
networks, life sciences organizations, and payers in implementing and advancing AI within highly
regulated healthcare environments. Drawing on this cross-sector experience, we offer practical
insights into how policy, reimbursement, governance, and technology must align to translate
strategy into operational impact. We look forward to continued collaboration with HHS to advance
a trusted, scalable, and patient-centered AI ecosystem that strengthens clinical care delivery.
1. Barriers to AI Innovation and Adoption (RFI Question #1)
The most significant barriers to private sector innovation and clinical adoption of AI are
structural rather than technical. Regulatory fragmentation remains a persistent obstacle.
Developers and health systems must navigate overlapping and occasionally inconsistent
requirements across FDA, CMS, NIH, and ASTP/ONC. This ambiguity increases compliance
costs, delays commercialization, and creates disproportionate burden for smaller innovators.
Even when AI tools demonstrate measurable clinical value, predictable reimbursement pathways
are often absent. Medicare regulations do not consistently recognize or value AI-enabled
diagnostic interpretation and decision support services. As a result, providers face financial
disincentives to adopt tools that require integration, monitoring, and governance oversight.
Adoption therefore remains concentrated in well-capitalized health systems, exacerbating
disparities in access to advanced care capabilities.
Data access and interoperability limitations further constrain innovation. Although progress
has been made under the 21st Century Cures Act, clinical data remain fragmented across
electronic health record platforms and proprietary systems. Variability in FHIR implementation
and restrictive data-use practices limit the ability to train, validate, and benchmark AI models at
scale. Significant engineering effort is frequently diverted toward data normalization rather than
performance validation, bias mitigation, or workflow integration.
Workforce readiness remains a challenge, with gaps in trust and digital literacy among
clinicians. Many providers lack formal training in algorithmic performance metrics, bias
evaluation, or appropriate-use boundaries, limiting their ability to critically interpret AI-
generated outputs. Concerns regarding patient safety, liability exposure, and accountability for
AI-influenced decisions further suppress confidence, particularly in environments where
governance frameworks and oversight mechanisms are not clearly defined.

2. HHS Regulatory Priorities (RFI Question #2)
Based on IBM’s experiences supporting providers, payers, regulators, and industry partners,
HHS should prioritize regulatory and payment policy changes that directly address the structural
barriers preventing AI from being deployed at scale in routine clinical care.
CMS should urgently modernize reimbursement frameworks to recognize AI-assisted
diagnostic interpretation and clinical decision support as covered and appropriately valued
services under Medicare Part B. This includes clarifying coverage under 42 CFR Part 410,
establishing valuation methodologies under the Physician Fee Schedule (42 CFR Part 414), and
updating National Coverage Determinations to address AI tools systematically. AI adoption
should also be aligned with value-based care by expanding incentives under the Medicare Shared
Savings Program (42 CFR Part 425) and updating Merit-based Incentive Payment System
(MIPS) measures (42 CFR Part 414.1305) to recognize measurable improvements in cost,
quality, safety, and coordination attributable to validated AI tools.
FDA regulatory modernization is equally critical, particularly for developers seeking to
deploy adaptive AI tools responsibly. Current approval pathways under 21 CFR Part 814 and 21
CFR Part 807 are designed for static technologies and do not accommodate continuously
learning algorithms without repeated, costly resubmissions. HHS should formalize the use of
predetermined change control plans and establish AI-specific Software as a Medical Device
guidance under 21 CFR Part 807, enabling algorithm updates within defined boundaries. In
parallel, FDA should refine device classification criteria under 21 CFR Part 860.7 to account for
clinical impact and the degree of human oversight, while shifting greater emphasis toward post-
market, real-world performance monitoring.
Interoperability and data access reforms are foundational to both AI development and
deployment. HHS should strengthen ONC Health IT Certification requirements under 45 CFR
Part 170 to mandate standardized API access to all clinically relevant data types, including
imaging and longitudinal records, and require comprehensive FHIR implementation across EHR-
generated data. At the same time, HHS should expand information blocking prohibitions under
45 CFR Part 171.103 to ensure that legitimate data access for AI development and validation is
not improperly restricted. Clarifying HIPAA de-identification standards under 45 CFR Part
164.514 would further augment AI development by providing clearer safe harbors for training
data, while expedited pathways for minimal-risk validation studies under 45 CFR Part 46 would
reduce unnecessary research friction.
Beyond agency-specific reforms, HHS should establish a coordinated, risk-based regulatory
framework aligning the roles of FDA, CMS, ONC, and OCR. Oversight should be tiered
according to clinical risk, with rigorous standards for high-risk decision support tools and
proportionate governance for lower-risk operational applications. Transparency and bias
mitigation requirements could be incorporated into hospital Conditions of Participation (42 CFR
Part 482), alongside clearer federal guidance on liability and standard-of-care considerations.

3. Governance and Accountability (RFI Question #3)
The integration of AI into clinical care introduces governance and accountability challenges that
extend beyond traditional health IT oversight. Unlike static clinical software, AI systems may
evolve over time, rely on probabilistic outputs, and depend heavily on the quality and
representativeness of training data. These characteristics raise novel questions related to
liability, transparency, bias mitigation, lifecycle monitoring, and shared responsibility
among developers, providers, and institutions.
HHS should play a central coordinating and standard-setting role in addressing these
challenges. Federal leadership is necessary to establish consistent national expectations, ensure
risk-proportionate oversight, and safeguard equity across the healthcare ecosystem while
avoiding fragmentation across agencies and states.
IBM’s AI Governance framework (Figure 1) provides a useful operational blueprint,
demonstrating how policy objectives can be translated into practice through the integration of
enterprise-level governance structures with continuous AI technical lifecycle management,
aligning institutional accountability with ongoing AI model validation, monitoring, and
oversight.
At the organizational level, this framework emphasizes maintaining a comprehensive AI
inventory that documents system purpose, risk classification, validation evidence, intended use
boundaries, and known limitations. It incorporates structured risk-tiering to differentiate
administrative automation tools from clinical decision support systems and higher-risk
autonomous or semi-autonomous applications. Clearly defined governance bodies and decision
rights guide procurement, validation, deployment, monitoring, and retirement, ensuring
accountability across clinical, technical, compliance, and executive leadership.
At the technical level, the framework extends governance across the full AI lifecycle. This
includes rigorous pre-deployment validation, ongoing post-deployment performance monitoring,
version control, documentation of training data provenance, and formal processes for detecting
and addressing model drift, performance degradation, or emerging bias. Ongoing subgroup
performance monitoring promotes equitable outcomes across populations. Transparency is
operationalized through structured documentation of model assumptions, context-appropriate
explainability mechanisms, and auditable records of system behavior, reinforcing both regulatory
compliance and public trust.
At IBM, dedicated Safety Officers play a central role in supporting this model by providing
independent assurance, overseeing safety cases, monitoring AI‑related risks, and supporting
incident management and escalation. Human‑in‑the‑loop oversight remains mandatory for
safety‑critical use cases, supported by full auditability and traceability of AI outputs. Governance
bodies continuously monitor performance, bias, and unintended impacts, enabling rapid response

4. Evaluation and Robustness (RFI Question #4)
AI systems used in clinical care require rigorous, lifecycle-based evaluation methodologies
that extend beyond traditional software testing. Evaluation should be conceptualized as a
continuous process rather than a one-time validation event.
Pre-deployment validation must assess clinical performance in realistic conditions. This
includes sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and calibration across relevant patient
populations. Subgroup equity analysis should be mandatory for systems influencing diagnosis,
treatment selection, or care prioritization. Robust testing should evaluate performance under data
perturbation, missingness, and potential adversarial inputs. Importantly, validation should
include workflow simulation to determine how outputs are interpreted and acted upon in
practice, not merely whether predictions are statistically accurate.
Post-deployment monitoring is equally critical. AI performance may degrade over time due to
changes in patient populations, clinical practice patterns, data collection methods, or external
factors. Continuous monitoring should track drift in input distributions, outcome divergence,
override patterns by clinicians, and unexpected error clusters. Monitoring systems should support
rapid root-cause analysis and structured remediation pathways. For higher-risk applications,
periodic revalidation or recalibration thresholds should be defined in advance.
Human-centered evaluation must complement technical metrics. Clinical AI effectiveness
depends on comprehension, trust calibration, and usability. Evaluation should therefore assess
clinician understanding of model outputs, alignment with cognitive workflow, and impact on
decision confidence. Metrics such as alert fatigue rates, time-to-decision, and override frequency
can provide insight into real-world integration quality. Patient-centered measures, including
understanding of AI-supported recommendations and satisfaction with transparency, are also
important components of trust-building.
HHS can meaningfully advance evaluation standards through a mix of grants, cooperative
agreements, prize competitions, and targeted contract vehicles. Grants and cooperative
agreements are well suited to academic-led methodological research and the development of
standardized benchmarking frameworks. In contrast, contract mechanisms may be more effective
for building shared benchmarking infrastructure, reference implementations, and standardized
evaluation toolkits that require clearly defined deliverables, timelines, and cross-sector technical
coordination.
Longitudinal studies examining clinical outcomes, cost implications, health equity impacts, and
workforce effects will be essential to inform reimbursement reform and regulatory
modernization. These efforts may benefit from blended funding models that combine academic
research rigor with industry implementation expertise. Establishing common evaluation norms
through coordinated funding approaches will reduce variability in institutional review processes
and support safe, scalable adoption of AI in clinical care.

6. Successes, Lessons Learned, and Priority Areas for AI in Clinical Care
(RFI Question #6)
Where AI Succeeds in Healthcare
Based on IBM’s experience supporting healthcare providers, payers, and life sciences
organizations, AI tools in clinical environments tend to meet or exceed expectations when
deployed in well-defined workflows with clear performance metrics and strong human
oversight. Administrative and operational applications, such as documentation support, coding
assistance, prior authorization analytics, scheduling optimization, and revenue cycle intelligence,
have consistently demonstrated measurable improvements in cycle time, throughput, and staff
productivity. In these contexts, outputs are verifiable, risk tolerance is manageable, and success
metrics are concrete. Similarly, AI applied to bounded clinical tasks, such as imaging triage,
signal interpretation, deterioration risk scoring within defined populations, and medication safety
surveillance, has shown strong performance when integrated into existing workflows and paired
with clinician review. These tools add value by improving prioritization, consistency, and early
detection rather than replacing clinical judgment. For instance, IBM has successfully deployed
AI-enabled scheduling, workflow optimization, and operational analytics platforms that
improved throughput and reduced administrative burden in UK hospitals. These deployments
achieved measurable gains because they were embedded within existing workflows, aligned with
organizational governance structures, and evaluated against defined operational metrics such as
cycle time, staffing efficiency, and quality performance indicators.

cost savings by focusing on model development without investing in process redesign,
training, monitoring infrastructure, and accountability structures required to
operationalize insights. Without clear ownership of downstream actions and ongoing lifecycle
oversight, early gains can diminish over time.

7. AI Adoption Dynamics in Health Care Organizations (RFI Question #7)
Adoption of AI in clinical care is shaped as much by institutional dynamics as by technical merit.
Within healthcare organizations, decision-making authority is distributed across clinical
leadership, digital and information technology leadership, data governance bodies,
compliance committees, and financial executives. Chief Medical Officers often determine
whether AI tools are clinically credible and aligned with standards of care, while Chief
Information or Digital Officers assess infrastructure readiness and integration feasibility. Finance
committees evaluate return on investment and budget trade-offs, and compliance and privacy
officers scrutinize risk exposure. Adoption frequently stalls when these constituencies are not
aligned or when accountability boundaries are unclear.
One of the primary administrative hurdles is limited AI literacy across clinical and
operational leadership. Without a shared understanding of capabilities, limitations, and
governance safeguards, decision-makers may either overestimate risk or place inappropriate trust
in system outputs. Structured education initiatives, including executive briefings, clinician
training modules, and competency frameworks, can significantly reduce hesitation and improve
responsible use.
Another barrier is the absence of standardized internal evaluation pathways. Many
institutions apply procurement processes designed for traditional medical devices to AI software
that may iterate more rapidly. This can lead to prolonged approval cycles and inconsistent review
criteria. Establishing streamlined AI review committees with defined evaluation frameworks,
risk-tiering protocols, and clear escalation pathways would improve efficiency while maintaining
safety oversight.
Budget constraints also play a significant role. AI deployment requires investment in
integration, monitoring infrastructure, cybersecurity controls, and ongoing governance. Without
reimbursement alignment or shared savings incentives, these investments may compete
unfavorably with other priorities such as staffing, EHR modernization, or cybersecurity
upgrades. Linking AI adoption to value-based care metrics and outcome improvements is
therefore essential to support internal business justification.
Workflow redesign represents an additional challenge. AI systems can inadvertently
reinforce inefficient processes if underlying documentation or care pathways are poorly
structured. Successful adoption requires parallel workflow optimization and change management
efforts to ensure that AI augments rather than disrupts clinical operations.
Sustained AI adoption in healthcare requires clear, measurable ROI, including revenue
improvement, cost reduction, efficiency gains, throughput, quality, and safety outcomes.
Standardized KPIs and baseline benchmarking are essential to link deployment to value; without
demonstrable impact, initiatives rarely scale beyond pilots.

concerned with the technical architecture of models and more concerned with whether AI
meaningfully improves access, accuracy, timeliness, and coordination of care. Many patients
wish to see AI address persistent systemic challenges, including long wait times for specialty
care, delayed diagnoses, fragmented medical records, administrative burden, and inconsistent

deterioration, improved treatment personalization, and better alignment across multidisciplinary

Patients are also interested in more personalized and proactive care. AI has the potential to

technological sophistication but improved outcomes, clearer communication, and a more
responsive care experience.
At the same time, significant concerns remain. Transparency is paramount: patients want
understandable explanations of how AI-informed recommendations are generated and what their
limitations are. Equity is another major concern, particularly the risk that models trained on
non-representative data could reinforce disparities. Privacy and data use practices must be
clearly governed and communicated, especially regarding secondary use of health information.

the human relationship in care and maintaining clear accountability when errors occur are
essential to sustaining trust. Ultimately, adoption will depend on whether AI demonstrably
improves outcomes and experience while maintaining transparency, fairness, privacy, and
meaningful human oversight.
10.Research Priorities (RFI Question #10)
HHS should prioritize AI research areas that directly strengthen clinical decision-making
by connecting upstream biomedical evidence to point-of-care actions. A particularly high-
impact area is AI-enabled integration of the full therapeutic lifecycle, from preclinical discovery
through regulatory approval and post-market evidence, into clinically usable intelligence.
Advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) and Agentic AI make it technically feasible to
compress and contextualize vast bodies of heterogeneous data (e.g., molecular mechanisms, New
Alternative Methodologies (NAMs), clinical trial outcomes, and regulatory submissions) and
surface them at the moment a clinician is selecting among therapeutic options. Prioritizing
research that enables traceability and explainability across this lifecycle would help clinicians
move beyond trial-and-error prescribing toward more informed decisions, reducing avoidable
treatment failures and downstream harm.
HHS should also invest in AI research focused on agentic AI systems with long-term
memory, reasoning, and workflow orchestration for clinical care. Unlike static decision
support tools, agentic systems can autonomously synthesize patient-specific data with evolving
scientific evidence, guidelines, and real-world outcomes. The complexity and scale of data
generated by modern drug development, particularly human-relevant NAMs and multi-omic
cellular models, exceed what individual clinicians can reasonably internalize. Research into
agentic architectures that can reason across these data sources, maintain provenance, and adapt

11.Conclusion
AI will not scale in clinical care through technical advancement alone. Sustainable adoption
requires coordinated reform across reimbursement policy, regulatory oversight, interoperability
standards, governance structures, and workforce readiness. When these structural enablers are
aligned, AI can improve diagnostic accuracy, personalize treatment selection, reduce
administrative burden, and strengthen care coordination, while maintaining safety, equity, and
transparency.

with HHS through discovery, user-centered design, and proof of value to help shape, test, and
scale solutions in a way that is grounded in real-world needs and delivers measurable impact

patient-centered AI ecosystem that strengthens clinical care delivery nationwide while upholding
the highest standards of safety, equity, and public trust.
