Response to HHS RFI:
Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical
Care
Strategic Summary
This submission responds to the HHS Request for Information by outlining a decision-
architecture approach designed to operate under conditions of high uncertainty, fragmented
data, and accelerated policy timelines. The approach focuses on structuring multi-domain inputs
into transparent decision pathways that preserve traceability, governance, and human oversight
while increasing decision velocity and outcome confidence. It is modular, non-prescriptive, and
intended to augment—not replace—existing HHS systems, processes, and authorities.
Submission Context
This response reflects cross-sector experience designing, governing, and evaluating AI-enabled
decision systems in complex, regulated healthcare environments. The comments focus on
adoption at scale—specifically the governance, evaluation, and incentive structures required to
move clinical AI from pilots into routine care while preserving trust, safety, and fiscal integrity.
Framing Statement (Decision Spine)
Clinical AI is not constrained by technical capability; it is constrained by decision governance.
Adoption at scale requires continuous accountability, auditable decision-making, and alignment
across regulation, reimbursement, evaluation, and clinical workflows.
Primary Levers (1–3)
• Governance: Clarify accountability for non-device AI through continuous oversight
expectations.
• Evaluation: Normalize post-deployment monitoring as operational infrastructure, not a one-
time event.
• Incentives: Align reimbursement with monitored, real-world performance to sustain adoption
and protect program integrity.
Observed Barriers to Adoption
The most significant barriers are governance ambiguity, workflow disruption, and incentive
misalignment rather than model performance. Providers lack clear expectations for liability,
auditability, post-deployment monitoring, and human oversight for non-device AI. This
uncertainty increases administrative burden, slows internal decision-making, and keeps AI
trapped in pilots.
In practice, fragmented governance often results in duplicative reviews across compliance,
legal, IT, and clinical leadership—adding weeks or months to adoption timelines. AI is also
frequently introduced as a parallel system rather than embedded within existing workflows,
increasing documentation time and clinician resistance.
Regulatory, Reimbursement, and Programmatic Actions
HHS can accelerate adoption by clarifying acceptable governance patterns for non-device AI
rather than regulating specific technologies. Guidance should define baseline expectations for
human oversight, documentation and traceability of AI-influenced decisions, post-deployment
monitoring, and conditions for rollback or suspension.

On reimbursement, HHS could pilot payment pathways explicitly tied to monitored outcomes
and operational performance, reducing the risk of waste, fraud, and abuse while rewarding
demonstrated value. Programmatically, HHS should emphasize implementation science and
real-world evaluation infrastructure alongside innovation.
Governance, Liability, Privacy, and Security for Non-Device AI
Non-device AI often influences care decisions without fitting existing device frameworks,
creating gaps in accountability and oversight. Challenges include unclear responsibility for
outcomes, insufficient audit trails, limited standardized monitoring, and patient uncertainty
regarding consent and recourse.
A decision-centric governance approach—focused on traceability, continuous monitoring,
defined human authority boundaries, and privacy-by-design—can close these gaps without
constraining innovation.
Evaluation and Monitoring as Infrastructure
Pre-deployment testing is necessary but insufficient. Safe scale requires continuous post-
deployment monitoring, including performance drift detection, outcome tracking, bias and equity
monitoring, and workflow impact measurement.
For example, a clinical decision-support system that performs well at deployment may drift as
patient populations, coding practices, or care protocols change. Continuous monitoring enables
early detection, targeted retraining, or suspension before harm or reimbursement risk occurs.
HHS can catalyze adoption by standardizing evaluation frameworks and shared metrics that
make ongoing monitoring a norm rather than an exception.
Certification, Accreditation, and Credentialing
Process-based certification is more scalable than model-specific approval. HHS can support
accreditation of governance and monitoring practices, certification of evaluation and audit
processes, and credentialing focused on operational readiness. This approach preserves
innovation while ensuring consistent safeguards across care settings.
Interoperability and Benchmarking
Interoperability efforts should prioritize benchmarking and evaluation, not just data exchange.
Standardized outcome metrics, comparable evaluation datasets, and common reporting
structures for post-deployment monitoring will expand markets, reduce friction, and accelerate
learning across systems.
Patient and Provider Perspectives
Patients and caregivers seek transparency into how AI influences care, meaningful consent,
assurance of privacy, and clear recourse when errors occur. Providers want tools that reduce
burden rather than add parallel workflows—measurably decreasing documentation time, review
cycles, and administrative overhead.
Trust increases when AI supports—rather than replaces—human decision-making and
accountability remains with care teams.

Research Priorities
HHS research investments should prioritize implementation science, real-world evaluation, and
governance models over incremental model novelty. High-impact areas include post-
deployment monitoring methods, human–AI interaction in workflows, governance for non-device
AI, and mechanisms aligning reimbursement with monitored outcomes.
Closing Statement
HHS’s greatest leverage is not selecting technologies, but shaping the governance and
incentive environment that allows trustworthy AI to move from pilots to policy to execution —
safely, measurably, and at scale.
Key Takeaways
• Problem: Clinical AI adoption stalls due to governance ambiguity, administrative burden, and
misaligned incentives.
• Core Insight: Decision-centric governance with continuous evaluation and outcome-aligned
reimbursement.
• Result: Safe, scalable, and fiscally responsible AI adoption in clinical care.
