About the Miami AI Club (MAIC)
The Miami AI Club (MAIC) is a not-for-profit multi-stakeholder convening platform dedicated to
advancing responsible, human-centered artificial intelligence across healthcare, education, and
public-sector systems. MAIC brings together clinicians, health system executives, researchers,
technologists, policymakers, investors, and patient advocates to examine real-world AI
deployment challenges and opportunities.
Through its Healthcare Task Force, MAIC focuses specifically on the safe, effective, and
accountable integration of AI into clinical care environments. Discussions emphasize
implementation science, governance frameworks, interoperability, clinician workflow integration,
reimbursement alignment, and public trust.
MAIC does not represent a single vendor or commercial interest. Rather, it serves as a neutral
forum for cross-sector dialogue and practical deployment insights. The recommendations in this
submission reflect collective observations from real-world AI implementation environments, with
particular attention to regulatory clarity, incentive alignment, accountability infrastructure, and
patient-centered outcomes.
Cross-Cutting Observations
Across clinical settings, the primary barriers to AI adoption are not a lack of innovation or technical
capability, but uncertainty, fragmentation, and limited trust in real-world deployment.
AI adoption in healthcare is multi-pronged. Successful use depends on alignment among
incentives, governance structures, clinical workflows, payment structures, and data
environments. When these elements are not aligned, even technically strong tools struggle to
scale.
Trust remains central. Clinicians and patients must be confident in how AI tools are evaluated,
how decisions are attributed, how data are protected, and how human oversight is maintained.
Clear expectations and shared frameworks can accelerate adoption more effectively than volume
of regulation alone.
Fragmentation across stakeholders, data systems, and evaluation approaches slows collective
learning. Policies that encourage interoperability, real-world validation, and coordination can
unlock safe and meaningful adoption.
The following responses reflect practical deployment experience and focus on enabling
trustworthy, outcome-driven AI in clinical care.

Regulation
AI is advancing more rapidly than our readiness to deploy it safely and consistently in clinical
settings. Numerous applications enter the market daily, yet adoption of AI in clinical care lags far
behind its use in administrative functions and other industries. This gap is driven less by lack of
innovation and more by lack of regulatory clarity, predictable guardrails, and trusted deployment
frameworks.
At present, the national approach to clinical AI governance remains fragmented and unevenly
understood. The absence of clear interoperability expectations, evaluation standards,
accountability structures, and risk-sharing mechanisms reduces confidence among clinicians and
health systems. Addressing these gaps can accelerate adoption while preserving safety and public
trust.
As members of the Miami AI Club (MAIC), we regularly convene organizations that are building,
buying, evaluating, investing in, and using AI tools in clinical environments. The recommendations
below reflect practical, real-world deployment experience:
1. Move Toward a Uniform, Predictable Regulatory Framework
A patchwork of federal guidance and emerging state-level rules creates uncertainty for innovators
and implementers. Greater national alignment and clarity would reduce hesitation and encourage
responsible investment. A predictable, risk-tiered framework proportionate to clinical impact
would allow stakeholders to understand expectations while preserving flexibility for innovation.
Clarity often accelerates safe adoption more than additional regulation.
2. Advance Interoperability as Foundational Infrastructure
Clinical data remain fragmented across EHRs, claims, labs, pharmacies, genomics platforms, and
consumer sources. AI performance and safety depend heavily on data completeness and context.
Encouraging enforceable interoperability standards and aligned incentives would unlock higher-
quality AI development and more equitable performance across populations. Interoperability is
not only a technical issue but also an incentive and coordination issue.
3. Support Shared Evaluation and Risk Frameworks
Vendors and health systems lack common frameworks for assessing safety, performance, and risk.
This creates uncertainty for decision-makers and slows vendor selection and deployment. Existing
industry efforts, such as those from the Coalition for Health AI (CHAI) and others, provide useful
starting points. HHS could recognize and build upon such work to encourage shared evaluation
approaches without mandating a single methodology. Clear expectations for pre-deployment
testing, post-deployment monitoring, and lifecycle oversight would foster trust while allowing
adaptive AI systems to improve over time.
4. Encourage Development of AI Risk-Sharing and Insurance Models

AI is probabilistic by nature. Risk can be reduced but not eliminated. Today, many health systems
remain cautious because liability and insurance models for AI-related risk are immature.
Encouraging dialogue with insurers and exploring incentive structures for AI risk underwriting
could reduce adoption barriers. Clearer accountability and risk-sharing frameworks would
support responsible deployment.
5. Promote Data Transparency
Bias and uneven performance remain legitimate concerns. Transparency around training and
testing data, including demographics, geography, and clinical context, helps clinicians assess
applicability and limitations. Emerging 'nutrition label' style disclosures are a promising approach.
HHS could encourage transparency norms that support equity and informed decision-making
without exposing proprietary information.
6. Strengthen Trust Infrastructure: Identity, Authentication, and Accountability
Regulation often focuses on data and algorithms but less on the infrastructure surrounding AI use.
Clinical AI increasingly influences decisions, documentation, and workflows. Yet there are no
consistent expectations for verifying clinician identity during AI use; ensuring correct patient-
context matching; tracking who accepted or modified AI recommendations; and maintaining
reliable audit trails of human-AI interactions. Without strong identity, authentication, and
accountability mechanisms, liability frameworks remain difficult to operationalize. Privacy-
preserving identity assurance and auditable human-AI interaction records should be viewed as
safety infrastructure, not optional features.
7. Enable Regulatory-Safe Pilot Environments
Innovation is slowed by uncertainty around acceptable experimentation. Regulatory-safe pilot
environments could allow health systems to test AI tools under defined guardrails, enabling real-
world learning while managing risk. Such pilots can surface risks earlier, generate practical
evidence, inform future rulemaking, and encourage responsible innovation.
8. Play a Convening Role to Reduce Fragmentation
A major barrier to adoption is fragmentation among stakeholders. Hospitals, developers,
researchers, and policymakers often operate in silos, limiting shared learning. HHS is well
positioned to serve as a convener to align stakeholders, share deployment lessons, and highlight
best practices without adding regulatory burden. Improved coordination can accelerate adoption
as much as formal regulation.
Summary: Effective clinical AI regulation should emphasize clarity, predictability, proportional
oversight, and trust-building infrastructure. Aligning incentives, supporting interoperability, and
encouraging shared learning can accelerate safe adoption while maintaining public confidence.

Reimbursement
HHS payment policies strongly shape how care is delivered and which innovations are adopted.
While AI has the potential to enhance productivity, reduce burden, and improve outcomes,
current reimbursement structures often do not reward these benefits.
Traditional fee-for-service (FFS) models emphasize volume over outcomes and can inadvertently
discourage adoption of technologies that improve efficiency or reduce unnecessary services. As a
result, even high-value AI tools may struggle to achieve sustainable adoption. Aligning payment
policy with measurable clinical and operational value can accelerate responsible AI use in clinical
care.
1. Align Reimbursement With Outcomes and Productivity
AI should not be reimbursed simply for being used. It should be rewarded when it demonstrably
improves patient outcomes, reduces avoidable utilization, enhances diagnostic accuracy, enables
earlier interventions, reduces clinician administrative burden, or improves care coordination.
Outcome-aligned payment and value-based models are better suited to capture AI’s benefits than
FFS structures.
2. Recognize Clinician Time and Workflow Impact
Many AI tools require clinician supervision, prompting, and validation. These activities carry real
opportunity costs. If AI increases clinician workload or cognitive burden, adoption will remain
limited regardless of technical performance. Payment models that recognize workflow efficiency
and clinician time savings can better reflect the true value of AI-enabled care.
3. Encourage Evidence-Generating Reimbursement Models
AI systems improve over time and may require real-world learning. Traditional reimbursement
models often expect mature evidence upfront. HHS could explore coverage-with-evidence-
development approaches, pilot payment models, or time-limited reimbursement pathways that
allow responsible testing and validation in real-world settings.
4. Reward System-Level Impact, Not Just Tool Adoption
The greatest value from AI often occurs at the system level rather than the individual encounter.
Examples include reduced readmissions, improved population health management, earlier
disease detection, more efficient resource allocation, better medication adherence, and
improved equity and access. Payment models that reward system-level outcomes can better
capture AI’s value than encounter-based reimbursement.
5. Support Physician-Led Evidence and Clinical Validation
Physician-led evidence and peer-reviewed validation strongly influence adoption. Encouraging
clinical leadership in AI evaluation and deployment increases trust and accelerates uptake.

6. Maintain Guardrails Against Overuse and 'AI for AI’s Sake'
Reimbursement policy should avoid incentivizing unnecessary AI deployment or low-value use.
Clear expectations for demonstrated value, safety, and equity can prevent waste while supporting

Summary: Modernized payment policy can be a powerful lever for responsible AI adoption.
Aligning reimbursement with outcomes, productivity, and workflow improvement rather than
mere utilization will encourage deployment of AI that benefits patients, clinicians, and the

Research and Development
HHS supports one of the world’s largest health research ecosystems and plays a critical role in
translating innovation into clinical impact. In AI, the challenge is not only invention but also safe,
scalable implementation.
Effective R&D policy should focus on enabling applied research, real-world validation, and shared
learning that accelerates responsible adoption across the healthcare system.
1. Prioritize Applied and Implementation-Focused Research
Many AI tools show promise in controlled settings but face challenges in clinical workflows.
Greater emphasis on implementation science, workflow integration research, human-AI
collaboration models, and deployment in diverse clinical environments can help close the gap
between prototype and practice.
2. Invest in High-Quality, Privacy-Preserving Datasets
Standardized, well-governed datasets are foundational for safe and equitable AI. HHS can support
creation of representative, high-quality datasets; privacy-preserving data-sharing frameworks;
de-identification best practices; mechanisms for patient consent and transparency; and inclusion
of diverse populations to reduce bias.
3. Support Real-World Testing and Pilot Networks
Safe real-world testing environments are critical for learning. HHS should encourage multi-site
pilot programs, structured real-world evaluations, post-deployment monitoring networks, and
cross-institution learning collaboratives.
4. Encourage Public-Private and Multi-Stakeholder Collaboration

AI progress depends on coordination among health systems, researchers, developers, patient
communities, and standards organizations. HHS can act as a facilitator of collaboration without
directing specific outcomes.
5. Consider a Lightweight National Coordination Forum
A recurring barrier in U.S. healthcare AI is fragmentation across stakeholders. HHS should consider
encouraging a lightweight, multi-stakeholder coordination forum focused on practical
deployment and shared learning to share real-world lessons, surface safety and implementation
challenges early, identify emerging best practices, and encourage alignment around evaluation
approaches.
6. Incorporate International Lessons
Global experience suggests that clear policy direction reduces hesitation; coordinated ecosystems
accelerate learning; safe pilot environments enable responsible experimentation; data liquidity
supports innovation; and outcome-focused deployment encourages pragmatism.
Summary: R&D policy is most impactful when it supports shared infrastructure, applied research,
and real-world validation. By enabling collaboration, data access, and safe pilots, HHS can catalyze
innovation without needing to select specific technologies or vendors.

1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health

The primary barriers to private sector innovation and adoption of AI in clinical care are not a lack

environments. Key barriers include:
Regulatory Fragmentation and Uncertainty
A patchwork of federal guidance and emerging state-level regulations creates uncertainty for
developers and health systems. Predictable, risk-tiered regulatory clarity would reduce hesitation
and enable responsible innovation.
Lack of Shared Risk, Evaluation, and Monitoring Standards
There are no commonly accepted frameworks for risk assessment and mitigation, ongoing
performance monitoring, real-world validation, and lifecycle oversight of adaptive AI systems.
Without shared standards, vendors struggle to demonstrate safety and health systems struggle
to evaluate it.
Immature Liability and Insurance Models

Liability and accountability remain unresolved when AI contributes to clinical decisions, and AI-

Economic and Workflow Realities in Clinical Settings
For AI to be adopted, it must fit clinical workflow and demonstrate real value. Many tools require
clinician supervision, prompting, and validation; if AI increases cognitive or administrative burden,
adoption declines.
Data Fragmentation and Interoperability Gaps
Clinical data remain siloed across EHRs, claims systems, labs, pharmacies, and consumer sources.

Trust, Identity, and Accountability Infrastructure Gaps

3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise
novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and
accountability structures (e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and
security). What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any,
should HHS play to help address them?
The use of AI in clinical care introduces legal and implementation questions that existing
governance frameworks were not designed to fully address. These issues benefit from clearer
guidance and updated interpretation in AI-specific contexts. Key issues include:
Attribution and Liability in AI-Assisted Decisions
Responsibility can become ambiguous when AI contributes to clinical decision-making. Clear
expectations for documentation, human oversight, and attribution can help clarify accountability
without removing clinician judgment.
Governance of Adaptive and Continuously Learning Systems
Continuously learning systems raise questions about when revalidation is required, how drift is
monitored, and how updates are governed and documented.
Transparency and Representativeness of Training and Testing Data
Performance varies across populations and settings. Encouraging transparent disclosure of data
characteristics and limitations supports informed use and reduces unintended disparities.
Movement of Data Between HIPAA and Non-HIPAA Contexts
AI can involve data flows between covered entities and consumer technologies, creating
ambiguity around secondary use and responsibilities for safeguarding data.
Identity, Authentication, and Auditability
Implementation requires reliable attribution of actions, correct patient context, and audit trails
of human-AI interactions. HHS can encourage standards that strengthen attribution and
auditability.
Misrepresentation of AI Capabilities
Overstating AI performance can create legal and safety risks; evidence-based claims and
transparency around limitations protect patients and providers.
Summary: Most legal and implementation challenges stem from ambiguity rather than absence
of law. Clearer guidance on attribution, data governance, lifecycle oversight, and transparency
can enable safe adoption while maintaining accountability and public trust.

4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods
(pre- and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and
human-centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further
support these processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most impactful (e.g.,
contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or prize competitions)?
Effective evaluation of clinical AI requires both technical validation and real-world assessment.
Model performance alone is insufficient; evaluation must reflect how AI functions in clinical
environments and how humans interact with it. Promising approaches include:
Risk-Based Evaluation Frameworks
Evaluation should be proportional to clinical impact and potential harm, incorporating patient
safety impact, consequences of failure, population vulnerability, environment of use, and equity
considerations. HHS can encourage convergence around shared principles without mandating a
single framework.
Human-in-the-Loop and Workflow Evaluation
Evaluation should assess cognitive load, alert fatigue, usability and workflow integration,
supervision burden, and ability for timely human intervention.
Real-World Performance Monitoring
Ongoing evaluation should include post-deployment monitoring, drift detection, periodic
revalidation, and performance across diverse populations and settings.
Transparency in Training and Testing Data
Evaluation should consider data representativeness, demographic coverage, geographic diversity,
and clinical context of testing.
Auditability and Traceability
Frameworks should include the ability to trace how outputs were generated, review decision
pathways, and maintain records of human-AI interaction.
HHS Support: Yes. Impactful mechanisms include targeted grants for evaluation research,
cooperative agreements supporting multi-site validation, prize challenges encouraging robust
testing methods, and support for post-deployment learning networks.
Summary: The most effective evaluation approaches combine technical performance, human
factors, and real-world monitoring. Supporting shared evaluation principles and real-world
learning can accelerate safe adoption while maintaining flexibility for innovation.

5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation,
certification, industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative
and effective AI use in clinical care?
HHS can enable private sector validation and accountability while avoiding overly prescriptive
certification structures that could slow innovation. The most effective support would focus on
clarity, alignment, and shared learning rather than centralized control:
Encourage Voluntary, Industry-Led Standards
Recognize credible industry-led frameworks, encourage transparency and comparability, and
highlight best practices.
Support Independent Real-World Evaluation
Encourage third-party testing and benchmarking, multi-site validation studies, and real-world
performance reporting.
Promote Transparency and Disclosure Norms
Encourage standardized disclosures around intended use, data representativeness, known
limitations, and appropriate supervision levels.
Integrate AI Competencies Into Clinical Training
Encourage AI literacy and competency development among clinicians and administrators to
improve safe use and informed procurement.
Reduce Structural Barriers to Responsible Innovation
Promote interoperability, clarify data governance expectations, support risk-tiered oversight, and
encourage evidence generation.
Summary: The most effective role for HHS is to enable trust, transparency, and shared learning
while allowing market-driven validation to evolve. Light-touch support for independent
evaluation and disclosure norms can promote safe innovation without unnecessary regulatory
burden.

Closing
MAIC appreciates the opportunity to provide input. The policy levers most likely to accelerate safe
adoption are those that reduce uncertainty, strengthen interoperability and trust infrastructure,
align incentives with outcomes, and enable real-world learning. We welcome continued
engagement to support shared learning and responsible deployment.
As AI adoption accelerates, establishing durable trust infrastructure will be as important as
advancing model capability.
Through its Healthcare Task Force, MAIC is continuing structured dialogue and implementation-
focused collaboration to translate policy guidance into practical adoption frameworks. We
welcome continued engagement with HHS and other stakeholders to support safe, accountable,
and scalable AI deployment.
