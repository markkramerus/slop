Request for Information
Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care
RIN 0955-AA13
Rubrum Advising appreciates the opportunity to comment on the Department of Health and
Human Services (HHS) Request for Information on “Accelerating the Adoption and Use of
Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care”. Rubrum Advising, co‑founded by Lee A. Fleisher,
MD, ML, and Matthew A. Fleisher, JD, MPH, advises clients on the adoption, diffusion, and
reimbursement of innovative healthcare solutions and products to improve patient outcomes
and reduce total costs of care within the context of regulation, safety, quality, and clinical
standards.
What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
i. Fragmented, low-quality data that make it hard to build safe models that are applicable to a
wide range of populations and situations.
a. There is growing concern that non-representative data lead to the creation of inaccurate
algorithms, resulting in poorer performance in certain patient groups. To ensure
algorithmic safety, datasets need to be appropriately representative of their intended
use population.1
b. Since nationwide exchange of health information remains fragmented, developers and
health systems struggle to assemble sufficiently representative, longitudinal datasets for
training and external validation. Even with exchange networks in place, important
clinical information often remains in clinician notes or non-standardized fields, making it
difficult to extract relevant data. Moreover, companies may be reluctant to share the
data sets used to train an AI algorithm, given concerns that competitors could reverse
engineer the data to create their own tools.2 The use of AI tools requires a high level of
specificity that has not yet been fully reconciled with the realities of current
documentation and record-keeping practices.
c. As private entities increasingly aggregate, analyze, and sell large-scale clinical datasets,
the expansion of commercial data markets introduces important policy considerations
related to transparency, representativeness, and access. In many cases, there is limited
visibility into how these datasets are constructed, validated, and maintained, which
creates challenges for both regulators and developers seeking to assess data quality,
completeness, and potential bias. The growing commercialization of clinical data also
raises fundamental questions regarding data stewardship and ownership, including
whether proprietary rights should rest with the organizations that collect, process, and

ii. Clinician and patient skepticism of “black-box” tools
a. The “black box” nature of deep neural networks and other advanced algorithms can
make it nearly impossible for clinicians to interpret how the system arrives at specific
recommendations.3 While many studies report gains in AI-based diagnostic efficiency
and resource allocation, accountability and liability remain pervasive concerns,
especially when models lack clear rationale or interpretability.
b. An AMA survey from November 2024 found that although physician sentiment and use
of AI is trending positive, physicians continue to express concerns about the design of
health AI tools and the potential for flawed AI-enabled tools to put privacy at risk,
integrate poorly with EHR systems, offer incorrect conclusions or recommendations, and
introduce new liability concerns. Nearly half of physicians surveyed also ranked
increased oversight as the number one regulatory action that needed to happen to
increase their trust in adopting AI tools.4
1. Under current malpractice law, liability is determined based on the “reasonable
physician under similar circumstances” standard. Regardless of whether AI was
used, courts will judge the conduct of the physician. As of now, there is no doctrine
assigning shared responsibility to AI systems, even when their recommendations
directly influence patient care.5
c. Recent surveys suggest that many patients lack confidence in health care systems’ ability
to use AI responsibly and protect them from AI-related harms. One study found that
most respondents reported low trust in their health care system to use AI responsibly
(65.8%) and low trust that their health care system would make sure an AI tool would
not harm them (57.7%).6 Patients also generally prefer explainable AI, even if that means
a trade-off in accuracy compared with black-box models.7
d. Given these ongoing concerns about accountability, liability, and trust, strengthening
instructions-for-use (IFU) and implementation education can also play an important role

iii. Unclear pathways for CMS coverage
a. MedPAC has noted that software technologies are frequently bundled into existing
payments and that payments for SaaS (software as a service) have to meet specific
conditions (e.g. is FDA-approved, fits into a Medicare benefit category, is reasonable and
necessary) for reimbursement. This can make it challenging for developers to secure
predictable reimbursement, especially when they must also negotiate separately with
commercial insurers and creates particular challenges for tools that primarily deliver
general risk reduction, utilization avoidance, or workflow efficiencies rather than a
discrete billable service.9
b. These challenges are compounded by the central role of the AMA in maintaining CPT
codes, which remain the primary mechanism for payment under Medicare and most
commercial plans. Many AI-enabled services lack dedicated CPT codes or fit poorly
within existing categories, and even when new codes are established, they may not be
tied to a clear Medicare benefit category or coverage pathway, limiting providers’ ability

What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or
programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
Please provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
i. Create clear coverage for AI interventions that enable safer, more timely care rather than
increasing volume
a. CMS could prioritize payment models that reward reductions in avoidable admissions,
better chronic disease management, fewer diagnostic errors, and improved access to
care, such as proposed in the ACCESS model, which is explicitly designed to expand
technology-supported chronic care using an outcome-aligned payment approach.
b. CMS could also build a clearer reimbursement pathway for software/AI that results in
clinical improvements and should continue to publish instructions and guidelines for
differentiating AI as its own clinical service, as an administrative service, or as part of an
existing service.
ii. Physicians should not be paid more just because AI tools make them more efficient
a. In the CY 2026 Physician Fee Schedule (PFS) final rule, CMS finalized a -2.5% efficiency
adjustment to select services to better recognize that some services are likely to become
more efficient over time, as compared to time-based services like office visits or
behavioral health therapy. The adjustment targeted services like diagnostic imaging
interpretation, outpatient interventions, interventional pain management, and
orthopedic services, which have benefited from technological advancements and
standardized workflows that reduce time and resource use, without corresponding
payment adjustments.11

b. Similarly, where AI leads to greater efficiency or streamlines the provision of care, CMS
could treat this increased productivity as a deflationary effect and apply an efficiency
adjustment to services that AI is expected to streamline. Aligning payments with the
actual resources required to provide the AI-enabled services would ensure that
payments are not higher simply due to efficiency gains, while still allowing AI tools that
measurably improve outcomes or reduce downstream costs to be supported through
outcome-based or model-specific payments.
iii. Embed AI-related safety expectations in Medicare quality programs and innovation models
a. Existing and newly launched quality and payment programs, such as the Quality
Payment Program (QPP) and the Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS), as well
as Innovation Center models like ACCESS, can incorporate AI governance where AI is
used for clinical decision-making, including regular oversight, risk assessment, and
training requirements tied to the safe use of AI tools.
iv. Use demonstration projects to test payment models that explicitly fund AI tools that prevent
hospitalizations or support high-risk, high-need patients at home
a. As stated above, the new CMS ACCESS model is designed to expand technology-
supported chronic care using an outcome-aligned payment approach and could tie in AI-
enabled tools that support care for high-needs patients and emphasize preventative care
to avoid hospitalizations. We strongly support both the construct of the ACCESS model
and the model’s use of well-defined endpoints and transparency of results. Future
demonstration projects should be structured so that payments reflect both outcomes
and appropriate efficiency gains, rather than creating stand-alone fees for AI that do not
change care delivery.
For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel legal
and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures
(e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
i. Require strong privacy and governance standards for patient-generated data from consumer
devices used in clinical care, including safeguards against discriminatory secondary uses of
sensitive information.
a. Data from non-medical devices, such as wearables and fitness/wellness apps,
increasingly informs clinical decision-making when patients share this information with
their providers, yet it is often collected and stored outside HIPAA’s regulatory
framework. Once incorporated into clinical records, these data can influence care and
outcomes, making robust privacy and security safeguards essential. These systems
should require explicit, informed patient consent for any sale or secondary use of data
and provide enhanced protections and remedies when those protections are violated.
HHS should prioritize extending clear protections and governance standards to patient-
generated data from consumer devices to ensure that information used in clinical care is
subject to consistent privacy, security, and accountability requirements.

For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and post-
deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered
evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so,
which mechanisms would be most impactful ( e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative
agreements, and/or prize competitions)?
i. Shift from pre- to post-market surveillance
a. Pre-market evaluation has worked reasonably well for physical devices and traditional
software with stable, predictable outputs, but it is poorly matched to AI systems whose
behavior evolves with data, context, and implementation over time. Rather than relying
primarily on pre-deployment testing, HHS (through FDA and ONC) should support a risk-
based, post-market surveillance framework for clinical AI that emphasizes: mandatory
adverse event and error reporting, outcome monitoring in real-world use, structured
identification of implementation issues, and periodic performance re-evaluation. The
goal should be to avoid both under-regulation that allows mass injury and over-
regulation that delays access to beneficial technologies, while minimizing compliance
burden and total cost of ownership for providers and developers. This framework should
be targeted to higher-risk clinical use cases and AI systems whose design or training
makes output unpredictability likely, so that oversight resources are focused where
patient harm risk is highest.12
ii. Fund real-world evaluation via grants, cooperative agreements
a. HHS could use grants, cooperative agreements, and contracts to support public-private
evaluation partnerships, where developers receive de-identified post-market
performance data and providers can safely continue using AI tools in higher-risk
scenarios under structured monitoring arrangements.
b. HHS should also create a national, registry-style, federated post-market surveillance
network for clinical AI, modeled in part on the FDA Sentinel Initiative’s distribution data
infrastructure, to aggregate outcome data and support standardized reporting of errors,
adverse events, and key performance metrics. Such an aggregated outcome data registry
would enable early detection of safety issues across health systems and provide
feedback loops for regulators, developers, and providers, without requiring continuous,
centralized storage of identifiable patient data.

Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the
greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help
reduce costs?
I. Prioritize development and payment for AI tools that demonstrably change care delivery in
ways that improve outcomes and reduce downstream costs, rather than tools that solely
increase clinician efficiency
a. AI tools have met or exceeded performance and cost expectations primarily where they
have demonstrably reduced downstream utilization while maintaining or improving
outcomes, rather than merely increasing clinician efficiency. Examples include models
that prospectively identify patients at high risk for sepsis or avoidable readmission, tools
that reduce unnecessary imaging or procedures, and decision-support systems that
lower complication rates or emergency department revisits; in these cases, the value
proposition is clear because payers and providers can observe fewer admissions, repeat
tests, or adverse events, and thus lower the total cost of care. By contrast, tools that
mainly make clinicians faster or generate documentation more efficiently, without clear
evidence of changed care patterns or improved outcomes, have fallen short of
expectations from a coverage and payment perspective, because the financial return
largely accrues to efficiency gains that purchasers can already value without separate
reimbursement. Consistent with emerging payment principles for AI, the novel tools
with the greatest potential are those that can rigorously demonstrate that they change
clinical decisions and care pathways in ways that improve outcomes and reduce
downstream costs (for example, earlier targeted intervention in high-risk chronic
conditions, reduced low-value care, or fewer complications), and that can be
incorporated into payment models that reward measurable improvements in total cost
and quality rather than efficiency alone.

I. Enhance interoperability for structured clinical data (LOINC, USCDI)
a. LOINC-coded results already underpin many digital quality measures and FHIR US Core
profiles (for example, the US Core Blood Pressure profile requires fixed LOINC codes for
systolic and diastolic values), which shows how standardized codes can make data
predictable, computable, and reusable for AI and quality measurement. However,
misalignment between laboratory information systems and EHR data elements, in
addition to the use of scanned reports rather than discrete results, still leads to missing
values, nonspecific codes, and loss of clinical context that limit AI model performance.
ONC/ASTP should promote a national LOINC reference implementation for diagnostic
tests, tied to USCDI and FHIR implementation guides, so that laboratories, EHRs, and AI
developers can reliably exchange test-level data needed for training, validation, and
benchmarking.
II. Interoperability and fair access for AI tools under TEFCA
a. To improve interoperability, ONC/ASTP should create a LOINC reference database for
diagnostic tests and standardize use among diagnostic manufacturers. TEFCA and related
nationwide networks are beginning to support exchange of USCDI data classes across
organizations, but without attention to governance, large incumbents or QHINs could
effectively gatekeep data flows needed by third-party AI developers. HHS should ensure
that TEFCA implementation includes clear requirements that certified APIs and exchange
frameworks provide non-discriminatory technical access to standardized data for
authorized AI tools, prevent exclusive or preferential access arrangements that
disadvantage smaller innovators, and propagate guidance on consistent use of SNOMED
CT, ICD, and CPT for diagnoses and procedures across clinical documentation, payment,
and reporting.
III. Making unstructured data more usable
a. Much clinically rich information remains in unstructured formats, such as scanned PDFs,
narrative notes, or loosely structured “other” fields, that are not consistently mapped to
USCDI elements or standard terminologies, limiting their value for AI development and
evaluation. HHS should support implementation guides, incentives, and technical
assistance to move key data elements from scanned or free-text formats into discrete,
coded fields aligned with USCDI and FHIR profiles, while encouraging tools that can
safely structure legacy unstructured content without degrading accuracy.

have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?

a. Access to specialist-level care for individuals with complex or rare diseases in rural
hospitals
1. Access to specialist-level care for individuals with complex or rare diseases in rural
hospitals can be strengthened through appropriate use of AI-enabled tools. These

tools can help reallocate limited clinical and operational resources toward more
complex cases in resource-constrained settings, allowing clinicians to focus their time

prediction of rare diseases in rural areas, particularly where local access to relevant
specialists is limited, so that patients receive timely and appropriate referrals to
higher levels of care.
b. Help navigating complex regimens, symptoms, and appointments
1. Patients and physicians report greater trust in efficiency-based and administrative
applications, as well as in tools that offer explanations and are more transparent over
black-box tools.
c. Patient and caregiver concerns:
1. Loss of human review/connection, specifically as related to clinical decision-making.
2. AI being used primarily to cut costs or deny care rather than improve safety and
outcomes
i. e.g. AI-based prior authorization tools, AI replacing clinical tasks and decision-
making.
3. Potential to exacerbate discrimination in care delivery due to reliance on data sets
that may not accurately reflect the population in which the AI is being used and may
therefore not provide the most accurate outputs, leading to unreliable patient
outcomes.
i. These risks are further compounded by a lack of adequate clinician training on use
of AI, including how to interpret, validate, and appropriately apply AI-generated
outputs or recommendations, increasing the likelihood that inaccurate
recommendations will be used and reinforce existing gaps in care delivery.
Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the adoption of
AI as part of clinical care?
I. Real-world impact of AI on patient outcomes, safety, and trust over time
a. Implement multicenter, prospective studies and post-market monitoring that track
effects on outcomes, safety events, diagnostic accuracy, and perception of trust over
time.
II. Comparative evaluations of AI-enabled versus traditional workflows on clinical burden,
documentation time, and time with patients
a. Support studies using standardized workload and time-use measures, including frontline
clinician feedback, to determine when AI meaningfully improves care versus simply
redistributing tasks.
III. Methods to detect and mitigate inaccuracies, drift, and unintended harms
a. Establish a national outcomes-focused reporting and monitoring system for AI-enabled
tools, drawing on lessons from FDA-regulated products (such as drugs and biologics)
without simply replicating their exact model. This system should prioritize higher-risk AI

use cases and focus on meaningful outcome and safety signals rather than requiring
uniform reporting for all tools.
i. Building on concepts from the Sentinel Initiative, stakeholders (including health
systems, manufacturers, and technology providers) could participate in an
aggregated outcome data registry to monitor real-world performance, identify
potential safety concerns, and support appropriate updates or modifications to AI-
enabled tools over time. This type of targeted post-market surveillance can be
structured to align with the incentives of clinicians, health care organizations, and
developers.13
ii. An AI outcomes registry could allow for the conduct of post-market monitoring and
collection of data on performance across diverse settings and populations. An
aggregated registry could track and analyze large volumes of clinical and operational
data to identify patterns of performance, adverse effects, errors, or inaccuracies that
emerge only after deployment. The registry could also track utilization patterns
associated with positive clinical outcomes, providing benchmarks for best practices
that developers and health systems can adopt. Moreover, AI systems that learn over
time or are periodically retrained based on new data can exhibit changes in
performance that are not captured by pre-market studies. Continuous monitoring
would enable early detection of adverse trends, triggering investigations or changes
including updated training protocols, algorithmic recalibration, or pausing certain
use cases. By documenting these, the registry would contribute to improving trust
among clinicians and patients and informing regulatory and oversight decisions.
iii. The registry could leverage a distributed data model similar to the Sentinel
Initiative’s, where data partners retain control of their data behind firewalls and only
aggregate, de-identified outputs are shared. This approach preserves HIPAA
compliance while allowing regulators, researchers, and developers to track the
performance of AI tools without centralizing identifiable patient information. The
data elements and interoperability formats could also be aligned with frameworks
like USCDI to improve consistency and reuse.
iv. Over time, a voluntary outcomes registry could serve as the foundation for a more
formal national framework, expanding participation and data sources to create a
comprehensive, scalable oversight infrastructure for AI-enabled clinical technologies.

Conclusion
Rubrum Advising appreciates the Department’s leadership in seeking concrete, experience-
based input at this early stage of federal policymaking on clinical AI. The recommendations in
this response reflect our belief that AI should be deployed in ways that measurably improve
outcomes and reduce total costs of care, while relying on existing safety and quality
infrastructure, clear governance expectations, and predictable reimbursement pathways to
support responsible innovation. We look forward to continued engagement with HHS as it
refines its regulatory, payment, and research strategies to accelerate the safe, effective use of AI
in clinical care.
