RESPONSE TO HHS REQUEST FOR INFORMATION
Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of
Clinical Care
90 FR 60108 | Document Number 2025-23641 | Submitted February 23, 2026
Submitted by: Dennis Morrison, PhD, Eleos Health | Focus Area: Behavioral Health
Introduction
This response addresses the ten specific questions posed in HHS's RFI on accelerating AI
adoption in clinical care (90 FR 60108), with an exclusive focus on behavioral health—
encompassing mental health, substance use disorders (SUD), and community behavioral health
organizations (CBHOs). Behavioral health occupies a unique position in the AI landscape: it faces
the some of the most severe workforce shortages, the greatest documentation burden relative to
encounter volume, the most significant access disparities, and clinical complexity that demands
nuanced, evidence-based approaches that current AI tools are only beginning to approximate.
Behavioral health organizations, particularly Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinics
(CCBHCs) and community mental health centers (CMHCs), treat the nation's most vulnerable
populations—individuals with serious mental illness (SMI), co-occurring disorders, and those
involved in the justice system. These organizations have chronically operated on thin margins,
and the combination of Medicaid funding pressures, clinician burnout, and documentation
mandates threatens their sustainability. AI tools—properly governed, evaluated, and
reimbursed—represent a rare opportunity to meaningfully address each of these challenges
simultaneously.
This response draws on the work of pioneering behavioral health AI companies including Eleos
Health (ambient documentation and compliance AI) and Lyssn (clinical quality improvement and
evidence-based practice fidelity AI), as well as the clinical research of Dr. John Torous, MD, MBI,
Director of the Digital Psychiatry Division at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) /
Harvard Medical School, whose decade-plus body of work on digital mental health tools, AI
chatbots, and mobile mental health applications provides essential evidence-based grounding.

Question 1:
What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in
AI for health care and its adoption and use in clinical care?
Workforce Crisis and Documentation Burden
Behavioral health faces a workforce crisis of historic proportions. Clinicians in community
behavioral health settings report spending 30-50% of their working hours on documentation rather
than direct care. This documentation burden is a primary driver of the 90% burnout rate cited
across the sector. Companies like Eleos Health have demonstrated that ambient AI
documentation tools can reduce documentation time by more than 70%, with providers accepting
80% of AI-generated note suggestions and 90% of notes submitted within 24 hours—dramatically
reducing late submission denials.
Yet adoption remains uneven because many behavioral health organizations, particularly rural
and safety-net providers, lack the IT infrastructure, broadband connectivity, and technical staff to
implement sophisticated AI platforms. The "digital divide" that Dr. John Torous has long
documented in his research on digital equity and the BIDMC Digital Navigator program extends
directly into organizational AI readiness.
Regulatory Fragmentation
The behavioral health regulatory environment is uniquely fragmented. Unlike general medical
AI, which primarily navigates FDA and HIPAA frameworks, behavioral health AI must
simultaneously address 42 CFR Part 2 restrictions on substance use disorder records, state-by-
state licensure and telehealth regulations, payer-specific prior authorization requirements, and
an emerging patchwork of state AI-specific legislation. Dr. Torous's 2025 50-state legislative
review published in JMIR Mental Health identified widely varying approaches — from Illinois's
near-ban on certain AI uses in behavioral health to Utah's disclosure-focused framework —
creating a compliance environment that disadvantages smaller innovators and CBHOs.
The Part 2 landscape shifted substantially with HHS's February 2024 Final Rule (89 Fed. Reg.
12496), which implemented Section 3221 of the CARES Act by aligning significant portions of
42 CFR Part 2 with HIPAA. The rule took effect April 16, 2024, with a compliance deadline of
February 16, 2026 — the week this RFI comment period closes. Key changes include: a single
patient consent now covers all future uses and disclosures for treatment, payment, and
healthcare operations (TPO), replacing the previous per-disclosure consent requirement;
covered entities and business associates receiving Part 2 records under TPO consent may
redisclose in accordance with HIPAA, without separate patient authorization; record segregation
is no longer required for entities receiving Part 2 records under a single TPO consent; and
enforcement authority has shifted from DOJ-only criminal penalties to OCR civil and criminal
enforcement aligned with the HIPAA penalty framework.

Despite this significant alignment, important Part 2 barriers specific to behavioral health AI tools
remain. The Final Rule created a new, highly protected category called "SUD Counseling
Notes" — defined as a clinician's notes documenting or analyzing the content of a SUD
counseling session, maintained separately from the treatment record — analogous to HIPAA's
psychotherapy notes protections. SUD Counseling Notes require a separate patient consent
and cannot be covered by a broad TPO consent. This new category is directly relevant to
ambient AI documentation tools: when a system like Eleos Health captures and analyzes a SUD
counseling session, the resulting AI-generated session analysis may constitute SUD Counseling
Notes under the new definition, requiring consent workflows that current ambient AI
implementations were not designed to accommodate. HHS has not yet issued guidance
clarifying how ambient AI documentation systems should navigate this new category, leaving
SUD treatment programs and their AI vendors in legal uncertainty.
Furthermore, Part 2 does not preempt more protective state laws, and the prohibition on using
SUD records in civil, criminal, administrative, or legislative proceedings against a patient
remains absolute without separate written consent or a court order — a protection that goes well
beyond HIPAA and has direct implications for AI-generated SUD documentation that could be
discoverable in legal proceedings. HHS should issue specific guidance addressing how ambient
AI documentation systems operating in SUD treatment settings should implement the SUD
Counseling Notes consent requirements, and should clarify the conditions under which AI-
generated session analysis constitutes Part 2-protected records. Because some ambient
listening documentation assistants require retention of audio recordings and/or transcripts of the
therapy session to analyze the session, HHS should declare these recordings as non-
discoverable.
A foundational barrier to AI adoption and interoperability in Community Mental Health Centers
(CMHCs) serving Medicaid populations is the absence of standardized procedure coding and
service definitions across state lines. While HCPCS Level II and CPT codes provide a nominal
federal framework, Medicaid is a state-administered program, and individual states retain broad
latitude to define covered services, establish documentation requirements, specify unit
structures, and determine reimbursement rates independently. The practical consequence is
that the same billing code — for example, an H-code for community-based mental health
services — may carry materially different service definitions, staff qualification requirements,
and clinical documentation expectations depending on the state and even the managed care
payer within that state. For AI-powered clinical documentation and coding assistance tools to
function reliably in CMHC environments, they must be trained, configured, and continuously
updated to accommodate this patchwork of state- and payer-specific rules. This variability
substantially increases the cost and complexity of AI implementation, limits the scalability of
vendor solutions across state markets, and creates persistent risk of claim denials, audit
exposure, and compliance failures when AI-generated outputs do not precisely align with
jurisdictionally-specific requirements. HHS should consider whether federal guidance, technical
assistance, or Medicaid waiver incentives could encourage greater harmonization of behavioral
health service definitions and coding standards as a prerequisite for effective AI adoption in this
sector.

Lack of Behavioral Health-Specific AI Training Data and
Validation
General-purpose large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT, Gemini and others perform poorly
on behavioral health-specific tasks without domain-specific fine-tuning. As Dr. Torous noted in
Congressional testimony (November 2025), one patient's apt observation captures the challenge:
general AI tools tend to repeat the same responses 'in different ways' without demonstrating
genuine clinical insight. These tools rely on sycophancy to build rapport. Lyssn, which has built
its platform on 17 years of academic research and analysis of more than 4.3 million clinical
sessions, and has produced over 60 peer-reviewed publications validating its approach,
represents the kind of domain-specific foundation behavioral health AI requires—but that standard
AI development pipelines do not automatically provide.
Eleos Health has pursued a parallel but distinct data strategy — building what it describes as the
industry's largest dataset of real-world behavioral health treatment sessions to train its ambient
documentation and clinical insight AI. A peer-reviewed study published in the Journal of Medical
Internet Research reports that Eleos's machine learning research drew on 2.83 million therapist-
client micro-dialogues — a dataset the lead researcher described as "unprecedented," specifically
because it captures how therapy is actually delivered in real-world clinical settings rather than in
the controlled conditions that have historically characterized psychotherapy research. Eleos's in-
house clinical team, with over 180 combined years of behavioral health experience, continuously
tests, validates, and fine-tunes its models to recognize the clinical terminology, interaction
patterns, and emotional indicators that a general-purpose LLM trained on broad internet text
cannot reliably detect. The platform's coverage extends across CBT, DBT, ACT, Motivational
Interviewing, and the Zero Suicide Framework, with active research studies conducted in
collaboration with Stanford, MIT Sloan, Vanderbilt, Harvard, Palo Alto University, Imperial College
London, and Hebrew University — including novel AI-based detection of social determinants of
health discussions in session content and multi-modal audio feature analysis as indicators of
depression and anxiety symptom severity. Critically, this dataset was assembled under a strict
privacy architecture: Eleos does not store raw session recordings or use protected health
information to train its models; any data used for model improvement is fully de-identified and
reviewed exclusively by a clinically licensed, security-cleared team. Together, the Lyssn and
Eleos datasets represent the most significant behavioral health-specific AI training foundations in
existence, and the research programs surrounding them demonstrate precisely the kind of
domain-specific investment that HHS should incentivize, protect, and replicate through federal
research funding.
The scarcity of large, well-labeled, consent-appropriate behavioral health datasets for AI
training—particularly for specialized modalities like group therapy, peer support, and crisis
intervention—limits the quality ceiling for behavioral health-specific AI.
Reimbursement Gaps
Current CMS reimbursement frameworks do not recognize AI-assisted clinical documentation or
AI-enhanced quality improvement as billable services. Behavioral health organizations cannot

claim additional reimbursement for deploying AI tools that improve documentation integrity,
enhance evidence-based practice fidelity, or reduce clinician turnover—even when these tools
demonstrably improve patient outcomes and organizational financial sustainability. The result is
that the upfront cost of AI tools falls entirely on organizations that are already operating on thin
margins.

Question 2:
What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design
changes should HHS prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care
and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to
augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care? Please provide specific
changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
42 CFR Part 2 Safe Harbor for AI Documentation Tools
As described in the “Regulatory Fragmentation” section above, HIPAA and 42 CFR § 2
alignment has helped providers’ significantly. However, issues remain especially regarding SUD
Counseling Notes. HHS should take two targeted actions. First, issue authoritative interpretive
guidance clarifying: (a) whether AI-generated session analysis in an ambient documentation tool
constitutes SUD Counseling Notes under 42 CFR § 2.11; (b) the conditions under which AI
vendor processing of session content constitutes a use or disclosure requiring Part 2 consent;
and (c) what a compliant "separate consent" workflow for SUD Counseling Notes looks like in
an ambient AI implementation. Second, establish a prospective safe harbor for AI
documentation tools that process SUD session content within the treating provider's care
setting, under a defined consent architecture, where the AI-generated output remains within the
treating provider's EHR and is not disclosed to external parties — on the grounds that such use
is analytically equivalent to a clinician's own note-taking rather than a disclosure to a third party.
Applicable CFR citations: 42 CFR §§ 2.11 (SUD Counseling Notes definition), 2.31 (consent
requirements), 2.32 (notice accompanying disclosure).
CCBHC Prospective Payment System Recognition of AI-
Enhanced Services
CMS should modify the CCBHC Prospective Payment System (PPS) methodology under the
CCBHC Medicaid Demonstration program to recognize AI-assisted documentation and quality
improvement tools as qualifying infrastructure investments that affect the cost report used to
establish PPS rates. Under the current framework, investments in AI tools that reduce
documentation time and improve compliance do not result in sustainable revenue adjustments,
because their value accrues primarily through avoided costs (reduced denials, improved staff
retention) rather than through billable activity. Applicable CFR citation: 42 CFR § 438.6 (managed
care CCBHC provisions); 42 CFR Part 447 (payment for services).
Medicare Behavioral Health Integration and Digital Health
Codes
CMS should expand CPT code coverage for AI-augmented behavioral health services. The
existing Behavioral Health Integration (BHI) codes (99492-99494) and General BHI (99484)
should be updated or supplemented to recognize AI-assisted care coordination, digital

phenotyping-informed treatment adjustment, and AI-enabled evidence-based practice
supervision. Additionally, CMS should establish new HCPCS codes specifically for AI-assisted
clinical documentation in behavioral health settings, analogous to how remote patient monitoring
codes (CPT 99453-99458) created a reimbursement pathway for technology-mediated
monitoring. Applicable CFR citation: 42 CFR § 410.67 (behavioral health integration services).
ONC Behavioral Health EHR Certification Criteria
ASTP/ONC should establish behavioral health-specific criteria within the Health IT Certification
Program (45 CFR Part 170) that include requirements for AI transparency, bias testing on
behavioral health populations, and interoperability with behavioral health data standards including
SNOMED CT mental health subsets and the Gravity Project's social determinants of health
(SDOH) terminology. Current certification criteria were developed primarily for acute and primary
care workflows and do not adequately address behavioral health-specific documentation
structures, group therapy notes, or the unique data elements relevant to SMI populations.

Question 3:
For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in
clinical care may raise novel legal and implementation issues that
challenge existing governance and accountability structures ( e.g., relating
to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help
address them?
In general, there are currently two broad uses of AI in behavioral healthcare: the first is the use
of AI to expedite and improve a clinician’s ability to complete the onerous documentation burden
seen in behavioral healthcare, especially community based behavioral healthcare. When used
in this manner, clinicians are being provided information to help them write clinical notes. They
and they alone are the arbiters of what, if any of the suggestions provided by the AI are used in
the clinical note. The second is AI being deployed without clinician oversight. AI chatbots are the
most common example of this form of AI usage in behavioral health care. The fundamental
difference between these two uses is that the former assists clinicians in their work but does not
replace clinical judgement. The latter has no human clinical oversight.
Liability in AI-Augmented Clinical Documentation
Ambient AI documentation tools like Eleos Health's platform generate progress note suggestions
that clinicians review, modify, incorporate into the EHR note and sign. For some, this has created
confusion regarding liability questions: if an AI-generated note contains a clinical inaccuracy that
contributes to a patient harm event, how should liability be apportioned among the parties that
participated in the creation of that including the AI vendor, the EHR vendor, the clinician who
approved the note, and the organization? Since these tools assist professionals only in
documenting clinical interactions, current malpractice frameworks appropriately assume human
authorship and human accountability for clinical documentation. HHS should reaffirm this position
and work with state insurance commissioners and CMS to develop guidance clarifying that the
supervising clinician retains professional responsibility for reviewed and approved AI-generated
content, while establishing minimum accountability standards for AI vendors providing
documentation assistance including transparency regarding model limitations, error rates, and
update protocols. Some AI products allow or even require that transcripts and/or audio recordings
of the clinical session and the recommendations made but not accepted by the clinician be
retained for some period of time. These recordings and transcripts are similar to other quality
improvement documents such as chart reviews and supervision and should be protected from
legal discovery.
AI Mental Health Chatbots and Crisis Situations
Compared to AI tools that assist clinicians in documenting clinical transactions, mental health
chatbots provide significantly more serious risks to end users. These products work semi-
automously often without a “human in the loop”. Dr. John Torous and others have documented

extensively the risks of AI chatbots deployed for mental health support without adequate safety
guardrails. As he noted in Congressional testimony, OpenAI reported over one million users per
week engaging ChatGPT with explicit indicators of suicidal planning, and numerous lawsuits have
alleged AI chatbot involvement in patient suicides. The legal status of AI chatbots that provide
mental health guidance but are not classified as medical devices creates a significant
accountability gap. HHS should issue guidance establishing minimum safety requirements—
including mandatory crisis detection, escalation protocols, and handoff to human crisis services—
for AI applications marketed for mental health support, regardless of FDA classification. This
would build on Dr. Torous's work benchmarking AI chatbot performance on mental health
scenarios through platforms like mindApps.ai. See “Real World Performance Surveillance” below
for more on this topic.
Privacy in Psychotherapy and Group Therapy AI
Lyssn's AI platform analyzes session recordings for quality improvement and evidence-based
practice fidelity—a valuable capability that raises novel privacy questions when applied to group
therapy, where multiple clients simultaneously disclose protected health information. Unlike
individual therapy, group therapy transcripts contain the PHI of potentially 10-15 individuals per
session. However, like individual notes, the information not used to create the clinical notes for
group therapy participants should be regarded as legally non-discoverable.
Informed Consent Standards for AI-Assisted Care
No federal standard currently exists for what behavioral health patients must be told when AI tools
are used in their care. Dr. Torous and others have long advocated for transparent disclosure to
patients about digital health tool use. There are two use cases for consent regarding the use of
AI tools. The first is general consent which is typically given when treatment begins along with
other, more traditional consents to treatment. The second is specific consent, or more exactly,
the ability for a patient to withdraw previously given consent for one or more treatment sessions.
AI vendors must accommodate both. HHS should establish minimum informed consent standards
for behavioral health settings that address: the use of ambient listening technologies during
sessions, AI analysis of session content for documentation or quality improvement, and the use
of AI chatbots or digital therapeutics as adjuncts to care.

Question 4:
For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI
evaluation methods (pre- and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and
other workflow and human-centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should
HHS further support these processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most
impactful ( e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or prize
competitions)?
Evidence-Based Practice Fidelity as a Core Metric
For behavioral health AI, the most clinically meaningful evaluation metric is fidelity to evidence-
based practices (EBPs)—the degree to which AI-assisted clinical encounters actually improve
adherence to validated treatment protocols like Motivational Interviewing (MI), Cognitive
Behavioral Therapy (CBT), and Dialectical Behavioral Therapy (DBT). Lyssn's platform, built on
17 years of academic research and validated against more than 4.3 million analyzed sessions,
represents the state of the art in AI-powered EBP fidelity measurement. Its approach—using
natural language processing to assess therapist behaviors against validated coding systems like
the Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity (MITI) scale—provides a model for how AI
evaluation in behavioral health can be clinically grounded rather than purely technical. Using
EBPs in this way presumes that managing the process i.e. the protocol will result in the same (or
similar) outcomes achieved by the EBP when that protocol was validated. It is important to note
that doing an EBP is a means to an end, not the end itself. EBPs exist to provide clinicians with a
research-based way to get good clinical outcomes. They define what improvement is possible for
various treatments, in a way defining a gold standard. In cases where delivering high fidelity EBPs
is not possible or advisable, the outcomes demonstrated by the EBPs provide a benchmark for
non-EBP interventions. That is, if a non-EBP intervention can achieve the same or better
outcomes as demonstrated with the EBP for the same or less cost, the non-EBP intervention is
achieving what the EBP treatment did even though it was not maintaining fidelity to the protocol.
In this way, EBPs serve dual purposes: providing a detailed protocol for clinicians to follow and a
clinical outcome benchmark that non-EBPs should strive to attain.
Therapeutic Alliance Preservation
The therapeutic relationship—the alliance between clinician and client—is one of the most
powerful predictors of behavioral health treatment outcomes across all modalities. AI evaluation
frameworks must assess whether AI tools enhance or diminish therapeutic alliance quality. Eleos
Health's published research and received numerous anecdotal observations demonstrating that
providers using its ambient documentation platform achieve 50% higher client engagement, 3-4x
better symptom improvement, and 36% greater use of evidence-based techniques compared to
baseline, suggesting that removing the documentation burden allows clinicians to be more fully
present with clients. Post-deployment evaluation should systematically measure therapeutic
alliance using validated tools such as the Working Alliance Inventory.

Bias and Health Equity Testing
AI systems trained primarily on data from majority populations can perform poorly for
underrepresented groups—a particular concern in behavioral health, where racial and ethnic
minorities, LGBTQ+ individuals, and people with limited English proficiency face well-documented
care disparities. Dr. Torous's Digital Navigator program at BIDMC specifically addresses digital
equity as a prerequisite for beneficial AI deployment. HHS should require that behavioral health
AI tools undergo pre-deployment bias testing disaggregated by race, ethnicity, language, gender
identity, and socioeconomic status, with post-deployment monitoring to detect disparate
performance. Grant mechanisms through SAMHSA and NIMH would be the most appropriate
vehicles for funding this research.
Real-World Performance Surveillance
Dr. Torous has consistently argued that most mental health apps and AI chatbots remain
understudied, with unknown real-world risks — a position now supported by a robust independent
evidence base. Linardon and colleagues conducted a systematic review of 171 clinical trials of
mental health apps published in npj Digital Medicine (2024), finding that only 55 trials reported
adverse events at all, the meta-analytic deterioration rate across app conditions was 6.7%, and
those trials that did report adverse events frequently provided insufficient information to allow
proper assessment of user risk. Writing in World Psychiatry (2025), the same research team
documented that negative effects from apps can extend to symptom deterioration, onset of new
symptoms, and suicidal ideation, with rates of negative effects in severe mental illness
populations reaching as high as 20% in some trials — a state of evidence that, in their words,
"makes it difficult to integrate apps into clinical practice safely."
The crisis response failure problem has been quantified independently. Dwyer and colleagues
publishing in Psychiatric Services (2024), evaluated 302 mental health apps available in January
2024 using the APA's app evaluation model and found that only 15% referred users to 988, the
national crisis standard, and — most strikingly — 14 apps with more than 3.5 million combined
downloads provided incorrect or nonfunctional alternative crisis hotlines. This is not a theoretical
risk: millions of people in potential crisis were being routed to telephone numbers that do not
connect. This finding directly extends foundational work by Larsen, Nicholas, and Christensen,
whose systematic review of suicide-focused apps found that none abided with best practices for
providing visible crisis support information, and that the vast majority demonstrated limited ability
to recognize anticipatory warning signs of suicidal crisis.
For behavioral health AI, HHS should support the development of post-deployment surveillance
infrastructure analogous to FDA's MedWatch system—a mechanism for clinicians and patients
to report AI-related clinical errors, safety events, and unexpected behaviors in behavioral health
settings. AHRQ's existing patient safety infrastructure could serve as a model.

Question 5:
How can HHS best support private sector activities ( e.g.,
accreditation, certification, industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to
promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
Behavioral Health-Specific AI Certification
HHS should support the development of a behavioral health-specific AI certification program,
potentially through SAMHSA or in partnership with the National Council for Mental Wellbeing.
Such a program would extend beyond ONC's general health IT certification criteria to address
behavioral health-specific requirements including EBP fidelity measurement, crisis protocol
adequacy, 42 CFR Part 2 compliance for SUD applications, and therapeutic alliance preservation.
Eleos Health's recent achievement of ISO 42001 certification (the AI-specific management
system standard) demonstrates that behavioral health AI vendors are ready for rigorous
certification frameworks.
SAMHSA Technology Assessment Program
SAMHSA should establish a technology assessment program analogous to the Substance Abuse
Prevention and Treatment Block Grant's evidence-based practices mandate, creating a tiered
recognition system for behavioral health AI tools based on evidence strength. Dr. Torous, who
led the American Psychiatric Association's work group on smartphone app evaluation and
developed the APA's technology evaluation framework, has created models for how this can be
structured. His mindapps.org database—the largest mental health app registry—and mindApps.ai
benchmarking platform provide a template for systematic, ongoing behavioral health AI evaluation
that HHS could support and formalize. Other partners in this effort include The Joint Commission
and CARF, two of the most used accrediting bodies in the behavioral health industry.
Academic-Industry Research Partnerships
HHS should incentivize partnerships between behavioral health AI companies and academic
medical centers with established digital psychiatry programs. The BIDMC Digital Psychiatry
Division's research collaborations—including studies validating Eleos Health's impact on
documentation burden and clinical outcomes—demonstrate how these partnerships can produce
rigorous peer-reviewed evidence that benefits the entire field. Cooperative research and
development agreements (CRADAs) between NIMH-funded research centers and behavioral
health AI companies would accelerate the evidence base.Eleos Health has established multiple
academic partnerships with Stanford, Palo Alto University, Hebrew University and others.

Question 6:
Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded
performance and cost expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds
of novel AI tools would have the greatest potential to improve health care
outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
Successes: Ambient Documentation in Behavioral Health
AI-assisted clinical documentation has clearly met or exceeded performance expectations in
behavioral health settings. Eleos Health's deployments across organizations including Wellpower,
Jefferson Center, GRAND Mental Health, and Gaudenzia demonstrate consistent documentation
time reductions exceeding 70%, with providers accepting 80% of AI-generated note suggestions.
It should be noted that Eleos Health believes this 80% mark is a best practice. That is, no more
than 80% of the note should be AI-generated since all notes should also include the unique voice
of the clinician. These results translate directly into organizational sustainability: 90% of notes
submitted within 24 hours reduces claim denials, and reduced documentation burden improves
staff retention in a sector experiencing severe workforce shortages. Dr. Brandon Ward, when he
was CIO at Jefferson Center, reported that providers are enthusiastic about the technology,
including initial skeptics. These results are particularly significant given that behavioral health
organizations serve some of the most complex patients in the healthcare system.
Successes: AI-Powered Quality Improvement and EBP
Fidelity
Lyssn's AI platform has demonstrated success in scaling evidence-based practice training and
quality improvement in ways that were previously impossible at the organizational level.
Traditional EBP fidelity assessment required manual coding of session recordings—a process so
resource-intensive that most organizations could only evaluate a tiny fraction (5-10%) of clinical
encounters. Lyssn's AI enables continuous, automated fidelity monitoring across entire
workforces, allowing organizations to identify training needs, target coaching resources, and
demonstrate fidelity to funders and accreditors. This addresses a longstanding gap between what
EBP research says is effective and what actually occurs in real-world clinical practice.
Shortfalls: AI Chatbots for Direct Mental Health Support
AI chatbots deployed directly to patients for mental health support have not met clinical
expectations and present documented safety risks. Dr. Torous, whose early research was
foundational in assessing the potential of digital mental health tools, has documented that most
mental health chatbots remain untested in clinical populations and present unknown risks. The
2017 emergence of Woebot and similar chatbots was accompanied by enthusiasm that has been
tempered by subsequent evidence: few have undergone randomized controlled trials, many fail
to recognize crisis situations, and some have been associated with patient harm. The FDA's April
2024 approval of the first app to treat depression (Rejoyn for adjunctive treatment of MDD)
represents a positive regulatory development, but the vast majority of the thousands of mental

health apps on the market remain unvalidated. NAMI does not endorse AI for mental health
treatment and has partnered with Dr. Torous to develop independent benchmarks for AI tool
safety.
Shortfalls: Interoperability and EHR Integration
AI tools in behavioral health consistently struggle with EHR integration. The behavioral health
EHR market is highly fragmented, with dozens of specialized platforms (Netsmart's myAvatar and
Streamline, Qualifacts/Credible, Kipu, ICANotes, and others) lacking the standardized APIs that
acute care EHRs have developed under ONC's information blocking rules. Eleos Health
addresses this by operating as an EHR-agnostic overlay via browser extension—a practical
solution, but one that highlights the underlying interoperability problem rather than solving it. True
integration requires standardized behavioral health data exchange frameworks that do not
currently exist.

Question 7:
Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within
health care organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for
clinical care? What are the primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in
clinical care?
Key Decision Makers
In community behavioral health organizations, AI adoption decisions typically require alignment
among: the Chief Executive Officer (organizational mission and financial sustainability), the Chief
Clinical Officer (clinical appropriateness, staff engagement, evidence base), the Chief Information
Officer (technical infrastructure, security, EHR integration), and the Chief Medical Officer
(psychiatrist or physician oversight of AI-generated clinical content). Unlike hospital systems,
most CBHOs lack a dedicated Chief Digital Officer or Chief Innovation Officer, meaning AI
advocacy must come from within existing leadership roles. Board-level engagement is
increasingly common as AI represents both opportunity and liability.
Eleos Health's deployment experience across organizations like Jefferson Center (where the
Chief Innovation Officer and President/CEO both participated actively in adoption decisions)
illustrates that multi-stakeholder buy-in is essential—and that mission alignment and clinician
enthusiasm are as important as technical capability.
Primary Administrative Hurdles
The most consistently reported administrative hurdles to behavioral health AI adoption include:
(1) procurement complexity, including legal review of BAAs for ambient listening technologies and
vendor security assessments that small organizations lack capacity to conduct; (2) state licensing
and consent requirements for AI tools that capture session content, which vary widely and are
changing frequently and quickly and are rarely addressed in existing technology guidance; (3)
payer contracting constraints, as some Medicaid managed care contracts include provisions that
could be interpreted to restrict AI-generated documentation; (4) workforce training requirements,
as even well-designed AI tools require change management investment that organizations with
limited training budgets struggle to fund; and (5) skepticism from clinicians who have experienced
technology-driven increases in administrative burden and are appropriately cautious about new
tools promising relief.

Question 8:
Where would enhanced interoperability widen market
opportunities, fuel research, and accelerate the development of AI for clinical
care? Please consider specific data types, data standards, and benchmarking
tools.
Behavioral Health-Specific Data Standards
The most significant interoperability gap affecting behavioral health AI is the absence of
standardized behavioral health data exchange frameworks. The Meaningful Use standards first
implemented with the passage of the Affordable Care Act started the process of interoperability,
but behavioral health care organizations were immediately at a disadvantage since they were
exempted from the EHR stimulus incentives available to medical care providers. Many Health
Information Exchanges (HIEs) were hesitant to accept and coordinate behavioral health data
because of the challenges of 42CFR Part 2 and a general hesitancy about the confidentiality
requirements inherent in behavioral health information. Now, while HL7 FHIR has transformed
data exchange in acute and primary care, behavioral health data—including mental status
examinations, symptom rating scales, substance use assessment tools, and session notes—
lacks standardized FHIR resource profiles. SAMHSA and ONC should jointly develop and
mandate FHIR implementation guides specifically for behavioral health, including: standardized
profiles for PHQ-9, GAD-7, Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS), AUDIT, DAST,
and other validated assessment instruments; FHIR resources for group therapy documentation;
and behavioral health-specific social determinants of health data elements aligned with the
Gravity Project.
Digital Phenotyping Data Standards
Dr. Torous's mindLAMP platform at BIDMC represents a leading approach to digital
phenotyping—using passive smartphone sensor data (GPS, accelerometry, screen time,
communication patterns) and active ecological momentary assessment to capture the lived
experience of individuals with mental illness between clinical encounters. Standardized data
models for digital phenotyping would enable AI systems to incorporate real-world behavioral data
into clinical decision support, treatment planning, and relapse prediction. NIH's All of Us research
program, in which Dr. Torous serves as an advisor for the smartphone mood study, provides a
model for how such data could be collected at population scale.
Crisis and Emergency Data Exchange
Behavioral health AI crisis tools—including suicide risk prediction algorithms and crisis chatbot
escalation systems—require seamless data exchange with crisis services, emergency
departments, and 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline infrastructure. Currently, no standardized data
exchange protocols exist for transmitting risk-relevant information between AI-powered
behavioral health applications and crisis response systems. The 988 infrastructure buildout

represents an opportunity to establish behavioral health AI crisis data standards that could benefit
both direct patient-facing AI tools and clinician-facing decision support systems.

Question 9:
What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers
wish to see addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally,
what concerns do patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and use of
AI in clinical care?
Desired Applications
Patients and caregivers in behavioral health settings consistently express desire for AI
applications that: increase access to care (particularly for those in underserved rural and urban
areas); reduce administrative friction (appointment scheduling, medication refill requests, prior
authorization navigation); extend the reach of therapeutic support between sessions through
evidence-based digital tools; and improve the quality and attentiveness of clinical encounters by
reducing the time their clinician spends looking at a screen. The last point is particularly salient—
patients notice when their clinician is distracted by documentation. Eleos Health's research and
clinical testimonials frequently highlight that clients receive more engaged, screen-free care when
their providers use ambient documentation AI.
Dr. Torous's research on patient perspectives on digital mental health, including his widely-cited
guides to evaluating mental health apps, consistently finds that patients want tools that are
evidence-based, privacy-respecting, and clinically supervised. The patient experience of AI
chatbots varies widely—some find them accessible and destigmatizing, while others find them
superficial and inadequate for their needs.
Patient Concerns
Patient concerns about AI in behavioral health are concentrated in three areas. First, privacy:
psychotherapy is one of the most sensitive data categories, and patients fear that AI-analyzed
session content could be used for insurance underwriting, employment decisions, or law
enforcement purposes. The history of stigma and discrimination against people with mental illness
and substance use disorders makes these fears reasonable and well-founded. Second,
depersonalization: patients fear that AI will replace the human therapeutic relationship with
algorithmic interaction, reducing their care to a transaction with a machine. Third, accuracy:
patients worry that AI-generated documentation may misrepresent what was said or decided in a
session, affecting their treatment or generating records they cannot correct.
NAMI's partnership with Dr. Torous to develop independent AI safety benchmarks—explicitly
centered on lived experience perspectives—represents a model for how patient voices can be
systematically incorporated into behavioral health AI evaluation. HHS should require lived
experience representation on any federal advisory bodies addressing behavioral health AI.

Question 10:
Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should
prioritize to accelerate the adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
a. Are there published findings about the impact of adopted AI tools and their use
clinical care?
b. How does the literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI
as part of clinical care?
10a: Published Findings on Adopted AI Tools
The behavioral health AI evidence base is growing but remains insufficient relative to the pace of
deployment. Key published findings include:
• Eleos Health has produced 9 peer-reviewed studies on its ambient AI documentation
platform, with more than a dozen in progress, including preliminary randomized
controlled trial data suggesting improved mental health outcomes for patients whose
clinicians use the platform. Findings include 50% higher client engagement, 3-4x better
symptom improvement, and 36% greater use of evidence-based techniques.
• Lyssn's platform is grounded in 60+ peer-reviewed academic publications developed
over 17 years of research, validating its NLP-based approach to assessing Motivational
Interviewing fidelity and other EBPs. The platform has analyzed more than 4.3 million
sessions, providing an unparalleled evidence base for behavioral health AI quality
measurement.
• Dr. Torous and colleagues published a comprehensive review in World Psychiatry
(2025) on the evolving field of digital mental health, covering smartphone apps,
generative AI, chatbots, and virtual reality, concluding that while evidence for some
modalities is accumulating, most mental health chatbots remain undertested. His team's
50-state legislative review (JMIR Mental Health, 2025) provides the most comprehensive
analysis of how states are regulating AI in mental health.
• The FDA's April 2024 approval of Rejoyn (Otsuka and Alto Neuroscience's
prescription digital therapeutic) for adjunctive treatment of major depressive disorder
represents a regulatory milestone, demonstrating that behavioral health AI can meet the
FDA's evidence standards for medical device clearance when rigorously evaluated.
10b: Costs, Benefits, and Transfers
The behavioral health AI literature approaches cost-benefit analysis with increasing
sophistication, though significant gaps remain:
• Documentation AI demonstrates clear return on investment through reduced
clinician hours spent on notes (>70% reduction), improved claim submission timeliness
(90% of notes within 24 hours with Eleos Health), reduced late/rejected claims, and

improved staff retention. Given that behavioral health clinician turnover costs
organizations an estimated $15,000-$30,000 per vacancy, retention benefits alone can
justify AI investment within months.
• Quality improvement AI (Lyssn) generates value through improved EBP fidelity,
which correlates with better patient outcomes and reduced treatment duration—
ultimately reducing the per-episode cost of care. The ability to evaluate 100% of clinical
encounters rather than a 5-10% sample also reduces audit risk and accreditation
vulnerability.
• AI chatbots and direct-to-consumer mental health AI present a more complex cost-
benefit picture. While they offer potential cost savings through reduced clinician time, Dr.
Torous and colleagues have documented risks including failure to recognize crisis
situations, inadequate evidence bases, and the possibility of displacing rather than
supplementing human care. The costs of adverse events—including patient harm from
inadequate crisis response—are difficult to quantify but potentially catastrophic.
• A critical and underappreciated transfer dynamic is the reimbursement cliff:
behavioral health organizations bear the full cost of AI tools while payers capture most of
the financial benefit through reduced claims (fewer denials, improved documentation
integrity). CMS and state Medicaid agencies should develop value-sharing mechanisms
that ensure AI-adopting providers capture a fair share of the financial value they
generate.
Priority Research Investments for HHS
Based on the above analysis, HHS—through NIMH, SAMHSA, AHRQ, and NIH—should prioritize
the following behavioral health AI research investments:
• Research support for AI use in crisis assessment and triage including AI tools to
improve suicidal risk identification and prevention.
• Rigorous clinical trials of AI ambient documentation tools measuring both provider
outcomes (burnout, satisfaction, retention) and patient outcomes (therapeutic alliance,
symptom improvement, treatment completion rates).
• Development and validation of standardized behavioral health AI evaluation
frameworks, building on Dr. Torous's APA technology evaluation framework and Lyssn's
evidence-based practice fidelity measurement approaches.
• Safety research on AI chatbots and digital mental health tools, including prospective
studies of adverse event rates, crisis response adequacy, and the impact of AI tool use
on suicidal ideation and self-harm outcomes.
• Digital equity research examining differential outcomes of behavioral health AI
deployment across racial, ethnic, linguistic, and socioeconomic groups, addressing the
disparities documented in Dr. Torous's Digital Navigator program research.
• Implementation science research on AI adoption in CCBHCs and CMHCs
specifically, examining the organizational, workforce, and regulatory factors that predict
successful adoption in under-resourced safety-net settings.
