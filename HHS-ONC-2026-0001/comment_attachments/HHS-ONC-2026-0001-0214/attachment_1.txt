Supplemental Closing Statement
To: RFI Review Committee
From: David Bynon
Date: February 23, 2026
Subject: Supplemental Closing Comments – HHS-ONC-2026-0001
Executive Summary
In reviewing the volume and tenor of recent comments, it is evident that many stakeholders
perceive a tension between responsible AI oversight and administrative burden. I
respectfully submit that this tension arises not from oversight itself, but from ambiguity in
how policy meaning and validation are structured.
The path to efficiency is not reduction of governance—but architectural refinement of how
governance is implemented.
On Oversight and Operational Load
Oversight becomes burdensome when compliance is interpretive, manual, and reactive. It
becomes efficient when policy meaning is explicit, machine-readable, and auditable by
design.
As outlined in my initial submission, a deterministic semantic framework allows AI systems
to retrieve from authoritative, versioned policy memory rather than infer from narrative text.
This approach shifts compliance from after-the-fact review toward structured, audit-ready
outputs.
Clarity reduces friction. Structure reduces interpretive drift. Deterministic retrieval reduces
downstream reconciliation.
On Cost-Efficiency and Innovation
Innovation is not constrained by documentation; it is constrained by uncertainty.
By aligning AI systems with structured, machine-verifiable policy representations,
organizations can reduce reconciliation costs currently borne by CMS, OMHA, and

beneficiary support operations. When oversight is embedded at the data layer, validation
becomes continuous rather than episodic.
Efficiency in AI governance is achieved through standardization of semantic
responsibility—not through deregulation. In the context of ongoing government efficiency
initiatives such as the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), clarity — not
reduction — in policy semantics enables scalable oversight that aligns with both
organizational efficiency goals and AI governance principles.
On Data Transparency and Accountability
Concerns regarding data sovereignty are understandable in an evolving AI environment.
However, structured provenance, versioning, and machine-readable definitions protect
both regulated entities and the public interest.
A transparent, pre-defined semantic layer creates mutual accountability while preserving
proprietary implementation. It clarifies “what is policy” without exposing “how systems are
built.”
Closing Observation
As this RFI closes, I encourage the committee to consider that the future of high-impact AI
governance in healthcare will depend less on the volume of reporting and more on the
precision of structured policy memory.
The federal challenge is not whether to govern AI—but how to do so in a way that reduces
interpretive variance, minimizes audit friction, and preserves public trust in regulated
domains such as Medicare.
Respectfully,
David W. Bynon
