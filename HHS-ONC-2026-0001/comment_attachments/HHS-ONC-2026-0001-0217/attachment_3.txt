Background:
On December 4, 2025, the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) issued the HHS Artificial Intelligence (AI)
Strategy [1] in response to Office of Management and Budget (OMB) Memorandum 25-21.[2] The HHS AI Strategy represents a
“OneHHS” approach to AI and is primarily focused on the Department's internal interests and ambitions to harness the
transformative potential of AI and embed it into HHS's operations, while upholding patient privacy, civil rights, and civil
liberties. Consistent with the President's artificial intelligence (AI) Action Plan,[3] recent Executive Orders [4] on AI, and Office
of Management and Budget AI memoranda,[5] the Department seeks public feedback on the actions it can take to establish a
forward-leaning, industry-supportive, and secure approach to accelerate the adoption and use of AI [6] as part of clinical care.
In the past 12 months, HHS Divisions, including the Food and Drug Administration (FDA),[7] National Institutes of Health
(NIH),[8] Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS),[9] and ASTP/ONC [10] have sought public feedback on various
aspects of AI and how it intersects with the Department's policy interests. In this RFI we seek concrete, experience-based
feedback from those building, buying, evaluating, using, and receiving care from AI tools that are part of clinical care as well
as from those who wish to do so but face barriers. Public feedback will inform HHS-wide use of three different approaches:
regulation, reimbursement, and research & development. In general, HHS seeks feedback on ways in which these
approaches can be most effectively applied to support the rapid adoption and use of AI in clinical care, to foster public trust
and confidence in modern technology solutions, to reduce uncertainty that impedes AI innovation, and to align federal
incentives so that AI is deployed in ways that enhance productivity, reduce burden, lower health care costs, and improve
health outcomes for patients, caregivers, and communities.

A.1 Perspective and focus
This response is provided by a small business advisory focused on AI-adoption architecture (enterprise/business/technical)
and business systems engineering for AI, with emphasis on governed, auditable, human-centered operation of AI
capabilities in regulated environments. The offering is advisory + architecture (no product claims; no implementation
commitments), with the intent to support primes and health-sector organizations as they operationalize HHS goals for rapid
adoption that maintains public trust.
HHS is seeking “concrete, experience-based feedback” to accelerate AI in clinical care using regulation, reimbursement, and
R&D, while fostering trust, reducing uncertainty, and aligning incentives.
A.2 Core thesis: adoption bottlenecks are governance-and-operations bottlenecks, not “model”
bottlenecks
Across federal and state health programs, we see a consistent pattern: the biggest adoption drag is rarely the algorithm in
isolation; it is the system around it—including requirements, accountability, change control, evidence, interoperability,
and operational oversight.

Three practical implications:
1. AI is operated, not delivered. Intelligent behavior changes over time (data drift, prompt/tool changes, workflow
changes) and must be governed continuously.
2. Regulated trust requires traceability from intent/requirement → decision → action → evidence, including auditable
approvals and governed change.
3. Requirements for AI are not just “features.” They must explicitly model: what is off-limits, when to escalate, how
humans intervene, and what evidence must be produced.
Practical “method call-outs” (used as concepts, not marketing)
A. Structured, agent-aware requirements
A proven approach is to surface ambiguous needs early, classify requirements (including agentive and policy/constraint
requirements), and link them to events/actors to preserve design traceability.
B. Capability-governed, event-driven operating model
For governed intelligent systems, model behavior on an “event spine,” define explicit approval gates, maintain audit trails
for each run/case, enforce change control for prompts/tools/thresholds, and produce “evidence packs” suitable for audit
sampling.
A.3 Responses to HHS Specific Questions (1–10)
1) Biggest barriers to private-sector innovation and adoption in clinical care
HHS asks: “What are the biggest barriers…?”
Barrier themes (adoption horizontal):
• Unclear governance boundary for non-device AI (documentation, validation, accountability expectations vary by
organization and legal posture).
• Operational risk and “silent change” (prompt/tool updates, model drift, workflow drift) without standardized
change-control patterns.
• Evidence deficit: organizations cannot reliably answer “what changed / who approved / what data / can we
reproduce outputs,” which blocks scale-out.
• Interoperability friction: costly, inconsistent integration to clinical data types; uneven benchmarking; and limited
“shared evaluation infrastructure.”
• Procurement mismatch: acquisition often funds pilots but not the operational controls (monitoring, governance,
evidence, sustainment) needed for production.
Actionable HHS enablers:

• Publish a Non-Device Clinical AI Governance Profile (minimum “operational control set”): approvals, audit trail,
traceability, monitoring, change control, escalation paths, and evidence artifacts. (These are the controls regulated
organizations repeatedly need to scale safely.
• Fund reusable reference architectures for: clinical workflow integration + evidence logging + controlled
deployment (patterns, not products).
• Create “fast-lane” adoption guidance for low-risk, assistive AI with clearly bounded autonomy and required
safeguards.
2) Regulatory, payment, or program design changes HHS should prioritize (include CFR references)
HHS asks what changes to prioritize and requests CFR citations. HHS-AI-RFI
Regulatory priorities (reduce uncertainty without weakening protections):
• Clarify how existing privacy/security obligations apply to AI workflows, especially where AI vendors act as business
associates and where models/logs may contain sensitive data. HIPAA’s Privacy and Security framework is already
grounded in 45 CFR Part 160 and Part 164. HHS+1
• Address adoption friction for sensitive subdomains (e.g., SUD records) by aligning practical implementation
guidance with 42 CFR Part 2 while protecting patient trust. eCFR+1
• Reinforce that interoperability is not optional: reduce “AI data lock-in” by aligning AI deployment expectations with
ONC’s information blocking framework in 45 CFR Part 171. eCFR+1
Programmatic / reimbursement design (architecture lens):
• Encourage payment models that reimburse the operational controls that make AI safe (monitoring, human
oversight time, evidence production), not just the “tool.” HHS notes reimbursement can unintentionally slow
innovation and seeks payment policy modernization.
• Where AI is used in coverage/utilization processes, reinforce individualized decision requirements and
transparency. Medicare Advantage regulations include medical-necessity determination requirements (see 42 CFR §
422.101). GovInfo+1
Concrete “do now” changes (low regret):
• Issue implementation guidance (not necessarily new regulation) defining acceptable patterns for: model/prompt
versioning, approval gates, run-level audit logging, and evidence packs—especially for non-device AI embedded in
clinical workflow.
• Expand HHS/ONC procurement playbooks to require vendors to deliver operational governance artifacts (change
logs, monitoring plans, escalation policies, reproducibility strategy) as first-class deliverables.
3) Novel legal and implementation issues for non-medical-device AI; HHS’s role
HHS highlights liability, indemnification, privacy, and security for non-device AI.

Novel issues (implementation reality):
• Accountability ambiguity when AI participates in decisions but humans remain responsible; “who is the decision-
maker” is often unclear in practice.
• Duty-to-monitor and change-control: if the AI behavior evolves, the organization needs a defensible, repeatable
oversight process.
• Evidence expectations: legal defensibility depends on being able to reconstruct the basis for outputs (data
provenance, model/prompt/tool versions, human overrides).
• Privacy/security risk surfaces expand: prompts, logs, embeddings, and fine-tuning datasets can inadvertently
become regulated data stores (HIPAA/Part 2 implications). HHS+1
Recommended HHS role:
• Publish a shared vocabulary and minimum controls for non-device AI in clinical workflow: autonomy levels,
escalation triggers, evidence artifacts, and approval expectations (a “common contract” between builders, buyers,
and auditors).
• Convene a cross-OpDiv working group (ASTP/ONC + CMS + NIH, as relevant) to align “trust & safety” expectations
with interoperability and reimbursement incentives. HHS is explicitly taking a “OneHHS” posture and seeks to
accelerate adoption while upholding privacy/civil liberties.
4) Promising evaluation methods (pre/post), metrics, robustness testing; how HHS should support
HHS asks for pre- and post-deployment evaluation methods and mechanisms.
Evaluation must be lifecycle-based (not a one-time “validation”):
• Pre-deployment: define the operational boundaries (allowed actions, prohibited actions, escalation rules) and test
them, not just model accuracy.
• Post-deployment: continuous monitoring for behavioral drift, workflow misfit, error modes, and human override
patterns (signals of unsafe automation).
Metrics categories that accelerate trust:
• Clinical/workflow outcomes (burden reduction, time-to-task completion, error rates, escalation quality).
• Safety-of-execution metrics (rate of off-limits outputs, near-miss frequency, override frequency, “exception raised”
patterns).
• Governance metrics (evidence completeness score; % of runs traceable to approved versions; time-to-
revoke/rollback). These map directly to auditability controls.
How HHS should support (highest leverage):
• Fund shared test harnesses + benchmark toolchains for clinical AI workflows (including human-centered
evaluation), not just datasets.

• Use prize competitions for: (a) “best evidence pack / reproducibility design,” (b) “best controlled escalation UX,”
and (c) “best drift detection + rollback patterns,” because these are adoption blockers across domains.
5) Supporting private-sector accreditation/certification/testing/credentialing
HHS asks how it can best support accreditation/certification and industry-driven testing.
Recommendation: certify the *operating controls*, not the “AI.”
A pragmatic approach is to support accreditation programs that assess whether an AI-enabled workflow has:
• explicit approval gates,
• run-level audit trails,
• governed change control for prompts/tools/thresholds,
• traceability from requirement → decision → action → evidence,
• operational UX for review/override.
HHS contribution options:
• Publish reference checklists and “audit-ready evidence templates” (machine-readable where possible) so
accreditation is consistent across organizations.
• Sponsor an “AI for Clinical Care Controls Catalog” aligned to privacy/security and interoperability expectations.
6) Where AI has met/exceeded expectations vs fallen short; most promising novel AI tools
HHS asks where AI has met expectations and where it falls short; and what novel tools have greatest potential.
Where expectations are often met (pattern-level):
• Administrative burden reduction (documentation assistance, summarization, routing/triage) when autonomy is
bounded and human review is designed well.
• Decision support augmentation (surface relevant context, suggest next-best actions) when traceability and
escalation are explicit.
Where tools fall short (adoption failure modes):
• “Pilot success” but production failure due to missing operating controls: monitoring, versioning, change approval,
evidence packs, and interoperability plumbing.
• Opaque or inconsistent behavior caused by ungoverned prompt/tool changes (“silent production change”).
• Integration costs and data friction overwhelm value when interoperability isn’t planned early.
Most promising “novel” tools (framed as capabilities):

• Agentic workflow orchestration with strict boundaries: AI that can coordinate tasks across systems (e.g., gather
context, draft artifacts, route for approvals) while being constrained by policy and escalation rules. The key is a
governed event spine + approval gates + evidence.
• Evidence-producing AI: systems designed to generate audit-ready evidence packs (inputs, versions, decisions,
overrides) as a native output.
7) Who influences adoption inside healthcare orgs; primary admin hurdles
HHS asks which roles/bodies influence adoption and what hurdles exist.
Decision-makers and governing bodies (typical):
• Clinical leadership (CMO/CNO), Quality & Safety, Compliance/Privacy/Security, IT/EHR leadership, and
Procurement—often with enterprise risk committees involved for anything that touches clinical workflow at scale.
Primary administrative hurdles (systemic):
• No shared “definition of done” for production AI (governance artifacts missing from acquisition packages).
• Fragmented accountability (clinical vs IT vs compliance) and unclear escalation/override design.
• Evidence burden: teams cannot efficiently produce the artifacts risk/compliance needs to approve and sustain AI.
What helps (architecture lever):
• Use an agent-as-role accountability model: explicitly assign which human or automation role fulfills each
requirement and what triggers their actions.
• Treat operational UX (review queues, explanation views, delta dashboards) as part of governance, not “nice-to-
have.”
8) Where enhanced interoperability widens market opportunities; data types, standards, benchmarking
tools
HHS asks where interoperability would widen opportunities and accelerate development (data types, standards,
benchmarking).
Highest-leverage interoperability targets:
• Clinical context + provenance: not only “clinical facts,” but “what changed and why” (orders, results, meds,
problems, notes) with time context and source attribution.
• Workflow events: standardized event semantics for common transitions (admission/discharge/transfer; lab-result-
available; referral-created; prior-auth-requested; etc.)—because AI is event-driven in operation.
• Quality and outcomes signals to support post-deployment monitoring and continuous evaluation.
What HHS can do:

• Promote interoperability approaches that include benchmarking and evaluation harnesses, not just data exchange.
(Benchmarking is explicitly requested by HHS here.
• Encourage “composable” integrations so organizations can swap AI components without rebuilding pipelines
(reduces lock-in; accelerates competition).
9) Patient/caregiver challenges to address; concerns about AI adoption in clinical care
HHS asks what patients/caregivers want addressed and what concerns they have.
Challenges patients/caregivers often want improved (system-level):
• Reduced administrative friction (scheduling, referrals, prior auth navigation, instructions clarity).
• Better continuity and coordination (the system “remembers” context across encounters).
• More transparent decision pathways (why something was recommended/denied).
Common concerns (trust & safety):
• Privacy and secondary use of sensitive data (HIPAA/Part 2 contexts). HHS+1
• Bias, unequal performance across populations, and lack of recourse when AI is wrong.
• Opaqueness: “who is accountable” and “how do I appeal or correct the record.”
Adoption recommendation:
• Require that AI-enabled workflows expose: (a) what the AI did, (b) what the human decided, (c) what data was
used at a high level, and (d) how to contest/correct—and log those interactions as part of the evidence trail.
10) AI research areas HHS should prioritize; published findings; costs/benefits/transfers
HHS asks what AI research to prioritize and how literature approaches impacts and economics.
Priority research gaps that unlock adoption (and align to ONC + CMS interests):
1. Operational governance science for AI in clinical workflow: measurement, auditability, safety-of-execution, and
how to design escalation/override UX that clinicians actually use.
2. Interoperability + benchmarking infrastructure: shared evaluation harnesses, standardized task definitions, and
reproducible “evidence packs” as first-class research outputs.
3. Implementation science for AI: what organization/role structures and operating models reliably scale beyond pilots
(cross-site generalizability).
4. Economics of operational controls: quantify the costs and benefits of monitoring, evidence, and governance (so
reimbursement and procurement can finance the controls that make AI safe).

Mechanisms likely to be most impactful:
• Cooperative agreements and contracts for reference architectures and open evaluation toolchains.
• Prize competitions for governance artifacts (evidence packs, reproducibility, drift detection/rollback), because these
are reusable across many clinical applications and reduce adoption friction.
