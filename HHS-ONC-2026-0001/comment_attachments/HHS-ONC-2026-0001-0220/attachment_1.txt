Introduction
The Center for Regulatory Freedom (CRF) supports responsible, innovation-driven reform in
health care delivery. Advances in data analytics, digital infrastructure, and artificial intelligence
present meaningful opportunities to modernize clinical workflows, reduce inefficiencies, and
1

strengthen the overall performance of the health system. Sound regulatory policy should enable
beneficial innovation while preserving the safeguards necessary to protect patients and sustain
trust in medical institutions.
Artificial intelligence, in particular, holds substantial promise. Properly deployed, AI systems
can reduce administrative burden on clinicians, streamline documentation requirements, assist
with longitudinal chart review, identify care gaps, and surface clinically relevant patterns that
might otherwise be overlooked in complex patient histories. In an era where physicians face
escalating paperwork obligations and time constraints, intelligent decision-support tools can
improve efficiency and allow clinicians to focus more fully on direct patient care.
At the same time, medical care is uniquely sensitive among regulated domains. Errors in this
context are not merely financial or reputational; they may produce irreversible physical harm.
Patients are often in vulnerable states, lack specialized knowledge, and must rely on professional
expertise. Moreover, health care involves profound information asymmetries between providers
and patients, as well as fiduciary obligations that distinguish it from most other commercial
environments. For these reasons, the governance framework for AI in medical care must be more
disciplined than general-purpose software regulation.
A further structural reality must inform HHS’s approach: the longstanding value of longitudinal
primary care relationships. Continuity with a primary care physician strengthens clinical
accuracy, improves medication management, reduces duplicative testing, and enhances early
detection of emerging conditions. A physician who knows a patient’s history—both medical and
contextual—is better positioned to make sound decisions than one encountering that patient for
the first time without adequate background.
However, evolving care models are reshaping how patients access services. Younger cohorts
increasingly rely on telehealth platforms, urgent care centers, and episodic or on-demand primary
care arrangements. While these models expand access and convenience, they also introduce
fragmentation risks. In such an environment, informational continuity becomes as important as
relational continuity. AI-assisted clinical briefing tools—designed to synthesize longitudinal
patient information at the point of care—may help bridge that gap, provided they are
appropriately governed.
The central policy question, therefore, is not whether AI should be used in medical care, but how
to preserve the safety and quality benefits of traditional primary care relationships while
accommodating technological advancement and evolving delivery models. The goal should be to
strengthen physician decision-making through structured, transparent information support—not
to displace clinical judgment or automate high-stakes decisions without accountability.
CRF’s position is that HHS should adopt a calibrated, risk-tiered governance framework for AI
in medical care. Permissionless innovation should remain the default posture for low-risk,
assistive functions that operate under clear transparency and human-review safeguards. At the
same time, heightened evidentiary and oversight requirements should apply where AI systems
materially influence diagnosis, triage, treatment selection, or other consequential clinical
decisions. Such a balanced framework will promote innovation, preserve patient safety, and
ensure that artificial intelligence serves as a tool to reinforce—rather than erode—the integrity of
the physician-patient relationship.

Executive Summary
Artificial intelligence is rapidly entering the clinical environment. Properly structured, these
technologies can enhance physician efficiency, reduce administrative burden, and improve
patient care. Improperly governed, however, they risk amplifying fragmentation, obscuring
accountability, and introducing preventable safety concerns. HHS faces a pivotal policy choice:
how to enable innovation in health care AI while preserving patient safety, physician judgment,
and system integrity.
CRF submits that the appropriate framework is neither blanket precaution nor unbounded
permissionless deployment. Instead, HHS should adopt a calibrated, risk-tiered, lifecycle
governance model that preserves innovation for low-risk assistive functions while applying
proportionate safeguards where AI materially influences clinical outcomes.
Substantively:
• Continuity of care is a measurable quality and safety asset. Longitudinal primary care
relationships reduce fragmentation, improve medication management, and enhance
clinical accuracy by preserving contextual patient knowledge.
• Care delivery models are evolving. Younger cohorts increasingly rely on episodic,
telehealth, and on-demand services, creating structural information discontinuity even as
access expands.
• AI-powered “clinical briefing” tools offer a potential bridge. Properly designed
systems can synthesize longitudinal patient data into concise, structured summaries that
enhance physician situational awareness at the point of care.
• Material risks must be addressed. These include hallucinated or fabricated data,
omission of critical contraindications, automation bias, privacy vulnerabilities, model
drift, and over-reliance that weakens independent clinical reasoning.
• A binary “precautionary vs. permissionless” framework is ill-suited to health care.
Medical AI presents heterogeneous risk profiles that require calibrated governance rather
than categorical approval or prohibition.
• CRF recommends a risk-tiered, lifecycle-based governance model:
o Permissionless deployment for low-risk assistive functions, subject to baseline
transparency, provenance, and human-review safeguards.
o Escalating oversight triggers for tools that materially influence diagnosis, triage,
treatment, or other consequential clinical decisions.
o Strong accountability, auditability, incident reporting, and post-deployment
performance monitoring to address model drift and real-world safety signals.
• HHS should harmonize governance across CMS, ONC, and FDA authorities.
Fragmented or duplicative regulatory approaches will undermine clarity, slow responsible
innovation, and increase compliance burdens without commensurate safety gains.
A disciplined, risk-tiered approach will allow AI to reinforce informational continuity in an era
of evolving care models, strengthen the physician-patient relationship, and improve affordability
through efficiency gains—while maintaining clear lines of accountability and patient safety
protections.

I. CONTINUITY OF CARE AS A QUALITY AND SAFETY FOUNDATION
Longitudinal primary care relationships function as a structural safeguard within the health care
system. A primary care physician who follows a patient over time accumulates contextual
knowledge that cannot be fully captured in isolated clinical notes. This continuity reduces
fragmentation in decision-making by anchoring diagnosis, treatment, and referral decisions in a
coherent understanding of the patient’s medical trajectory. In this sense, continuity is not merely
a matter of convenience; it is an operational mechanism for improving safety and clinical
precision.
A sustained physician–patient relationship strengthens diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic
calibration. Familiarity with prior medication responses, historical lab trends, chronic condition
progression, and patterns of symptom presentation enables clinicians to interpret new complaints
in light of established baselines. Subtle deviations that might appear insignificant in a single
encounter may carry greater meaning when viewed longitudinally. Likewise, knowledge of prior
adverse reactions or treatment failures helps prevent repetition of ineffective or harmful
interventions.
Continuity also extends beyond strictly clinical data. Primary care physicians often develop
awareness of social determinants, behavioral health factors, family history nuances, and lifestyle
constraints that influence treatment adherence and outcomes. Understanding a patient’s
employment schedule, caregiving responsibilities, transportation access, or financial limitations
can materially shape care planning. These contextual variables frequently determine whether a
treatment recommendation is feasible, sustainable, and safe.
When continuity erodes, the system incurs measurable inefficiencies and risks. Fragmented care
environments increase the likelihood of duplicative testing, inconsistent medication
management, and missed preventive screenings. Episodic providers who lack access to complete
patient histories may order repeat diagnostics out of caution, inadvertently raising costs and
patient burden. More concerning, incomplete knowledge of prior contraindications or drug
interactions can introduce avoidable safety hazards.
Discontinuity also weakens follow-through. When responsibility for care is diffuse,
accountability for monitoring results, adjusting medications, or ensuring specialist coordination
can become unclear. Missed follow-ups, unmanaged chronic conditions, and delayed
interventions become more likely in the absence of a central coordinating clinician. Continuity
therefore functions as a stabilizing force in an otherwise complex and decentralized system.
At the same time, care delivery patterns are evolving. Younger cohorts increasingly utilize
telehealth platforms, urgent care centers, retail clinics, and other on-demand service models.
These models expand access and convenience, and they may lower certain barriers to entry for
patients who might otherwise defer care. However, they inherently reduce relational continuity
and increase the likelihood that individual encounters occur without the benefit of long-term
contextual knowledge.
In this environment, policy should prioritize preservation of informational continuity even where
relational continuity declines. If patients increasingly move between providers or platforms, the
system must ensure that longitudinal medical information remains coherent, accessible, and
4

clinically actionable. The objective should not be to resist innovation in care access models, but
to mitigate the fragmentation risks that accompany them.
Artificial intelligence, properly structured, may serve as a continuity amplifier rather than a
substitute for physician judgment. AI-powered clinical briefing tools can synthesize longitudinal
data into structured summaries that restore situational awareness at the point of care. However,
these tools must operate in support of—rather than in place of—the primary care physician’s
reasoning. Continuity ultimately resides in accountable human judgment; technology should
strengthen that judgment by improving information clarity, not displace it.
II. THE “AI CLINICAL BRIEFING” MODEL: OPPORTUNITY AND FUNCTIONAL
ROLE
The specific use case CRF supports is the AI-powered “clinical briefing” tool deployed
immediately prior to a patient encounter. These systems ingest structured and unstructured data
from electronic health records—including problem lists, medication histories, laboratory trends,
imaging results, prior encounter notes, discharge summaries, and referral documentation—and
synthesize that information into a concise, structured pre-visit summary for the treating clinician.
The purpose is to reduce cognitive and time burdens associated with manual chart review while
preserving access to the underlying data.
Modern EHR environments often contain years of accumulated information presented in
fragmented, duplicative, or poorly organized formats. Clinicians operating under time constraints
may face dozens or even hundreds of pages of notes for a complex patient. AI summarization
tools can compress that volume into an actionable briefing that highlights relevant history, recent
changes, unresolved issues, and potential risk factors. When properly designed, such tools
enhance situational awareness without displacing clinical discretion.
It is essential to distinguish between three distinct functional categories. First, administrative
summarization tools primarily organize and condense information already present in the record.
They may extract key data points, compile timelines, or generate structured summaries, but they
do not introduce new diagnostic or treatment recommendations. These tools function as
informational aids rather than decision engines.
Second, clinical insight–highlighting tools go beyond simple summarization by identifying
patterns or flagging potential concerns. For example, they may highlight medication interactions,
overdue screenings, abnormal lab trends, or inconsistencies in documentation. While still
assistive, these systems begin to shape clinical attention and therefore carry incrementally greater
influence over decision-making.
Third, diagnostic or treatment recommendation engines actively suggest courses of action, risk
scores, triage determinations, or therapeutic pathways. These systems materially influence
clinical judgment and may, in some contexts, function with a degree of autonomy. They operate
at a higher risk tier and require correspondingly stronger validation and oversight.
The “AI clinical briefing” model contemplated here is primarily focused on the first two
categories—administrative summarization and bounded insight highlighting—rather than
autonomous diagnostic decision-making. The goal is to improve information clarity, not to
5

substitute algorithmic judgment for physician reasoning. The tool’s core function is compression
and organization, not independent clinical authority.
Properly designed briefing systems can materially reduce chart-review burden. By surfacing
medication changes, prior adverse events, hospitalizations, and pending follow-ups in a
structured format, they allow clinicians to enter the patient encounter better prepared and more
focused. This improved preparation may enhance both efficiency and quality, particularly in
environments where patients move between providers or care settings.
Importantly, the objective is information compression rather than clinical replacement. The
physician remains responsible for interpreting the summarized information, validating its
relevance, and making ultimate care decisions. The tool should present data with traceability and
transparency, enabling clinicians to drill down into underlying records as needed. At no point
should the system obscure the source of its conclusions or operate beyond physician oversight.
Integration into physician workflow is critical. The briefing must be delivered in a manner that
supports pre-visit preparation without disrupting established clinical processes. It should be
available within the existing EHR environment, clearly labeled as assistive, and accompanied by
appropriate disclaimers regarding scope and limitations. The system should facilitate review and
correction, not create friction or encourage passive acceptance.
When appropriately bounded—limited to summarization and structured highlighting, operating
under explicit physician authority, and supported by transparency safeguards—the AI clinical
briefing model represents a low-to-moderate risk application of artificial intelligence in health
care. Its potential to restore informational continuity in fragmented care environments justifies a
governance posture that enables deployment while maintaining disciplined guardrails. Properly
calibrated policy can allow these tools to strengthen physician performance without
compromising safety or accountability.
III. RISK CATEGORIES AND FAILURE MODES IN CLINICAL AI
Artificial intelligence in clinical environments presents heterogeneous and context-dependent
risks. A disciplined regulatory framework must begin with a clear understanding of distinct
failure modes rather than treating “AI risk” as a monolithic concept. The safety profile of an
assistive chart summarization tool differs materially from that of an autonomous diagnostic
engine, and policy must reflect those distinctions. The following categories outline the principal
risk domains relevant to AI-enabled clinical briefing systems and related applications.
A careful delineation of these risks is not an argument against innovation. Rather, it is a
prerequisite to proportionate governance. Identifying how systems fail—technically,
operationally, and behaviorally—enables regulators to design guardrails that preserve benefits
while mitigating foreseeable harm.
6

A. Hallucination Risk
Hallucination risk refers to the generation of fabricated or unsupported information. In a
clinical context, this may include nonexistent diagnoses, incorrect medication lists,
spurious allergy notations, or inaccurate descriptions of prior medical events. Even a
single fabricated data point can distort clinical reasoning if it appears authoritative.
The legal and patient safety implications are substantial. A hallucinated allergy could
improperly constrain treatment options; a fabricated prior diagnosis could lead to
unnecessary testing or inappropriate referrals; an inaccurate medication entry could
introduce dangerous drug interactions. Because clinicians often operate under time
pressure, they may not immediately detect subtle inaccuracies embedded within a
polished summary.
Unlike simple typographical errors, hallucinations carry an epistemic risk: they present as
coherent and plausible statements. This makes them more difficult to detect through
casual review. In high-acuity settings, even minor inaccuracies may cascade into
consequential errors.
Mitigation requires design features such as source traceability, confidence indicators, and
explicit differentiation between extracted data and inferred conclusions. Systems must
make it operationally easy for clinicians to verify claims against the underlying record.
Transparency is the primary countermeasure to hallucination risk.
B. Omission Risk
Omission risk arises when a system fails to surface critical information that is present in
the underlying record. In many clinical scenarios, omission may pose a greater danger
than fabrication. A missing notation regarding a prior adverse drug reaction,
anticoagulant use, or cardiac event can have immediate and serious consequences.
Clinical decision-making often depends on identifying contraindications or outlier events
within large datasets. If a summarization system compresses information in a way that
excludes such signals, clinicians may proceed under incomplete assumptions. The brevity
that enhances efficiency can inadvertently obscure nuance.
Omission risk is especially pronounced in longitudinal records containing years of
documentation across multiple providers and systems. Data fragmentation, inconsistent
coding, and unstructured narrative notes can make it difficult for algorithms to detect
relevant entries, increasing the likelihood that critical information is not highlighted.
Effective mitigation requires structured completeness checks, explicit acknowledgment
of data sources reviewed, and clear indicators when records are incomplete or
unavailable. Systems should err on the side of signaling uncertainty rather than
presenting a falsely comprehensive summary.
7

C. Automation Bias
Automation bias refers to the human tendency to over-rely on automated outputs,
particularly when those outputs are presented in a clear and authoritative format. In time-
constrained clinical environments, physicians may unconsciously defer to summarized or
highlighted information rather than independently interrogating the full record.
Interface design can exacerbate this tendency. Clean dashboards, risk scores, and concise
bullet-point summaries can create a perception of comprehensiveness even when the
system is assistive rather than determinative. The more seamless the integration, the
greater the risk that clinicians internalize AI outputs as definitive.
Over time, excessive reliance on automated summaries may degrade independent clinical
reasoning. If physicians become habituated to accepting compressed narratives without
verification, diagnostic vigilance may decline. This dynamic is particularly concerning in
training environments where less-experienced clinicians may treat AI output as
authoritative guidance.
Mitigating automation bias requires deliberate workflow design. Systems should clearly
indicate their assistive nature, facilitate easy access to underlying documentation, and
incorporate mechanisms for clinician review and correction. Accountability should
remain firmly anchored in human decision-makers.
D. Distribution Shift and Model Drift
Clinical environments are dynamic. Patient demographics change, treatment standards
evolve, new medications enter the market, and coding practices are revised. Distribution
shift occurs when the data environment in which a model operates diverges from the
environment in which it was trained.
Over time, such shifts can degrade performance. A model trained on historical patterns
may fail to recognize emerging conditions or updated therapeutic standards. In
summarization contexts, evolving documentation practices may alter how information is
recorded, reducing the system’s ability to extract relevant content accurately.
Model drift may occur gradually and invisibly. Performance degradation can accumulate
without obvious failure events, especially in assistive systems where outputs are not
directly benchmarked against clear ground truth metrics. This creates a latent safety risk.
Addressing distribution shift requires lifecycle governance. Ongoing monitoring, periodic
performance reassessment, and structured update protocols are essential. AI in health care
cannot be treated as static software; it must be subject to adaptive oversight consistent
with the dynamic nature of clinical practice.
E. Privacy and Security Risks
AI clinical systems necessarily process sensitive protected health information. This
creates heightened privacy and security obligations. Unauthorized access, data breaches,
8

or inadequate encryption protocols can expose patients to identity theft, discrimination, or
reputational harm.
Beyond traditional cybersecurity concerns, AI systems introduce risks related to
secondary use of data. Training processes, model improvement cycles, or third-party
integrations may expand data exposure beyond the original purpose for which
information was collected. Without clear guardrails, mission creep can erode patient trust.
Large-scale data aggregation also increases the attractiveness of systems as cyber targets.
The concentration of longitudinal health records within AI-enabled platforms amplifies
the consequences of security failures.
Mitigation requires strict adherence to purpose limitation, data minimization, access
controls, and audit logging. Governance structures must clearly define who may access
data, for what purposes, and under what safeguards. Privacy and security are not
peripheral concerns but foundational conditions for sustained adoption.
In evaluating these risk categories, it is essential to recognize that magnitude varies significantly
by use case and degree of autonomy. A bounded assistive summarization tool presents a different
risk profile than an autonomous diagnostic or treatment engine. Accordingly, governance should
scale with material clinical influence rather than applying uniform constraints across
heterogeneous technologies. A calibrated framework that distinguishes between low-risk
assistance and high-impact autonomy will better protect patients while preserving the benefits of
innovation.
IV. PERMISSIONLESS INNOVATION AND THE PRECAUTIONARY PRINCIPLE IN
MEDICAL AI
Debates over emerging technologies often collapse into a false binary: either innovation should
proceed without regulatory friction, or precaution should dominate to prevent potential harm. In
the context of medical AI, neither extreme is appropriate. A purely precautionary posture that
demands exhaustive pre-deployment proof for all AI-enabled tools would freeze low-risk
innovation and delay beneficial improvements in efficiency and care coordination. Conversely,
an unbounded permissionless approach would expose patients to preventable risks in high-stakes
clinical environments.
CRF rejects precautionary paralysis where assistive technologies pose minimal and reversible
risk. Low-risk tools that organize information, summarize records, or highlight existing data—
when deployed under clear transparency and human-review safeguards—should not be subjected
to disproportionate regulatory barriers. Excessive friction in these domains would preserve
administrative inefficiencies and undermine opportunities to improve informational continuity.
At the same time, CRF rejects the notion that medical AI should be deployed without calibrated
oversight in high-risk contexts. Where AI systems materially influence diagnostic pathways,
triage decisions, or treatment selection, the stakes rise significantly. In such circumstances, the
consequences of error are not abstract; they may directly affect patient morbidity or mortality.
Regulatory restraint must not become regulatory abdication.
9

Accordingly, CRF proposes a hybrid standard. Permissionless innovation should remain the
default posture for reversible, assistive, and non-autonomous functions. These include bounded
summarization tools and informational aids that operate under physician supervision and do not
independently initiate or execute clinical actions. Such tools should be permitted to deploy
subject to baseline transparency, traceability, and accountability safeguards.
Escalating safeguards should apply where AI systems exert what CRF defines as “Material
Clinical Influence.” Material Clinical Influence exists when AI outputs could reasonably alter
diagnosis, triage, or treatment decisions. In these contexts, validation, performance
benchmarking, monitoring, and clear delineation of intended use become essential to protect
patient safety.
CRF further defines “Autonomous Action” as AI-driven execution of clinical decisions or
administrative determinations without contemporaneous physician verification. Autonomous
routing of patients, automated treatment adjustments, or coverage determinations made without
human review represent a qualitatively different risk tier. It is autonomy—not mere assistance—
that should trigger heightened oversight, evidentiary requirements, and structured accountability
mechanisms.
This distinction between assistance and autonomy is critical. Many AI-enabled tools enhance
human judgment without displacing it. When clinicians retain authority, review outputs, and
exercise independent reasoning, the risk profile remains bounded. When AI systems act
independently or create de facto determinative outcomes, oversight must increase proportionally.
Finally, innovation discipline should be understood as a trust-building mechanism rather than a
constraint on progress. Clear standards that distinguish low-risk assistive tools from high-impact
autonomous systems provide certainty to developers, clinicians, and patients alike. A calibrated
framework that protects safety while enabling responsible experimentation will strengthen public
confidence in medical AI and promote sustainable technological advancement within the health
care system.
V. RISK-TIERED GOVERNANCE FRAMEWORK FOR HHS
A durable policy framework for AI in medical care must move beyond abstract principles and
provide operational clarity. HHS should adopt a risk-tiered governance architecture that scales
oversight to functional impact and autonomy level. Such an approach promotes innovation where
risk is bounded while ensuring that systems with material clinical consequences are subject to
proportionate safeguards. The objective is not to regulate “AI” as a monolith, but to regulate
specific use cases according to their real-world risk profile.
A tiered model also provides regulatory certainty. Developers, health systems, and clinicians
benefit from knowing in advance which category their tools fall into and what compliance
expectations attach to each tier. Clear delineation reduces ambiguity, avoids over-deterrence of
low-risk innovation, and concentrates oversight resources where patient safety implications are
most significant.
10

Tier 1: Assistive / Informational Tools
Tier 1 encompasses assistive and informational systems that support clinicians without
independently influencing or executing clinical decisions. These include documentation
drafting tools, chart summarization systems, and automated care gap reminders. Their
primary function is organizational or clerical efficiency rather than diagnostic or
therapeutic determination.
These systems operate within physician workflow and under direct human supervision.
They do not independently initiate clinical action, nor do they generate prescriptive
treatment recommendations. While they may influence clinician awareness, they remain
fundamentally assistive and reversible in impact.
Baseline governance requirements should apply uniformly across Tier 1 tools.
Provenance transparency must be embedded by default, enabling clinicians to trace
summarized content back to underlying source records. Systems should clearly disclose
which data sources were included and whether external or incomplete records were
excluded.
In addition, Tier 1 tools should be clearly labeled as assistive within the user interface,
avoiding any implication of autonomous authority. Audit logging should capture system
usage and clinician review activity to preserve accountability and enable post hoc
evaluation if necessary. These safeguards are sufficient for bounded, low-risk
applications.
Tier 2: Decision-Influencing Tools
Tier 2 includes systems that meaningfully influence clinical judgment, even if they do not
execute decisions autonomously. Examples include risk scoring tools, triage prioritization
systems, and diagnostic suggestion engines. These systems can shape physician attention
and alter decision pathways, thereby elevating their risk profile.
While physicians retain ultimate authority, Tier 2 tools may exert Material Clinical
Influence. Their outputs can reasonably affect diagnosis, referral patterns, medication
choices, or patient prioritization. As such, they warrant stronger evidentiary and
governance requirements than purely assistive tools.
Context-specific validation should be required prior to deployment. Performance
benchmarking must demonstrate that the system operates reliably within the intended
patient population and clinical setting. Error tolerance thresholds should be explicitly
defined, particularly where outputs may affect high-acuity or vulnerable populations.
Tier 2 systems should also incorporate structured physician attestation workflows.
Clinicians should confirm review of AI-generated outputs before finalizing clinical
decisions. This reinforces human accountability and mitigates automation bias. Ongoing
performance monitoring should detect drift or emerging safety concerns over time.
11

Tier 3: Autonomous or High-Impact Systems
Tier 3 encompasses autonomous or high-impact systems capable of executing or
effectively determining outcomes without contemporaneous physician verification.
Examples include independent triage routing, automated authorization or coverage
determinations, and direct execution of clinical actions such as dosage adjustments or
treatment pathway activation.
These systems present the highest risk profile because autonomy introduces the potential
for harm without immediate human correction. Even if designed to optimize efficiency,
autonomous systems may create systemic errors that scale rapidly if not properly
governed. Accordingly, Tier 3 tools require the most rigorous oversight architecture.
A pre-deployment safety case should be mandatory for Tier 3 systems. This includes
clearly defined intended use, validation within the target population, explicit performance
metrics, and documented risk mitigation strategies. Regulators should require
demonstration that the system performs at least as well as established clinical standards
under realistic operating conditions.
Post-deployment obligations must include ongoing performance monitoring, structured
reporting of adverse events, and periodic reassessment of model accuracy. Independent
review pathways should allow clinicians or affected parties to challenge system outputs.
Override and appeals mechanisms must be easily accessible and functionally meaningful
to ensure that autonomy does not displace accountability.
A risk-tiered governance framework enables HHS to balance innovation with safety in a
principled and operationally coherent manner. By scaling oversight to functional impact and
autonomy level, the Department can preserve permissionless deployment for low-risk assistive
tools while imposing proportionate safeguards where patient safety implications intensify. This
architecture promotes clarity, accountability, and sustained public trust in AI-enabled medical
care.
VI. HUMAN ACCOUNTABILITY AND WORKFLOW INTEGRATION
The introduction of AI into clinical environments must not obscure a foundational principle:
physicians remain the ultimate decision-makers in patient care. No deployment model should
dilute professional accountability or create ambiguity regarding responsibility for diagnosis,
treatment, or follow-up. AI systems may inform and support clinical judgment, but they must not
displace the physician’s fiduciary and ethical obligations to the patient.
Preserving physician primacy requires deliberate interface design. User interfaces should
encourage active review rather than passive acceptance of AI-generated outputs. Visual
presentation should clearly indicate that summaries, risk scores, or highlighted insights are
assistive tools. The system should prompt engagement—such as confirmation steps or
expandable evidence views—rather than defaulting to silent incorporation into the clinical
record.
12

Traceability is a core safeguard. Clinicians must be able to click through from any summarized
statement or highlighted alert directly to the underlying source data within the electronic health
record. This traceability allows physicians to validate accuracy, assess context, and identify
potential omissions. Without easy access to primary data, clinicians may be forced to rely on
compressed summaries without sufficient verification.
Accountability mechanisms should include documentation of physician corrections or overrides.
When a clinician identifies an inaccuracy, clarifies context, or rejects a suggested insight, that
interaction should be logged in a structured manner. This documentation not only preserves legal
clarity but also creates an opportunity for iterative system improvement. Correction pathways
should be efficient and integrated into workflow rather than burdensome.
Continuous feedback loops are essential to responsible AI deployment. Health systems and
developers should establish processes through which clinician feedback is aggregated, analyzed,
and used to refine model performance. Patterns of recurrent correction or misunderstanding may
signal systemic issues requiring recalibration. Governance should treat clinician interaction as an
input to ongoing quality assurance rather than a one-time review.
Workflow integration must be calibrated to avoid cognitive overload. AI tools should reduce
friction, not create additional documentation tasks that undermine efficiency gains. Human
oversight should be meaningful but streamlined, reinforcing thoughtful engagement without
imposing redundant administrative steps. Effective integration requires close alignment with
real-world clinical processes.
Ultimately, AI should augment clinical reasoning, not erode it. The objective is to enhance
situational awareness, improve informational clarity, and reduce administrative distraction so
that physicians can exercise independent judgment more effectively. A governance framework
that embeds transparency, traceability, and correction mechanisms into workflow will preserve
physician authority while enabling the benefits of technological advancement.
VII. POST-DEPLOYMENT MONITORING AND ADAPTIVE GOVERNANCE
Artificial intelligence systems in clinical environments cannot be treated as static products.
Unlike traditional medical devices or fixed software applications, AI models evolve over time
and operate within dynamic care settings. Patient populations shift, documentation practices
change, and standards of care advance. For these reasons, governance must extend beyond initial
deployment and incorporate structured, lifecycle-based oversight.
HHS should require periodic reassessment of model performance, calibrated to risk tier.
Assistive tools may warrant routine evaluation at defined intervals, while systems exerting
Material Clinical Influence should undergo more frequent and rigorous review. Performance
reassessment should examine accuracy, error rates, false-positive and false-negative patterns, and
any observable degradation relative to initial validation benchmarks.
Significant adverse events plausibly linked to AI outputs should be subject to structured
reporting requirements. Where an AI-generated summary, recommendation, or autonomous
action contributes to patient harm or near-miss events, health systems should document and
13

report those occurrences through appropriate oversight channels. Transparent reporting promotes
accountability and facilitates system-wide learning rather than isolated corrective action.
HHS should also encourage structured data collection to evaluate real-world impact. Deployment
should not rest solely on pre-launch validation; instead, agencies should promote longitudinal
assessment of clinical outcomes, workflow efficiency, patient safety indicators, and potential
disparities. Real-world evidence can inform whether systems are delivering promised benefits or
introducing unintended consequences.
Clear update protocols are essential to responsible lifecycle governance. When models are
modified—whether through retraining, parameter adjustment, or expanded scope—those updates
should be documented and, where appropriate, revalidated. Governance should distinguish
between minor performance refinements and material functional changes that alter risk profiles.
Structured version control and defined approval pathways enhance clarity and stability.
Transparency regarding model revisions strengthens trust among clinicians and patients. Health
systems should maintain accessible documentation describing what changes were made, why
they were implemented, and how performance was assessed. Silent or opaque updates undermine
accountability and may obscure emerging safety signals.
Finally, HHS should consider sunset or formal review triggers for higher-risk deployments.
Autonomous or high-impact systems should not operate indefinitely without structured
reevaluation. Periodic renewal, contingent upon demonstrated safety and performance, reinforces
adaptive governance. By embedding monitoring, transparency, and reassessment into policy
architecture, HHS can ensure that innovation remains aligned with patient safety over time rather
than relying solely on initial deployment assumptions.
VIII. FEDERAL COORDINATION AND REGULATORY CLARITY
The effectiveness of any AI governance framework depends not only on substantive standards
but also on institutional coherence. Within HHS, multiple components exercise jurisdiction over
aspects of health care technology, including payment policy, health IT certification, and medical
device regulation. Without deliberate coordination, overlapping authorities may produce
fragmented guidance, duplicative compliance obligations, and regulatory uncertainty that
discourages responsible innovation.
Alignment between CMS, ONC, and FDA is particularly important in the context of AI-enabled
clinical tools. CMS influences adoption through reimbursement structures and participation
requirements. ONC shapes health IT architecture and certification standards. FDA oversees
certain software functions that qualify as regulated medical devices. A coherent federal approach
requires these components to operate from a shared risk-tiered framework rather than issuing
isolated or inconsistent directives.
Clear boundaries between clinical decision support functions and device-regulated systems are
essential. Developers and health systems must understand when a tool is treated as assistive CDS
and when it crosses into device territory requiring additional regulatory processes. Ambiguity in
these boundaries can chill innovation at the margins and create compliance risk that exceeds the
actual safety concern.
14

HHS should also work to avoid duplicative or conflicting compliance regimes. If transparency
requirements under health IT certification diverge from expectations under payment policy or
device oversight, stakeholders may face redundant reporting and validation burdens. Harmonized
standards reduce administrative cost while maintaining safety protections.
Interoperability and data portability are foundational to safe AI deployment. Clinical briefing
tools and related systems depend on accurate, comprehensive data drawn from multiple sources.
Federal policy should promote consistent data standards, secure exchange mechanisms, and
portability across platforms. Fragmented data environments undermine both performance and
patient safety.
Consistency in transparency expectations across federal programs is equally important. If CMS
requires certain disclosures for AI-enabled tools used in Medicare settings, those expectations
should align with ONC certification requirements and FDA guidance where applicable. A
unified transparency baseline strengthens predictability and fosters trust among clinicians and
patients.
Coordinated federal policy also improves market signaling. Developers are more likely to invest
in compliant, safety-focused systems when regulatory expectations are clear and stable.
Conversely, shifting or inconsistent standards encourage minimal compliance strategies and
reactive adjustments rather than proactive safety design.
By harmonizing oversight across HHS components, clarifying jurisdictional boundaries, and
aligning transparency and interoperability standards, the Department can reduce fragmentation
while preserving robust safeguards. Regulatory clarity is not merely an administrative
convenience; it is a precondition for safe, scalable, and trustworthy deployment of AI in medical
care.
IX. PRESERVING PATIENT TRUST AND ACCESS
Patient trust is the foundation upon which all health care innovation rests. Technological
advancement that undermines confidence in clinical integrity will ultimately fail, regardless of its
theoretical efficiency gains. HHS must therefore evaluate AI governance not only through a
safety lens, but also through its effect on patient trust, system stability, and equitable access to
care.
Excessive regulatory uncertainty can deter beneficial innovation. When developers and health
systems lack clarity regarding compliance expectations, risk thresholds, or jurisdictional
boundaries, investment slows and responsible experimentation is deferred. In health care, where
administrative burden is already substantial, additional ambiguity may discourage deployment of
tools that could reduce clinician workload and improve care coordination.
At the same time, poorly governed AI systems can erode trust even more quickly. High-profile
failures, opaque decision-making, or unexplained clinical outcomes may lead patients to question
whether technology has displaced professional judgment. Trust once lost is difficult to restore. A
governance framework that anticipates foreseeable risks and embeds transparency mechanisms
helps prevent reactive regulatory backlash driven by isolated but visible incidents.
15

Trust is also closely linked to equity. If AI systems are deployed without adequate validation
across diverse patient populations, they risk reinforcing disparities. Performance gaps across
demographic groups can magnify existing inequities in diagnosis, treatment, or access.
Disciplined governance that requires performance monitoring and correction supports broader,
more equitable adoption.
Regulatory clarity encourages responsible private-sector investment. Developers are more likely
to allocate capital toward safety-conscious, interoperable systems when standards are predictable
and proportionate. Clear tiered requirements allow firms to design compliance pathways from
the outset rather than retrofitting systems after deployment. This stability supports long-term
innovation rather than short-term opportunism.
Calibrated oversight also promotes affordability. Assistive AI tools that reduce chart-review
time, streamline documentation, and minimize duplicative testing can lower administrative
overhead across the health system. When governance is proportionate to risk, low-impact tools
can deploy without unnecessary friction, enabling efficiency gains that translate into cost
containment.
Conversely, overregulation of low-risk assistive functions may entrench existing inefficiencies.
If health systems are prevented from adopting technologies that meaningfully reduce clinician
burden, administrative costs remain embedded in the delivery system and are ultimately borne by
patients and taxpayers. A risk-tiered approach ensures that safeguards do not inadvertently block
cost-saving innovation.
In sum, preserving patient trust and expanding access are not in tension with innovation; they are
dependent upon disciplined governance. Clear standards, proportional oversight, and transparent
accountability mechanisms foster confidence among patients, clinicians, and developers alike.
By calibrating regulatory expectations to actual risk, HHS can promote responsible innovation
that enhances affordability, broadens access, and strengthens trust in the evolving health care
system.
Conclusion
The Center for Regulatory Freedom strongly supports innovation that strengthens—rather than
fragments—the primary care foundation of the American health system. Technological
advancement should reinforce the physician-patient relationship, reduce unnecessary
administrative burden, and improve clinical precision. Artificial intelligence, if deployed
responsibly, can serve these ends by enhancing physician awareness and restoring coherence to
increasingly complex medical records.
As care delivery models evolve toward greater use of episodic and on-demand services,
informational continuity becomes the critical bridge between traditional longitudinal primary
care relationships and emerging access pathways. Even where relational continuity declines, the
system must preserve the integrity of longitudinal patient information. AI-powered clinical
briefing tools offer a means of compressing and organizing that information so physicians can
enter encounters informed and prepared.
16

However, AI must remain a tool of augmentation—not substitution. Properly bounded systems
can amplify physician insight and reduce fragmentation. Poorly governed systems risk obscuring
accountability, introducing error, and undermining public trust. The policy challenge is therefore
not whether to enable AI in health care, but how to calibrate governance to preserve safety and
clinical integrity.
CRF urges HHS to adopt a risk-tiered, lifecycle-based framework that distinguishes assistive
tools from materially influential or autonomous systems. Permissionless innovation should
remain the default for low-risk, reversible functions operating under clear transparency
safeguards. At the same time, heightened validation, monitoring, and accountability requirements
should apply where AI materially influences diagnosis, triage, or treatment decisions.
A clear, harmonized, and forward-looking regulatory architecture will support responsible
innovation, encourage private-sector investment, and protect patients. Alignment across CMS,
ONC, and FDA authorities will reduce fragmentation and provide the predictability necessary for
sustained technological development. Calibrated oversight can improve efficiency, strengthen
affordability, and preserve confidence in clinical decision-making.
CRF remains committed to disciplined, economically grounded, patient-centered regulatory
reform. By adopting a balanced framework that protects safety while enabling progress, HHS
can ensure that artificial intelligence serves as a continuity-enhancing force in American health
care—advancing innovation without compromising the integrity of primary care.
