To whom it may concern,
On behalf of the Medical Device Manufacturers Association (MDMA), below please find comments
on the Request for Information, Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part
of Clinical Care.
MDMA is a national trade association that provides educational and advocacy assistance to hundreds
of innovative companies in the field of medical technology. Our members, the majority of which are
small to mid-sized medical device companies, have a strong record of delivering breakthrough
therapies to treat chronic diseases and life-threatening conditions while lowering the cost of care.
MDMA’s mission is to ensure that patients have timely access to the latest advancements of safe and
effective medical technologies that improve health outcomes. We appreciate the opportunity to
provide these comments, and we look forward to ongoing collaboration with the Assistant Secretary
for Technology Policy and the Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology
on this important issue.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption
and use in clinical care?
AI has been incorporated into regulated medical devices for three decades, and the existing
Food and Drug Administration (FDA) medical device regulatory requirements remain
appropriate for these devices. As more novel AI technologies emerge in medical devices
(e.g., generative AI and agentic AI), it is important to assess whether any specific updates to
the regulatory framework may be needed to address their unique characteristics.
We encourage FDA to maintain a risk-based, technology-agnostic approach that is consistent
with its longstanding device framework. Regulatory oversight should be proportionate to the
clinical risk posed by the device and the degree of AI autonomy or system influence, rather
than triggered solely by the presence of AI. Devices incorporating limited AI functionality
should not be subject to the same expectations as systems that generate outputs or modify

clinically actionable outputs. This approach will foster continued innovation while preserving
patient safety.
Furthermore, it is essential that the FDA remains the primary regulatory authority for AI-
enabled medical devices. Establishing separate regulatory requirements or oversight bodies
for AI would add significant complexity and regulatory burden, hindering innovation without
providing additional benefits to patient safety.
We also urge the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) to advance standards and
interoperability by prioritizing evaluation and adoption of AI-relevant standards, including
model lifecycle, data/metadata quality, and transparency. This will help reduce fragmentation
for both providers and manufacturers.
HHS should advance US leadership in internationally recognized consensus standards and
regulatory cooperation mechanisms for AI-enabled health technologies to reduce duplicative
requirements across markets, strengthen American competitiveness, and accelerate timely
patient access, while preserving FDA’s risk-based statutory authority.
Unclear regulatory boundaries exist between the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy/
Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology (ASTP/ONC),
decision support interventions (DSIs), and predictive decision support interventions (pDSIs),
administrative AI, and workflow optimization tools. There is also fragmented oversight
between ASTP/ONC, FDA clinical decision support (CDS), and the Centers for Medicare
and Medicaid Services (CMS), which creates uncertainty for developers seeking to
understand applicable requirements across the AI lifecycle and highlights the need for better
cross-agency coordination to harmonize expectations for AI driven health technologies.
Lack of predictable expectations for model updates, learning systems, and post-deployment
monitoring is another barrier to private sector innovation and clinical adoption. Additionally,
misalignment between ASTP/ONC lifecycle frameworks and medical device regulatory
requirements result in unclear regulatory expectations. Specifically, it would provide clarity
if the marketing requirements for FDA regulated devices were the only regulatory
requirements, without any overlapping requirements from ONC.
We’d also encourage HHS to advance regulatory and reimbursement approaches for inpatient
and hospital outpatient settings in addition to decentralized setting since in these settings, AI
enabled medical technologies can have a significant impact on both cost and care delivery.
Finally, unpredictable and unclear reimbursement pathways present barriers to private sector
innovation in AI for health care and its adoption and use in clinical care. Because the
coverage, coding, and payment methodologies of CMS and other payers have not kept pace
with technology, developers face significant risk and uncertainty with respect to whether
physicians, hospitals, and other providers will be appropriately and adequately reimbursed
for utilizing their products, which can slow and even stifle innovation.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or
programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
Please provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.

AI is increasingly embedded within regulated medical devices, diagnostics, and integrated
hardware-software ecosystems to help support clinical decision making and therapeutic and
diagnostic interventions. It can allow for improved visualization, patient-specific plans, real-
time intraoperative decision support, and holds the potential to improve clinical outcomes
while reducing workforce burdens across the care team, including surgeons and medical
staff.
The challenge is that existing reimbursement systems are tied to the costs of earlier versions
of supplies and equipment that do not reflect the value, complexity, or ongoing costs of AI-
enabled systems. While billing codes may be used in some cases to report the procedures
performed, the incremental value and costs of AI-powered solutions are not captured in
payment rates. We urge HHS/CMS to consider reimbursement mechanisms that facilitate
adoption of these new technologies.
A reliable and predictable pathway to payment for AI and algorithmic tools is critical. Lack
of such a pathway creates uncertainty for manufacturers and investors alike, limiting the
development and adoption of AI-driven solutions. Venture investors consider a clear coding
and reimbursement strategy to be the single most important factor when deciding whether to
fund AI-based medical device solutions. For established medical technology manufacturers,
this uncertainty also constrains long-term capital planning, lifecycle software investment, and
U.S.-based innovation. Without a reliable pathway to payment, these innovations risk
reduced venture investment, slower adoption, and limited market uptake.
Further, we support the reimbursement for AI enablement via CPT or other codes
that are tied to demonstrable improvements in quality of care, reduction of errors, efficiency
gains, predictability, reproducibility, and productivity improvements resulting from AI use
(consistent with ACLA and American Medical Association positions). These mechanisms
should recognize AI functionality when it demonstrably contributes to improved outcomes,
safety, or efficiency whether as an integral component of medical devices, or as stand-alone
clinical tools. This might include supporting value-based reimbursement pathways,
reimbursing as part of care bundles, risk-based contracts, quality improvement programs, and
outcome-based reimbursement.
We recommend that CMS/ CMMI pilot an AI reimbursement model specifically for AI
enabled diagnostics, robotics, laboratory applications, operational improvements, outcome
metrics, and other measurable clinical and system-level benefits. Pilots should include
hospital and ambulatory surgical center settings and should account for the unique needs of
capital-intensive technologies that rely on continuous software improvement, with a clearly
defined pathway to transition successful pilots into permanent coding, payment, or coverage
mechanisms to accelerate sustainable reimbursement and broader adoption. CMS should
ensure that any new AI-specific reimbursement models supplement existing pathways rather
than supplant technologies that have already secured coverage. For AI-powered solutions
with established coding and payment, these existing frameworks must be preserved to
maintain market stability and patient access.
When new medical technologies emerge that deploy AI and have no predicate 510(k), it
would be helpful to have a more predictable review process than the one currently in place
for the De Novo pathway. The Medical Device User Fee Amendments (MDUFA) De Novo
target is to have only 70% of submissions meet a 150-day review target. Further, to date,

only about 50% of De Novos have obtained a positive determination allowing marketing the
subject product. The de novo process thus is an uncertain one that makes projecting and
preparing for product launch challenging. This level of unpredictability is particularly
difficult for AI-enabled platforms that require synchronized regulatory approval,
reimbursement planning, and clinical adoption. We urge HHS/FDA to implement
improvements to the De Novo process to address these issues, including issuance of clearer
evidentiary expectations, greater transparency on review timelines, and more consistent
application of standards.
While not exclusive but certainly related to AI product reviews, manufacturers need clearer
guidance on the acceptability of data originating outside of the US. Moreover, further
clarification on what demographic and site differentiations are required, both for training and
testing, also would help define the type of data required for submissions and improve the
predictability of submission outcomes.
The CMS HTI-1 rule has been interpreted publicly by CMS to both apply and not apply to
products regulated as medical devices if it is training algorithms on data from Electronic
Health Records. This interpretation extends to medical devices already reviewed and
cleared/approved by the FDA via the 510(k) or PMA process, which is duplicative,
burdensome and redundant. Modification of this rule should be prioritized to avoid needless
duplicative and burdensome review for products already determined to be safe and effective
by the FDA. Further clarification on the scope of the rule would ensure that medical device
manufacturers understand all the requirements that apply to their AI-driven medical devices
not subject to FDA review.
Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) AI requirements need more clarity.
We would also believe there should be a playing field level so that software functions that
meet the definition of a medical device are regulated the same way regardless of whether
they are deployed in a mobile application, an electronic health record (EHR), or traditional
medical device.
Additional guidance on applying HIPAA, de-identification standards, and secondary use
rules to AI training, validation, and model improvement would be beneficial. We encourage
reducing uncertainty around permissible uses of clinical data for model improvement
including the use of real-world data to support postmarket learning and iterative
improvement. Furthermore, we recommend that postmarket monitoring be outcome-focused
and risk-based rather than blanket requirements that could slow clinical adoption or
disproportionately burden high-performing, low-risk technologies.
3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel legal
and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures
(e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
Legal and implementation issues include liability allocation among developers, deployers,
and clinicians, indemnification and contractual risk-shifting, and transparency expectations
for AI-assisted decisions.

For AI tools used in clinical settings that are not regulated as medical devices, the biggest
issue is uncertainty about responsibility. When these tools influence documentation,
workflow, triage, or other aspects of care, it is not always clear who is accountable if
something goes wrong or how existing privacy and compliance rules apply. That ambiguity
can slow adoption, even when the technology is beneficial. HHS does not need to create new
liability structures, but clearer guidance on how existing rules apply would reduce
uncertainty and support responsible use.
An additional emerging issue involves the distinction between algorithm developers and
model deployers. In some cases, companies provide foundational algorithms or configurable
AI systems, while health systems or other end users perform the training and generate site-
specific models. Current guidance often focuses on the evaluation and oversight of deployed
models, leaving ambiguity regarding:
• The liability boundary between the underlying algorithm and the trained model
• The level of testing and validation expected at the algorithm stage versus the trained-
model stage
• The allocation of responsibility for performance, bias mitigation, and post-
deployment monitoring
This distributed development paradigm challenges traditional governance frameworks that
assume a single entity controls design, training, and deployment.
HHS could play a valuable role by clarifying shared-responsibility expectations across the AI
lifecycle and providing guidance on proportional testing and validation requirements at each
stage. Such clarity would reduce contractual uncertainty, promote innovation, and ensure
accountability without discouraging flexible AI development models.
4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and post-
deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered evaluation
methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so, which
mechanisms would be most impactful (e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or
prize competitions)?
Any evaluation methods promoted by HHS should align with existing regulatory and quality
systems and avoid duplicative third-party validation, redundant documentation, or
unnecessary disclosure of confidential commercial information or sensitive health data.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, industry-
driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
All medical devices, including AI-driven medical devices, are already highly regulated across
the entire product lifecycle, including both the pre- and post-market timeframes. Devices are
subject to robust post-market requirements that ensure their ongoing safety and
effectiveness. When considering additional post-market requirements for emerging AI
technologies, it is important that any new measures are tailored to specific device risks and
characteristics, rather than applied as broad, blanket requirements to all AI-enabled devices.
It is also important to recognize that AI-enabled devices are not uniform in function or risk.
Governance frameworks, including hospital-based AI review committees, should apply

proportionate, risk-based oversight that reflects the device’s intended use, level of autonomy,
and degree of clinical oversight, rather than treating all AI technologies equivalently.
Additionally, third-party certification should be avoided, as it will lead to redundant
validation activities and documentation and may lead to disclosure of commercial
confidential information and inappropriate release of private healthcare data.
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the
greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help
reduce costs?
AI has met or exceeded expectations as it relates to imaging and diagnostics, laboratory
workflow optimization, and supply chain visibility.
AI has fallen short where tools were deployed without sufficient workflow integration, where
performance was evaluated on retrospective datasets that did not reflect real-world
variability, or where limited interoperability constrained scalability. Unrealistic expectations
regarding full automation, rather than augmentation of clinical decision-making, have also
contributed to underperformance in some settings.
The ability to more fully evaluate and scale AI tools across care settings is directly influenced
by access to high-quality, interoperable data. Broader use of secure, standardized, and
privacy-protective data sharing agreements would enable more robust validation across
diverse populations and clinical environments. Such access would support stronger evidence
generation, improve generalizability, and allow clearer demonstration of clinical and
economic value.
7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations have
the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary
administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
Chief information security officers, chief technology officers, chief innovation officers,
quality assurance, information technology, hospital information technology, procurement,
revenue cycle management, engineering, and laboratory directors have the most influence on
the adoption of AI for clinical care. Hurdles include supply chain, data sharing agreements,
security, transparency, risk management, change management, validation, contract/legal
review, reimbursement, and procurement.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and
accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data types, data
standards, and benchmarking tools.
Greater interoperability between laboratory systems, diagnostic platforms, and downstream
clinical systems (including the imaging-to-EHR interoperability gap) would unlock
substantial AI value. Standards like FHIR, DICOM and DICOM-SR can help, but they have
not been adopted consistently. Technical and performance standards must be identified.
Allowing cross-system integration will enable imaging-based AI tools to avoid being siloed
within radiology workflows limiting downstream clinical adoption, multidisciplinary

decision-making, and development of value-based reimbursement models. Data cleanliness
and privacy should be prioritized. Finally, benchmarking by key opinion leaders would be
useful, as well as background benchmarking, though issues exist with the need to upskill to
use AI to detect issues and deskill prevention of domain knowledge.
9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the
adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers
have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
Patients and caregivers consistently express interest in AI tools that meaningfully improve
access, timeliness, and quality of care. Desired outcomes include faster and more preventive
diagnostics, better outcomes, reduced delays in testing and treatment, improved coordination
and bedside decision support, and better prediction of system downtime or care disruptions
that may affect access. AI that reduces administrative burden and enables clinicians to spend
more time with patients is also viewed positively.
At the same time, patients and caregivers raise important concerns related to privacy, data
security, bias, overreliance on automated systems, and the potential for malicious cyber
activity. These concerns are appropriate and should continue to be addressed transparently.
Importantly, many of these risks are already actively managed within existing regulatory and
quality frameworks. AI-enabled medical devices are subject to established cybersecurity
requirements, including secure design controls, vulnerability management, and post-market
monitoring to guard against malicious activity. Similarly, the risk of bias, while not unique to
AI, is addressed through data representativeness assessments, performance testing across
relevant populations, and risk mitigation strategies during development. For regulated
products, these efforts are evaluated as part of premarket review and ongoing quality system
oversight.
For non-device AI deployed in clinical environments, organizations similarly integrate
privacy, security, and performance monitoring controls within existing governance and
compliance programs. Continued transparency, lifecycle monitoring, and human oversight
remain essential to maintaining public trust.
By strengthening interoperability, reinforcing cybersecurity standards, and promoting
responsible development practices, AI can address patient priorities while operating within
established safeguards designed to protect safety, equity, and confidentiality.
10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the adoption of
AI as part of clinical care?
Priorities include trustworthy AI, bias mitigation across populations, human-AI collaboration
models, real-world effectiveness and cost-benefit analysis, implementation science,
evaluation frameworks, public private partnerships and literature gaps.
Several of these areas (particularly evaluation frameworks, robustness testing methods, and
transparency standards) are well suited for development through international consensus
standards bodies and global harmonization efforts. Leveraging international standards

promotes consistency, reduces duplicative regulatory burden, and supports global
interoperability of AI-enabled healthcare technologies.
Accordingly, HHS research investments should complement, not replace, international
standards development. The Department can play a valuable role by funding applied
research, supporting public-private partnerships, and participating in global harmonization
initiatives to ensure that U.S. regulatory and policy approaches remain aligned with
internationally recognized best practices.
