Dear Assistant Secretary Keane,
The National Association of Chain Drug Stores (NACDS) appreciates the opportunity to respond to the Request
for Information (RFI) on accelerating the adoption and responsible use of artificial intelligence (AI) in clinical
care. We applaud your commitment to advancing the appropriate use of AI to improve healthcare for the
American people and appreciate your consideration of our comments. NACDS represents chain pharmacies,
including traditional drug stores, supermarkets, and mass merchants with pharmacies. Nearly 90% of
Americans live within 5 miles of a community pharmacy1 and 85% of adults in the U.S. say pharmacists are
easy to access, ranking them the most accessible healthcare destination.2 Pharmacies are a trusted,
nationwide access point for medications, immunizations, preventive care, patient education and counseling,
and a growing range of health and wellness services that improve health outcomes.
To accelerate AI adoption in clinical care, NACDS recommends a pro-innovation, risk proportionate national
framework that maintains patient safety, privacy, transparency, equity, and public trust. In implementing AI
policy, HHS should rely on and reinforce existing guardrails, avoid duplicative regulation, align reimbursement
with value, and ensure harmonization across federal agencies and states. Furthermore, payment reform is
foundational. AI should support clinician-rendered care, and clinicians must be reimbursed for that care.

2. Align Payment Policy with AI-Supported Clinical Value & Recognize Pharmacists as Providers in
Medicare Part B
Sustainable adoption of AI in clinical care depends on reimbursement reform. Pharmacists must be
recognized as providers under Medicare Part B so that pharmacist-rendered clinical services, including
those supported and scaled by AI, are eligible for reimbursement. Without provider recognition by
CMS, payment pathways remain limited and investment in AI-enabled clinical services is constrained
for pharmacies.
Reimbursement methodologies must also account for the cost of acquiring, implementing,
maintaining, and monitoring AI tools that support clinical care delivery. Just as payment rates reflect
the resources required to furnish services, reimbursement for AI-supported services should
incorporate the reasonable and necessary costs of technology infrastructure, system integration,
validation, cybersecurity safeguards, staff training, and ongoing performance monitoring. Without
recognition of these costs in rate-setting methodologies, providers may be financially disincentivized
from adopting innovative, AI-driven tools that improve quality, safety, and efficiency.
3. Strengthen Interoperability to Enable Authorized, Workflow-Integrated Data Access
Effective clinical AI tools require reliable, structured access to electronic health information within
workflow. HHS should strengthen interoperability to ensure that community pharmacies can obtain
and transmit clinically relevant data including screening and lab results , discharge summaries,
diagnoses, medication changes, care plans, and other key information in real time. Meaningful
interoperability will widen market opportunities, promote competition among developers, and enable
safe, effective AI integration.
4. Provide Clear Governance Guardrails for Non-Device AI
HHS should issue risk-tiered guidance clarifying documentation standards, liability allocation, oversight
expectations, privacy and data governance responsibilities, and audit treatment for AI tools that
support, but do not replace, clinical judgment. Safe harbors for good-faith adoption of AI tools would
reduce uncertainty and encourage responsible innovation while preserving accountability and public
trust.
5. Invest in Applied Research and Real-World Demonstrations
HHS should prioritize public-private partnerships and demonstration projects that validate AI-
supported clinical tools in real-world pharmacy settings.
Introductory Perspectives: A National Framework, Payment Alignment, and R&D Investment
1. The Impact of Current HHS Regulations on AI Adoption
HHS currently regulates many aspects of clinical AI use through existing frameworks, including FDA
oversight for software as a medical device where applicable; CMS program integrity and

Page 3 of 8
documentation rules; HIPAA privacy and security regulations; Section 1557 nondiscrimination
requirements; and ASTP/ONC transparency and risk management requirements for predictive decision
support in certified health IT.
These guardrails provide meaningful patient protections and serve as a strong foundation for
responsible AI deployment. However, fragmentation and uncertainty across regulatory regimes can
slow AI adoption, particularly for non-device AI tools that support clinical documentation, medication
adherence outreach, and workflow prioritization. When expectations for oversight, documentation,
and audit treatment are unclear or inconsistently interpreted, providers are hesitant to deploy AI tools
even when those tools enhance quality and efficiency.
NACDS recommends that HHS rely on existing oversight structures rather than layering duplicative AI-
specific policies. Interagency coordination across FDA, CMS, OCR, and ASTP/ONC is essential to avoid
conflicting expectations and duplicative compliance burdens. Additionally, HHS should establish a
harmonized national framework that minimizes inconsistent state-level AI mandates. In particular, the
proliferation of overlapping state AI laws creates uncertainty and inefficiency for healthcare providers
operating across multiple jurisdictions. A coordinated federal framework that supersedes state laws
would reduce friction and enable responsible scaling of AI tools.
2. Payment Policy Changes to Incentivize High-Value AI and Promote Competition
Payment policy is a primary determinant of AI adoption. Even when AI tools improve quality, efficiency,
and access, providers frequently lack sustainable reimbursement pathways. In pharmacy settings, this
challenge is amplified by the lack of pharmacist recognition as healthcare providers in Medicare Part B,
limiting billing pathways for pharmacist-led clinical services, and uncertainty regarding documentation
standards when AI supports clinical workflows.
First, HHS should recognize and reimburse pharmacist-rendered services that AI helps scale. AI should
support pharmacist-led screening programs, adherence interventions, chronic care management,
preventive services, and other care. However, these services must be reimbursable to achieve
widespread adoption. Importantly, reimbursement must reflect not only the professional service
furnished but also the technology investments required to deliver AI-supported care. AI tools involve
upfront capital expenditures, integration costs, cybersecurity protections, ongoing licensing fees, and
continuous validation and monitoring. Payment policy should therefore incorporate technology
enablement costs into rate-setting methodologies and value-based payment calculations. Without
payment reform, AI will remain limited to administrative efficiencies rather than enabling scalable
clinical transformation.
Second, CMS should clarify billing and audit expectations for AI-assisted documentation. When a
licensed clinician reviews, edits as needed, and attests to documentation supported by AI, that
documentation should be treated consistently with traditional records. Clear guidance would reduce
audit uncertainty and encourage adoption of AI tools that reduce clinician burden.
Third, value-based payment models should reward prevention, adherence, and early intervention.
Pharmacists play a critical role in delivering these services through medication therapy management,
chronic disease monitoring, immunizations, test-and-treat protocols, targeted medication adherence

communities. AI tools that enhance pharmacists’ ability to identify high-risk patients, close therapy

3. HHS Investment in Research, Development, and Long-Term Market Creation
HHS can catalyze durable, high-value AI markets by investing in public-private partnerships that
validate AI-enabled clinical tools in real-world pharmacy settings. Demonstration projects in
community pharmacies can generate real-world evidence regarding safety, effectiveness, workflow
integration, and total cost of care.
Responses to Specific RFI Questions
RFI Question 1. Biggest Barriers to Private Sector Innovation in AI For Health Care and its
Adoption/Use in Clinical Care
The biggest barriers to private sector innovation and adoption are misaligned incentives, regulatory
uncertainty, and data access friction, particularly for non-device AI that supports workflow
optimization and documentation.
Payment misalignment remains the most significant barrier. Many high-value AI-enabled interventions,
including screening and referral workflows, adherence targeting, and chronic care support do not have
dependable reimbursement pathways. In pharmacy settings, the lack of consistent payment for
pharmacist-rendered clinical services, including in Medicare Part B, suppresses investment in AI tools
designed to scale those services, and further constrains innovation. Pharmacists’ lack of recognition as
providers under Medicare Part B restricts reimbursement eligibility and weakens the market signals
necessary to drive AI deployment in pharmacy-based care models.
Fragmented interoperability also limits AI effectiveness. AI systems depend on timely, structured
access to relevant clinical data, such as laboratory results, discharge summaries, updated diagnoses,
and medication changes. Without reliable real-time data access, AI tools cannot safely or effectively
support clinical decision-making.
Finally, fragmented governance frameworks and overlapping federal and state requirements create
compliance complexity that slows innovation, particularly for organizations operating across multiple
jurisdictions.

RFI Question 2. Regulatory, Payment Policy, or Programmatic Design Changes HHS Should Prioritize
HHS should prioritize payment and program design reforms that make high-value AI-supported clinical
services financially viable, paired with clearer guardrails for documentation and audit treatment and
strengthened interoperability.
A. Payment and Program Design Priorities (CMS)
HHS should create and expand pathways that reimburse pharmacist-rendered clinical services
supported by AI. Without payment reform, AI will remain confined to “back office” functions such as
Interactive Voice Response (IVR) optimization, intake triage, and billing support. Existing CMS
authorities, including demonstration programs and value-based models, should be used to support
pharmacist-led, AI-supported clinical services, such as screening and preventive care, medication
adherence interventions, transitions-of-care services, and chronic disease management.
CMS should also clarify billing and documentation expectations when AI supports clinical
documentation. If a licensed clinician, including a pharmacist, reviews and attests to the
documentation record, AI assistance should not create noncompliance or heightened audit risk.
B. Interoperability and Information Access Priorities (ASTP/ONC)
HHS and ASTP/ONC should strengthen meaningful access to electronic health information (EHI) for all
care settings, including community pharmacies. Pharmacies must be able to access clinically relevant
EHI in structured formats integrated into workflow, not merely through document-based exchange.
Reliable interoperability will expand market opportunity, improve competition among AI developers,
and support safe, scalable adoption. NACDS looks forward to working with HHS and ASTP/ONC to
strengthen pharmacy interoperability and ensure that community pharmacies are fully integrated
participants in national health information exchange infrastructure. As recognized in ONC’s 2023
Pharmacy Interoperability and Emerging Therapeutics Task Force Report, pharmacies must be fully
integrated into nationwide health information exchange infrastructure to support clinical services
through secure, structured data exchange.3
RFI Question 3. Novel Legal/Implementation Issues for Non-Medical Device AI and HHS’ Role
Non-device AI tools raise legal and governance issues not fully addressed by traditional medical device
frameworks.
Liability allocation is a central concern impeding adoption of clinical AI tools. When AI suggests
prioritization or drafts documentation, responsibility for potential errors must be clearly delineated
among clinicians, provider organizations, and AI vendors. Clear expectations are needed regarding
standards of clinician review, documentation, and appropriate logging practices.
Privacy, security, and data governance considerations are also significant. Handling electronic health
information at scale for AI requires clear expectations for access controls, retention policies, model

HHS should publish risk-tiered guardrails for non-device AI, clarify documentation and oversight

RFI Question 7. Key Decision Makers/Governing Bodies and Primary Administrative Hurdles
Key Decision Makers: Adoption of AI in clinical care settings, including community pharmacy, is rarely
driven by a single individual within an organization. Instead, it reflects coordinated decision-making
across multiple leadership domains, each of which evaluates risk, value, and operational feasibility
through a distinct lens.
Clinical leadership, including pharmacy leadership and quality committees, plays a central role in
determining whether an AI tool aligns with patient safety standards, clinical workflows, and quality
improvement goals. These leaders assess whether the technology meaningfully enhances care, reduces
errors, improves health outcomes, or supports access. Clinical leaders are often highly influential
voices in determining whether AI is perceived as a tool that augments professional judgment or
introduces unacceptable risk.
Compliance, legal, and risk management teams also exercise substantial influence. These stakeholders
evaluate regulatory exposure, documentation standards, audit risk, liability allocation, and consistency
with federal and state requirements. If expectations regarding oversight, documentation, or audit
treatment are unclear or overly complex, compliance leadership may appropriately take a cautious
posture that slows adoption.
Information technology, security, and data governance leadership are equally critical decisionmakers.
AI tools require secure integration with existing pharmacy management systems, access to electronic
health information, and adherence to cybersecurity standards. IT leaders must assess data
architecture, Application Programming Interface (API) capabilities, access controls, model monitoring
requirements, and long-term system maintenance obligations. Concerns regarding data security,
privacy compliance, and operational resilience can significantly affect implementation timelines.
Finance and reimbursement leadership evaluate the return on investment, sustainability, and payment
viability of AI-supported services. Even when clinical and operational leaders support adoption,
uncertainty regarding reimbursement pathways, billing compliance, and audit posture may constrain
investment decisions. In pharmacy practice, the absence of consistent payment mechanisms for
pharmacist-rendered clinical services further limits financial justification for deploying AI tools
designed to scale those services.

Primary Administrative Hurdles: Vendor due diligence is among the most significant barriers.
Healthcare organizations must evaluate cybersecurity safeguards, HIPAA compliance, data
minimization practices, bias testing methodologies, model validation processes, and ongoing
monitoring plans. This process is resource-intensive and often requires multidisciplinary review.
Contracting and indemnification complexity further complicates adoption. AI vendor agreements
frequently involve negotiations over liability allocation, performance guarantees, data ownership,
audit rights, and incident response responsibilities. Providers are often reluctant to accept
disproportionate liability exposure for tools they do not control, particularly where regulatory
standards are still evolving.
Integration burden with existing systems presents another substantial challenge. AI tools must function
within high-volume, workflow-driven environments such as community pharmacies. Poorly integrated
systems can increase cognitive load, create alert fatigue, or require duplicative documentation.
Seamless interoperability with pharmacy management systems and external electronic health record
platforms is essential but not always readily achievable.
Finally, unclear reimbursement and audit posture remains a critical barrier. Organizations need
confidence that AI-assisted documentation and clinical decision support will be treated consistently
under program integrity reviews. Ambiguity in billing standards or audit expectations creates risk
aversion and delays adoption, even when the technology demonstrates clinical value.
Reducing these administrative hurdles through clearer guidance, aligned payment policy, standardized
evaluation frameworks, and predictable audit treatment would significantly accelerate responsible AI
adoption in clinical care settings.
RFI Question 8. Where Enhanced Interoperability Would Widen Market Opportunities and
Accelerate AI Development
Interoperability improvements that matter most for pharmacy-enabled AI include structured access to
relevant clinical information, such as medication history, laboratory data, diagnoses, discharge
summaries, care plans, and referral documentation within workflow.
If pharmacies can reliably access electronic health information in usable formats integrated into daily
workflow, the AI market expands dramatically. More developers can compete, more clinical use cases
become viable, and patient outcomes improve through safer, more efficient, and better coordinated
care.
Conclusion
Pharmacies are uniquely positioned to expand access to a wide variety of healthcare services through
responsibly deployed clinical AI tools. However, meaningful and scalable adoption requires payment reform,
documentation clarity, strengthened interoperability, and risk-proportionate governance guardrails. NACDS
stands ready to collaborate with HHS and across agencies to ensure that AI enhances clinical care in ways that
are safe, responsible, competitive, and scalable nationwide. NACDS is eager to collaborate to improve
pharmacy data interoperability and ensure community pharmacies are completely included in the nation's
