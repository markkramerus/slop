Question 1: Barriers to Innovation
What are biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI adoption and use in clinical care?
At IQVIA, we have seen that private‑sector innovation in clinical‑care AI is constrained by a combination of
structural, regulatory, economic, and trust‑related barriers that limit the development, validation, and scalable
deployment of AI tools across health‑care settings. We commend ASTP/ONC in the release of the HTI rule
series and for establishing important transparency and risk‑management expectations within certified health
IT. We encourage ASTP/ONC to continue coordinating towards a cross‑HHS vision for AI lifecycle
management that spans regulation, reimbursement, and research. Without such a vision, and incorporation
of the life-cycle management framework, as appropriate, into HHS policies, we would expect fragmentation
and continued barriers to AI development and use in healthcare.
Collectively, these barriers cluster into four areas: (1) infrastructure and data constraints, including EHR
integration burden, vendor‑specific deployment pathways, limited interoperability, fragmented and locally
inconsistent data, and limited access to diverse real‑world care settings for validation; (2) regulatory and
governance fragmentation, including pathways and oversight approaches not well‑suited to adaptive or
generative AI, inconsistent security and privacy requirements across institutions, and continued uncertainty
about accountability when AI contributes to clinical decision‑making; (3) market and economic uncertainty,
particularly inconsistent reimbursement and unclear return on investment that keeps many AI tools in pilot
mode and limits sustained investment in product development and operational integration; and (4) evidence
and trust gaps, driven by inconsistent expectations for monitoring, documentation, version control,
auditability, and real‑world performance evidence, coupled with limited infrastructure to generate these
artifacts at scale. These challenges reinforce one another and are exacerbated when AI lifecycle
expectations are not harmonized across regulation, reimbursement, and research. Addressing these
barriers requires clear, harmonized lifecycle management expectations and reduced friction across existing
HHS authorities.
Question 2. Regulatory, Payment, and Programmatic Design Changes HHS to Prioritize
What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or
programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care? Please
provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
IQVIA has been working to advance the effective use of AI in clinical care by modernizing governance
frameworks, setting clear operational expectations, and creating strong economic incentives for our
customers. Policies built for static software do not reflect the behavior, risk profile, or evidence needs of
adaptive, generative, and agentic AI systems and should not be used for healthcare AI. Rather, instituting
best practice governance and lifecycle management increases predictability for developers and healthcare
organizations while bolstering patient safety. We recommend future ASTP/ONC and HHS policies establish
transparent, standards‑based expectations and enable coordination across HHS, in lieu of prescriptive,
model‑specific regulatory requirements. ASTP/ONC’s role would be to convene stakeholders, signal federal
expectations, and promote alignment across agencies under existing authorities related to certification,
interoperability, transparency, and programmatic incentives to streamline oversight while enabling safe
innovation at scale. For example:
▪ Modernize and Harmonize Regulatory Oversight for Dynamic AI. Many current oversight
pathways were built for static software and do not align with adaptive, generative, or agentic AI
systems. FDA’s “special controls” and pre‑market evidence expectations, such as multi‑reader,
multi‑case (MRMC) studies, introduce burdens that slow iteration and limit developers’ ability to
evaluate AI in its intended context. A shift toward evidence‑based, lifecycle‑oriented oversight,
balancing pre‑market expectations with structured post‑deployment monitoring and real‑world
evidence, would better reflect how AI interacts with evolving clinical workflows and data. This

approach emphasizes tailoring controls to demonstrated risk and performance, rather than default
reliance on rigid or static special controls that may unintentionally impede innovation.
In addition, clearer expectations for evaluating generative and agentic behaviors are needed to
support safe deployment. Aligning oversight with established frameworks, such as the NIST RMF,
ISO/IEC 42001, and CTA‑2135, would reduce fragmentation and create more predictable,
standards‑based expectations across FDA and HHS more broadly. ASTP/ONC is well positioned
within its existing certification, interoperability, and transparency authorities to convene stakeholders,
signal shared expectations, and promote alignment across FDA, CMS, and other HHS components
helping ensure that expectations for transparency, monitoring, and change management are
consistent across the AI lifecycle.
▪ Prioritize Clinical Problem‑Solving Over Regulatory Process. While FDA clears many
AI‑enabled devices each year, clearance often does not translate into adoption or reimbursement by
CMS. Market authorization alone is insufficient to ensure clinical uptake, particularly for AI
functionality. Manufacturers typically design AI devices based on requirements gathered from
clinicians, including physicians and nurses. However, FDA review processes frequently override
these clinical requirements, effectively forcing manufacturers to choose between market clearance
and clinician‑driven design. This one‑size‑fits‑all approach limits functionality and contributes to an
innovation gap, as similar devices often achieve market entry in the EU and other markets without
significant safety concerns. These challenges persist even for devices designated under the FDA
Breakthrough Devices Program. In many cases, changes required during FDA review alter the
device’s intended use, ultimately disqualifying it from CMS breakthrough reimbursement pathways
and undermining the purpose of the designation. HHS can play a role in aligning NIH, FDA, and
CMS incentives and requirements. Improved oversight and streamlined interagency processes would
help ensure that innovative AI devices that address real clinical needs are not stalled between
regulatory clearance and reimbursement, and that clinically valuable technologies reach patients.
ASTP/ONC‑led coordination across HHS can help ensure that transparency, interoperability, and
lifecycle governance expectations established at clearance do not become barriers to downstream
reimbursement and clinical adoption.
▪ Strengthen Governance Through Transparency, Privacy Clarity, and Safe‑Harbor
Approaches. Clear governance expectations are essential for consistent and trustworthy AI
deployment. For example:
• Building on ASTP/ONC’s existing transparency framework under 45 CFR Part 170, similar
transparency expectations for non‑device AI could be advanced through voluntary certification
pathways, federal procurement standards, and other non‑regulatory mechanisms to ensure
clinicians and implementers have access to provenance, performance summaries, version
history, and change documentation.
• Clearer expectations around continuous risk assessment, including drift detection, monitoring
intervals, update documentation, and performance reassessment as models operate in
real‑world conditions. For organizations that maintain robust AI management systems aligned
with NIST or ISO standards, HHS could explore shared‑responsibility safe‑harbor models that
reduce uncertainty and support more predictable compliance expectations when strong
governance and auditability controls are demonstrably in place.
Such approaches align with ASTP/ONC’s emphasis on voluntary, standards‑based transparency and
certification‑driven trust.
▪ Modernize Payment Policy to Incentivize Preventive, AI-Enabled Care. Many high‑value clinical
AI tools support early identification of rising‑risk patients or emerging care gaps, yet fee‑for‑service
structures provide limited incentives for proactive preventative care. More predictable pathways for

Question 4. Evaluation
What are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and post-deployment) for clinical care?
Should HHS further support these (evaluation) processes? If so, how?
IQVIA’s operational models strengthen AI evaluation by expanding support for real‑world testing, reduce
fragmentation across partnership expectations (harmonization and use of consensus‑based standards and
frameworks), and lowering operational barriers for our healthcare organizations partners. These IQVIA
operational models may align directly with ASTP/ONC’s priorities to enable trustworthy AI adoption through
lifecycle governance, interoperability, transparency, and cross‑HHS coordination rather than model‑level
regulation. In this light, we suggest the following concepts to HHS:
Expand funding for real-world and workflow-embedded evaluation. Support for prospective pilots,
EHR-integrated testing, and post-deployment monitoring infrastructure would help ensure evaluations reflect
the complexity of real clinical environments and the constraints clinicians face. Reimbursement systems
should provide incentive payments for entities working to embed AI and AI life-cycle management.
Standardize evaluation frameworks across HHS divisions. Common expectations for lifecycle
documentation, real-world evidence, monitoring frequency, and transparency would improve consistency and
allow developers and healthcare organizations to design more reliable evaluation strategies. Embed
requirements for best practice life-cycle management and governance into standards updates and
certification programs connected to CMS reimbursement policies; FDA market approval guidance; and
ASTP/ONC EHR certification.
Incentivize transparent reporting. HHS can encourage programs that support clear reporting of methods,
datasets, and performance results, including subgroup and drift analyses, so that buyers and clinicians can
meaningfully compare solutions by providing incentive payments for the use of such tools.
Use Other Transaction Authority (OTA) to accelerate evaluation through public-private partnership.
OTA can support collaboration with industry on shared test beds, demonstration projects, and iterative
evaluation in real‑world settings, enabling faster timelines, flexible IP arrangements, and adaptive evaluation
designs that reduce participation risk while preserving rigor. OTA’s flexibility can also de-risk participation by
enabling alternative IP arrangements, faster timelines, and more adaptive evaluation designs than traditional
procurement allows.
Together, these actions would help build a more consistent, clinically grounded evaluation ecosystem, one
that enables high-quality AI tools to demonstrate safety and effectiveness while supporting responsible
adoption across diverse care settings.

How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, industry-driven
testing, and credentialing)?

HHS can strengthen private‑sector accreditation, certification, and industry‑driven testing by setting clearer
expectations for evaluation, supporting shared testing infrastructure, and reducing barriers that impede the
deployment of trustworthy AI tools. As noted earlier, use of consensus-based standards in all regulations that
incent or regulate health care use of data and AI tools will begin to create a cohesive and clear path for
governance and life cycle management.
Establish National, Risk‑Stratified Validation Expectations. HHS can help coordinate private‑sector
assurance efforts by outlining risk‑stratified validation expectations that specify the types of evidence AI
developers should produce, by setting standards that must be met IF AI is to be reimbursed or deployed for
operations via CMS provider certification. This includes technical validation (accuracy, robustness,
calibration, fairness, and known failure modes), clinically meaningful evaluation, and post‑deployment
monitoring requirements such as drift detection, change control, and update documentation.
Align Certification Pathways with Established Standards. HHS can support private certification bodies by
endorsing a low‑overhead certification pathway grounded in best practices for data validation,
interoperability, workflow integration, testing, and bias monitoring. Aligning this pathway established
standards would provide a common foundation for assurance activities and simplify how developers and
HCOs demonstrate conformity to best practices. Setting use of consensus-based frameworks or standards at
the bar for reimbursement and provider certification will bring rigor to the use of AI in healthcare.
Incentivize Adoption of Certified, Standards‑Aligned Tools. HHS can increase the impact of
private‑sector certification by offering incentives for HCOs to adopt interoperable, standards‑aligned AI tools.
Targeted grants, reimbursement advantages, or preferential access to federal pilot programs would
encourage organizations to adopt tools that meet established certification criteria.
Support Neutral, Independent Evaluation Environments. HHS can enhance private‑sector evaluation
capacity by supporting neutral, independent testing environments where auditors can conduct standardized
assessments of robustness, bias, and documentation quality. Shared evaluation sandboxes and
procurement‑ready conformance checklists would reduce duplicative testing and strengthen the reliability of
private accreditation and certification efforts.
Taken together, these actions would help harmonize evaluation practices, lower adoption barriers for health
systems, and enable private‑sector innovation to progress within a consistent, standards‑aligned framework.
Question 7: HCO Decision makers
Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within healthcare organizations (HCOs) have
the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary administrative
hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
Roles and Decisionmakers Influencing AI Adoption in Clinical Care. AI adoption in clinical care depends
on a mix of clinical, operational, and governance authorities. Key decisionmakers typically include Chief
Medical, Nursing, Information, and Informatics Officers; CIOs and CTOs; and leaders overseeing Quality,
Patient Safety, Compliance, Privacy, and Security. Many health systems also maintain multidisciplinary AI or
clinical‑informatics review boards responsible for evaluating evidence, workflow fit, and ongoing monitoring.
Innovation, informatics, and implementation teams further assess technical feasibility and determine whether
tools can be configured within vendor‑constrained EHR environments.
While governance groups shape formal approval pathways, clinician endorsement often determines whether
AI becomes embedded in routine care. Sustained use depends on whether tools reduce cognitive burden, fit
naturally within existing workflows, and provide value at the point of care.

Patients also influence organizational decisions, especially for AI systems supporting shared
decision‑making, providing personalized risk information, or using patient‑generated data. In many
organizations, patient advocates serve on advisory councils or governance committees, ensuring that patient
priorities, such as usability, transparency, accessibility, and data stewardship, are represented in
deliberations. As AI capabilities expand (e.g., personalized risk estimation, digital navigation support),
patient‑experience perspectives increasingly shape whether tools are deemed appropriate for deployment.
Primary Administrative Hurdles. Administrative barriers frequently stem from fragmented decision‑making
processes, limited technical capacity, and workflow pressures. IT and clinical‑informatics teams are often
stretched by core operational priorities, such as EHR upgrades, security requirements, and regulatory
compliance, leaving limited bandwidth to support integration, monitoring, or iterative refinement of AI tools.
Vendor‑specific integration pathways, restrictions on customization, and the need to maintain certification
further complicate deployment and increase the time and cost required to bring tools online.
Governance‑related challenges also contribute to slow adoption. Many organizations lack clear procedures
for AI‑specific data approvals, lifecycle oversight, version control, and post‑deployment monitoring.
Risk‑averse procurement processes and unclear accountability for ongoing model performance add to
delays, even in cases where the clinical value is well demonstrated.
For tools involving patient‑generated data or personalized insights, administrative teams must update
consent workflows, strengthen transparency processes, and ensure adequate privacy safeguards. These
adjustments require coordination across clinical, legal, IT, and patient‑experience teams, adding another
layer of complexity.
These decision‑making dynamics and administrative hurdles align with ASTP/ONC’s focus on transparency,
interoperability, lifecycle governance, and human‑centered integration as the primary levers to reduce friction
for adoption and support consistent, scalable use of AI within healthcare settings.
