Introduction
The enhancement of health and the potential for individuals to be the executive of their health
through artificial intelligence is no longer a prospective claim; it is a demonstrable today, in part.
The primary impediment to widespread adoption is not a deficit in model capabilities, but rather
a lack of coordinated federal direction on AI infrastructure investments for health should be
allocated.
The integration of AI within healthcare delivery remains significantly hindered by individuals not
having a centralized easily accessible data sources for consumers of care. In the absence of
cohesive federal intervention across regulatory, financial, and research domains, AI deployment
will remain inconsistent and limited to constrained, low-risk applications.
The Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) possesses both the requisite authority
and the operational mechanisms to effectively dismantle these structural impediments while
creating structure to provide adequate protections for patient privacy. The subsequent
recommendations propose concrete, actionable policy levers for consideration.
Structural Barriers to Adoption: Fragmented Longitudinal Data Environments
Effective AI necessitates structured, machine-readable, longitudinal data spanning diverse care
settings. Despite documented progress under 45 CFR Part 170 (Health IT Certification) and 45
CFR Part 171 (Information Blocking), operational implementation remains highly variable.
Access to quality bulk data is inconsistent, semantic normalization is unevenly applied, and
standardized audit capabilities across systems are lacking.
Organizations endeavoring to implement cross-institutional AI solutions are compelled to
navigate redundant governance structures, inconsistent API implementations, and variable
access controls. This fragmentation substantially decelerates innovation and elevates
operational costs.
AI scalability is contingent upon interoperability being operationalized, enforceable, and subject
to clear measurement.
Regulatory Ambiguity for Non-Device AI. A substantial class of AI tools—including,
but not limited to, risk stratification engines, clinical documentation augmentation,
workflow optimization platforms, and surveillance triggers—does not satisfy the
established criteria for medical device classification, yet directly impacts the delivery of
care.

Healthcare systems currently lack definitive federal guidance pertaining to audit
requirements, drift monitoring protocols, documentation expectations, and reproducibility
standards for these particular tools. This absence of predictable regulatory guardrails
prolongs legal review processes and inhibits procurement timelines.
Reimbursement Misalignment. Existing fee-for-service payment models do not
systematically incentivize burden reduction, efficiency gains in productivity, or
preventative interventions. Furthermore, even within value-based and risk-bearing
payment arrangements, the infrastructure investments essential for supporting AI—such
as a longitudinal health record accessible by patients, data quality measurement
systems, federated query capabilities, and comprehensive monitoring systems—lack
explicit recognition.
Liability and Indemnification Uncertainty. Healthcare systems exhibit pervasive
caution in deploying AI owing to an unclear demarcation of responsibility between the
technology developers and the organizations implementing the solutions. Negotiations
concerning indemnification frequently extend procurement cycles beyond a twelve-
month horizon.

II. Reimbursement Modernization
Existing fee-for-service payment models do not systematically incentivize burden reduction,
efficiency gains in productivity, or preventative interventions. Furthermore, even within value-
based and risk-bearing payment arrangements, the infrastructure investments essential for
supporting AI—such as data quality measurement systems, federated query capabilities, and
comprehensive monitoring systems—lack explicit financial recognition.
The acceleration of AI adoption is directly contingent upon payment models that recognize both
infrastructure investment and longitudinal value creation.
A. Incorporate AI Infrastructure into Value-Based Arrangements. Under 42 CFR Part
425 (Medicare Shared Savings Program) and 42 CFR Part 422 (Medicare Advantage), the
Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) should permit the inclusion of AI-enabling
infrastructure investments—including data normalization efforts, quality measurement cycles,
and monitoring systems—within shared savings calculations.

Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation (CMMI) demonstrations authorized under
42 U.S.C. § 1315a should explicitly pilot and test AI-enabled care models that holistically
integrate analytics, workflow redesign, and governance oversight.
Pilot Innovation Add-On Pathways. CMS should investigate temporary reimbursement
mechanisms conceptually analogous to 42 CFR 412.87 for validated AI tools that demonstrably
achieve measurable improvements in timeliness, cost reduction, or burden mitigation.
Infrastructure investment must temporally precede the generation of insights. Payment
policy should accurately reflect this essential sequence.
III. Research Priorities
HHS should strategically prioritize research in the following critical domains:
• Explainable AI within authentic, auditable, real-world clinical workflows
• Federated multi-jurisdiction analytics methodologies
• Techniques for bias detection in longitudinal datasets
• AI-driven methodologies for data quality improvement
• Human-centered implementation science
• Identify data networks that provide real time, real world data sources that go beyond
Acute Care/EHR (Hospital) sources of data
While current literature documents gains in predictive performance and documentation
efficiency, empirical evidence regarding system-wide cost redistribution, workforce impact, and
long-term savings remains constrained. Federal research investment should be directed toward
closing this critical knowledge gap.

Appendix A: Detailed Responses to HHS Health Sector AI RFI
Comprehensive Analysis of Key Considerations for AI in Healthcare (Questions 1–10)
1. Biggest Barriers to AI Adoption and Scaling in the Health Sector
The primary obstacles preventing the widespread, scaled deployment of artificial intelligence in
healthcare are multifaceted and interconnected:
• Fragmented and Inaccessible Data: The lack of standardized, high-quality, and easily
accessible patient data across disparate systems (e.g., EHRs, imaging archives, claims
databases) severely limits the ability to train, validate, and generalize robust AI models.
Data silos and inconsistent semantic tagging are persistent issues.
• Regulatory Ambiguity for Non-Device AI: While the FDA provides a clear pathway for
AI designated as a medical device, there is significant uncertainty regarding the
governance, oversight, and liability for clinical, operational, and administrative AI tools
that do not meet the definition of a device (e.g., risk stratification tools, documentation
assistance, workflow optimizers). This ambiguity stifles innovation and adoption.
• Misaligned Reimbursement and Economic Models: Current fee-for-service payment
models and established coding structures often fail to adequately recognize or
incentivize the use of AI tools, particularly those that drive efficiency, prevention, or
improved population health outcomes rather than just procedural volume. A clear,
consistent reimbursement pathway is absent.
• Liability and Accountability Uncertainty: A lack of clarity regarding liability allocation
among developers, implementers, and clinical users in the event of an AI-driven error
(e.g., misdiagnosis, care pathway error) creates substantial legal risk and impedes
organizational willingness to deploy new technologies. Establishing clear indemnification
and accountability frameworks is critical.
2. Necessary Regulatory and Payment Model Changes to Support AI
HHS must take coordinated action across regulatory and payment structures to create an
environment conducive to safe and effective AI adoption:
• Clarify Governance under 45 CFR Part 170 (Health IT Certification): HHS, through
the Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology (ONC), must
clearly define the certification criteria and scope of governance for non-device AI tools,
ensuring they meet minimum standards for safety, transparency, and data provenance.
• Strengthen Enforcement of 45 CFR Part 171 (Information Blocking): Aggressive
enforcement of information blocking regulations is essential to unlock the data necessary
for model development and validation. This includes focusing on barriers related to API
access and the cost-prohibitive nature of data extraction.
• Recognize and Address Federated Models under HIPAA (45 CFR 164.502 and
164.514): Policies must explicitly recognize and facilitate federated learning
environments, which allow models to be trained across distributed, protected data sets
without the need for centralized transfer of Protected Health Information (PHI), ensuring
compliance with minimum necessary use principles.
• Modernize Value-Based Reimbursement (42 CFR Parts 425 and 422): Payment
models for Accountable Care Organizations (ACOs) and Medicare Advantage (MA)
plans must be updated to explicitly recognize and incentivize the use of AI that
demonstrably improves efficiency, quality metrics, and cost management.

• Pilot AI-Specific Add-On Payments (Consistent with 42 CFR 412.87): CMS should
establish pilots for new technology add-on payments (NTAPs) specifically tailored to
high-value AI solutions to facilitate early adoption and dissemination, similar to
mechanisms used for novel medical devices.
• Expand Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation (CMMI) Demonstrations (42
U.S.C. § 1315a): CMMI should prioritize and scale demonstrations focused on
evaluating the cost-effectiveness and clinical impact of AI, particularly in areas like
chronic disease management, population health, and reduction of administrative burden.
3. Novel Legal and Policy Issues Requiring Immediate HHS Attention
AI introduces significant novel issues requiring coordinated federal guidance:
• Liability Allocation and Indemnification: The most pressing issue is defining clear
roles and responsibilities when an error occurs. HHS needs to convene stakeholders to
develop model language for contracts and indemnification agreements between AI
developers and healthcare providers.
• Documentation Standards for AI Use: Clear standards are needed for documenting
the use of an AI tool, including the model version, the input data used, the date of last
retraining/validation, and the degree to which a clinician overrode the AI
recommendation. This is crucial for malpractice defense and quality reporting.
• Model Drift Accountability: Policies must address how providers and developers
monitor and are held accountable for model performance degradation over time (model
drift) due to shifts in patient population, coding changes, or clinical practice patterns.
• HHS Action Required: HHS should proactively issue standardized governance
templates and risk assessment frameworks for AI deployment. Furthermore, it must
convene cross-agency coordination (including FDA, ONC, CMS, and Office for Civil
Rights) to ensure consistent messaging and policy implementation.
4. Effective Methods for Evaluating AI Performance and Safety
Robust evaluation must span the entire lifecycle of the AI model:
• Pre-Deployment Evaluation: Rigorous, multi-site testing is required, focusing on bias
detection (e.g., performance parity across different demographic groups,
socioeconomic status, and care settings) and calibration testing (ensuring the model’s
predicted probability aligns with observed outcomes).
• Post-Deployment Monitoring: Continuous, real-world monitoring is essential, including
tracking for model drift (performance degradation) and systematic tracking of override
events (instances where a clinician rejects or modifies an AI recommendation) to
identify potential safety issues or clinical misalignment.
• Support Mechanisms: HHS should fund and support this evaluation through
cooperative agreements for multi-site validation studies and by establishing
benchmarking programs that provide standardized, de-identified datasets for external
model testing and comparison.
5. Role of Accreditation, Certification, and Standards Bodies
Voluntary and collaborative mechanisms can accelerate trust and adoption:

• Encourage Voluntary AI Accreditation: HHS should encourage the development of
voluntary, third-party AI accreditation programs (e.g., through The Joint Commission or
NCQA) that are aligned with federal audit standards for security, equity, and
performance documentation.
• Create Regulatory Testing Sandboxes: Establish secure, controlled environments
("sandboxes") where developers and providers can test nascent AI tools in a real-world
setting under temporary regulatory waivers, allowing for rapid iteration and evidence
generation.
• Develop Model Reporting Templates: Mandate or strongly encourage the use of
standardized reporting templates (e.g., adapted from the Model Card or Data Card
concept) to clearly document a model's intended use, performance limitations, training
data characteristics, and known bias testing results.
• Establish Transparency Registries: Create a centralized, public or semi-public registry
for reporting essential metadata on deployed clinical and operational AI models,
enhancing transparency for providers, researchers, and patients.
6. Current Performance Experience and Most Promising Future Tools
The health sector has seen varying degrees of success with current AI deployment:
• Areas of Strong Performance: AI has shown powerful, validated performance in areas
such as medical imaging analysis (e.g., radiology, pathology), clinical documentation
assistance (e.g., ambient listening, charting suggestions), and predictive modeling for
operational needs (e.g., forecasting patient flow, resource utilization).
• Areas of Weak Performance and Integration Challenges: Performance suffers
significantly where integration into existing clinical workflows is cumbersome, and
where explainability is absent, leading to a lack of clinician trust and poor adoption
rates.
• Most Promising Future Tools: The highest future impact is anticipated from federated
surveillance and public health analytics (allowing models to track disease outbreaks
or resource needs without centralizing PHI) and longitudinal risk scoring that
integrates clinical, claims, and social determinants of health data to proactively manage
high-risk patients.
7. Key Organizational Decision Makers and Adoption Hurdles
Adoption requires navigating a complex internal landscape:
• Key Decision Makers: Adoption decisions are often a committee-based process
involving the Chief Information Officer (CIO), Chief Medical Information Officer
(CMIO), Compliance Officer, Chief Financial Officer (CFO), and Legal Counsel.
• Primary Adoption Hurdles:
o Lengthy Procurement Cycles: The time and effort required to move from
evaluation to contracting is often prohibitive.
o Indemnification Negotiations: Protracted legal battles over liability and risk
sharing between developer and provider.
o EHR Integration Barriers: Technical complexity, vendor-specific limitations, and
the cost associated with deep integration into electronic health record systems
remain the single largest technical barrier.
8. Interoperability Priorities for AI Development and Deployment

Enhanced, standardized data flow is non-negotiable for AI success:
• Enforce FHIR Bulk Data Access (45 CFR 170.315(g)(10)): ONC must prioritize the
strict enforcement of the FHIR-based bulk data access requirement, enabling large-
scale, efficient data extraction necessary for training and deploying AI models across
patient populations.
• Advance Semantic Normalization and Benchmarking Tools: Investment and
standardization efforts are needed to harmonize disparate clinical terminologies (e.g.,
LOINC, SNOMED) and administrative codes, enabling models trained on one data set to
be reliably applied to another. HHS should support the creation of public-good tools for
semantic mapping.
9. Patient Concerns and Needs Regarding AI in Healthcare
Patient perspectives must guide the ethical implementation of AI:
• Desire for Efficiency and Personalization: Patients generally welcome AI tools that
promise to increase the speed of diagnosis, reduce administrative friction, and deliver
more personalized treatment recommendations.
• Concern about Bias and Opacity: The chief concerns relate to the potential for AI to
perpetuate or amplify existing health inequities (bias) and the lack of understanding
about how decisions are made (opacity or the "black box" problem).
• Required Reinforcements: Policy must emphasize radical transparency regarding
when and how AI influences care and mandate robust human oversight and review
mechanisms, ensuring that the ultimate decision-making authority rests with the human
clinician.
10. Critical Research Priorities for Public Investment
Federal research funding should be targeted to address foundational and implementation
challenges:
• Explainable AI (XAI): Research focused on developing reliable, clinically meaningful
methods for explaining AI outputs to both clinicians and patients, moving beyond simple
feature importance scores.
• Federated Analytics and Privacy-Preserving AI: Investment in technologies like
federated learning, homomorphic encryption, and differential privacy to enable model
training across sensitive, distributed data sources without violating privacy.
• Bias Detection, Mitigation, and Health Equity: Dedicated research on systematic
methods for proactively identifying, quantifying, and mitigating algorithmic bias across
diverse clinical populations and care settings.
• Implementation Science: Studies focused on the socio-technical aspects of AI
deployment, including human factors, workflow redesign, and the impact of AI on clinical
teams and patient safety culture.
• Cost-Transfer Analysis: Rigorous economic research to understand where the costs
and benefits of AI are realized (e.g., transfer of cost from the payer to the provider, or
from the hospital to the patient) to inform equitable reimbursement policy.
