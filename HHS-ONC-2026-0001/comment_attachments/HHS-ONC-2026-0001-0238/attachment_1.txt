The acceleration of artificial intelligence (AI) into clinical care holds tremendous promise for improving patient
outcomes, advancing public health, reducing the burdens on caregivers and providers across the healthcare
landscape, and helping to eliminate fraud, waste, and abuse (FWA). Deloitte recognizes HHS’s leadership in
accelerating responsible adoption of AI to deliver secure and high-value health solutions for the American people, and
optimizing operational spending for immediate and long-term impact. The following paper offers concrete
recommendations to help HHS shape regulatory, reimbursement, and research strategies that will enable a
“OneHHS” approach to AI, while upholding patient privacy, civil rights, and civil liberties.
Payment integrity is a priority for HHS, which can help patients understand payments ahead of service,
increase decision speed, lower administrative burden, and increase efficiency of the payer/provider
relationship. AI-supported clinical review solutions can improve the speed and consistency of Medicare payment
decisions. Application of machine learning in the payment space can minimize documentation and administrative
burden over time, drawing attention to the most relevant clinical data in the claims process, accelerating medical-
necessity review, and easing provider burden. Using AI to accelerate the review process, while retaining human
clinical oversight, ensures determinations remain clinically sound, transparent, and based on evidence. HHS could
adopt flexible design features such as pre-payment review, prior authorization support, and potential compliance-
based exemptions to further reduce administrative friction. Collectively, these capabilities strengthen payment
integrity across HHS systems, increasing consistency and reducing waste in alignment with agency goals.2
Using AI to standardize and codify data across electronic health records (EHRs) and insurance systems will
increase the speed of claims processing, support new regulations, fight FWA and improve policy
implementation.6 Today, medical policies often live in PDFs or narrative documents that require manual
interpretation, creating inconsistency, administrative burden, and audit risk for both payers and providers. AI can
rapidly extract and standardize these policies, providing clear, uniform rules that accelerate medical-necessity review
and reducing variation in how policies are applied. This codification also strengthens interoperability by transforming
PDFs into discrete data that can flow across payer and provider systems, enabling faster, more accurate claims
processing and supporting new regulatory mandates that depend on consistency, speed, and transparency. Moreover,
by providing explicit guidance and guardrails for how AI should be used in medical-necessity review, HHS can give
industry stakeholders confidence that automated processes are compliant, auditable, and fair, addressing long-
standing fears of AI-driven denials and ensuring responsible adoption.
Real-time claims adjudication (RTA) enables immediate, transparent, and accurate determination of patient
responsibility and provider payment at the point of care, eliminating the weeks-long delay inherent in today’s
claims cycle. In the current system, patients often wait weeks after a visit to understand their financial responsibility,
and providers experience substantial cash flow delays due to slow, manual, and administratively heavy claims review
processes; problems driven by inconsistent data, complex coding requirements, and payer imposed friction that can
-
reduce or delay payment.9 HHS adopting real time adjudication replaces this opaque and costly model by processing
-
claims instantly, allowing members to know what they owe before or during the visit and enabling providers, including
-
those treating Medicare patients, to be paid immediately at the point of service. Externally, research shows that
real time adjudication can dramatically reduce administrative burden, standardize coding and claims processes, cut
errors, improve transparency, and address coordination failures across health systems.4 When paired with AI, such as
-
automated coding validation, fraud detection, and real time policy checks, RTA can further lower denial rates, reduce
administrative overhead, and accelerate clean claims, offering a foundation for new incentive models where faster
-
payments can be exchanged for lower rates, aligning payer–provider interests and improving system wide efficiency.
To amplify the value of the AI use cases outlined above, HHS can implement a federated system- that enables
agencies and institutions to collaborate without pooling sensitive data. A federated learning approach delivers

privacy and collaboration to accelerate HHS impact. Federated Learning is an approach to train AI/ML models over
decentralized data. It is accomplished by distributing a model and its corresponding training code to a decentralized
set of edge nodes that hold local datasets, execute the code to train the model on the local data, and return the
trained model to a central orchestrator. The orchestrator then assembles all locally trained models into a global
model. This means that participating organizations can keep their data secure behind their firewalls, while permitting
other users to use their data for training purposes. By keeping data securely within its original environment, federated
learning enhances privacy, reduces transfer and infrastructure costs, and supports robust, uncompromised
collaboration at scale. When implemented across agencies with clear data standards and effective governance,
federated learning can empower HHS to rapidly develop AI models that can unlock breakthroughs in research across
chronic disease, nutrition, and other public health threats. Federated learning also enables quick wins for HHS across
automation of research workflows and accelerates research breakthroughs in areas such as chronic disease
management, nationwide outbreak detection, management of multi-center clinical trial augmentation, and discovery
of genetic/rare diseases.10
HHS can set standards for data use in federated learning.8
• Childhood Autism Research: With similar challenges as health research (e.g. data silos, fragmented
datasets located across hospitals, schools, therapy centers, etc.), a federated system can support autism
research by providing early diagnostic prediction models, improved understanding of heterogeneity in autism
symptoms and characteristics, and more accurate matching of interventions to patient profiles.
• Diabetes Research: Datasets that inform chronic disease cannot be readily pooled, but with federated
learning, HHS could identify progression models for Type 2 Diabetes based on diet, activity level, medication
adherence, social determinants of health, and glucose levels, among other factors.
• Vaccine Safety: Vaccine safety monitoring relies on vast and diverse datasets (e.g. clinical trial results,
electronic health records, surveillance data) which are held by different agencies. HHS can coordinate the
analysis of trusted and verified data to maintain the highest levels of understanding for vaccine safety.
