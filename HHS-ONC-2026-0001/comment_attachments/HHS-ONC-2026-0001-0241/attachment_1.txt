Background
CHIME is an executive organization dedicated to serving chief information officers (CIOs), chief
medical information officers (CMIOs), chief nursing information officers (CNIOs), chief
innovation officers (CIOs), chief digital officers (CDOs) and other senior healthcare IT leaders.
With thousands of individual members, and hundreds of CHIME Foundation healthcare IT
business and professional service firm members, CHIME and its communities provide a highly
interactive, trusted environment enabling senior professional and industry leaders to collaborate,
exchange best practices, address professional development needs, and advocate for the
effective use of information management to improve the health and care in the communities
they serve.
Key Recommendations and Takeaways
Our members – hospital and healthcare system CIOs, CISOs, and digital health leaders – are
early adopters and stewards of AI technologies and are acutely aware of both their promise and
their risks. We welcome HHS’s recognition that regulatory frameworks, reimbursement policies,
and research investments must evolve in tandem to ensure AI tools are safe, effective, and
deployable at scale. CHIME strongly believes that we must responsibly advance AI adoption,

improve patient outcomes, reduce clinician burden, and lower healthcare costs, while
maintaining the trust and confidence of patients and providers.
AI is no longer a distant concept. It is reshaping the very foundation of healthcare delivery. From
enhancing diagnostic accuracy to streamlining administrative workflows, AI offers
unprecedented opportunities to improve patient outcomes, reduce clinician burden, and foster
innovation across the care continuum. Yet, with these opportunities come profound
responsibilities.
Healthcare providers stand at the intersection of technology and trust. As stewards of patient
safety and privacy, they must navigate a rapidly evolving landscape where algorithms influence
clinical decisions and data flows beyond traditional boundaries. The CHIME Principles for
Responsible AI provide a comprehensive approach describing both challenges and potential
solutions to help nurture responsible AI adoption. They serve as a guide to understanding and
implementing AI responsibly anchored in principles of transparency, fairness, and patient-
centered care.
Core Pillars of CHIME’s AI Principles
• Protecting Patients and Their Data: Outlines expectations for robust cybersecurity
practices, streamlined privacy laws, clear patient consent policies, and standardized
security requirements for third-party technologies.
• Fostering Patient-Centered Care: Emphasizes the importance of human oversight,
adoption of voluntary frameworks and standards, risk-based monitoring, and ongoing
transparency regarding AI’s role in clinical decision-making.
• Empowering Providers: Highlights the need to equip providers, especially small and
under-resourced organizations, with education, resources, and support while promoting
transparency, innovation, and equitable access to AI-enabled tools.
As HHS recognizes, AI is inherently data-driven and dependent on robust interoperability,
governance, and safeguards to ensure appropriate use, privacy, and security. The Department’s
RFI is timely and necessary as providers navigate a rapidly evolving AI ecosystem in which
clinical utility, regulatory clarity, reimbursement pathways, and liability frameworks remain
fragmented and uncertain. We offer the following comments to highlight practical considerations
from frontline providers and to recommend policy approaches that balance innovation with
patient safety, provider accountability, and sustainable implementation.
Detailed Input & Recommendations
In this RFI, HHS is seeking concrete, experience-based feedback from those building, buying,
evaluating, using, and receiving care from AI tools that are part of clinical care as well as from
those who wish to do so but face barriers. CHIME’s feedback addresses the questions outlined
in this RFI, which seeks public feedback on the actions the Department can take to establish a
forward-leaning, industry-supportive, and secure approach to accelerate the adoption and use
of AI as part of clinical care.
AI holds significant promise to enhance clinical decision-making, streamline workflows, and
improve patient access and outcomes. At the same time, successful and sustainable AI
adoption requires clear, risk-based regulatory pathways, interoperable data infrastructure, and
policies that foster innovation while safeguarding patient privacy and security. HHS’s recognition
2

that federal regulatory, reimbursement, and research policies must be better aligned to support
responsible AI integration is both timely and necessary.
CHIME’s input seeks to provide practical, provider-informed recommendations to help the
Department advance a flexible, innovation-enabling policy framework that supports real-world
clinical implementation without imposing duplicative or prescriptive requirements that could
impede adoption or increase provider burden. AI is not a replacement for human judgment; it is
a tool to augment clinical expertise. By embracing innovation while safeguarding trust, HHS can
ensure that AI fulfills its promise: improving health for all.
AI implementation in clinical care raises complex questions regarding data governance,
interoperability, workforce readiness, liability, and a growing “digital divide.” HHS’s focus on
patient trust, data security, and interoperability reflects foundational prerequisites for scalable AI
adoption and aligns with long-standing provider priorities. CHIME members wish to emphasize
the need for clear, risk-based policies, alignment with existing health IT frameworks, and
pragmatic solutions that reflect the realities of healthcare system operations.
CHIME members are actively deploying and evaluating AI-enabled tools in clinical,
administrative, and operational settings. However – broadly, private-sector AI innovation and
clinical adoption for healthcare providers are slowed by financial, technical and data constraints,
regulatory and market-structure misalignment, and organizational challenges in implementation
and scale.
Peer-reviewed evidence indicates that AI-based clinical decision support tools embedded within
electronic health records (EHRs) are widely deployed, in part because certain tools have
historically fallen outside FDA oversight and because activation costs are perceived as modest;
however, clinicians and health systems continue to raise concerns regarding accuracy, clinical
value, and utility.1 The study further found that, “Outside [of] medical imaging and EHR-
embedded applications, dissemination of clinical AI tools has been slower. The primary barrier is
likely that healthcare systems responsible for buying these tools do not believe they offer
enough value. The costs of not just licensing a tool but also of ensuring adequate training,
digital infrastructure, maintenance, and monitoring can be considerable. There is no
reimbursement for most AI tool use, and even in select cases where insurers provide
reimbursement, it does not offset the full costs.”1
For those under-resourced providers attempting to adopt AI, some simply lack the purchasing
power to afford it, including those solutions available through their EHR. AI uptake is also
occurring at a higher rate among larger providers – while smaller, lesser-resourced providers
struggle to keep pace with long-standing issues like solvency and reimbursement.
A study published in JAMIA in May 2025 examining mid- to large-provider adoption of AI
concluded that systems with greater financial and technical resources are more likely to adopt
and scale AI technologies. The rationale for AI adoption cited most frequently among
respondents was related to clinician burden. All survey respondents indicated successful
adoption of ambient AI but outside of this use case, AI adoption was uneven among providers.
The authors found that “Other select AI use cases such as imaging/radiology and clinical risk
stratification are adopted by many, though successes are modest.”

National Frameworks are Needed
CHIME continues to reiterate that the U.S. needs a comprehensive national data privacy law to
better protect consumers’ sensitive health information and inform consumers of how their data is
being used without duplicating what is already required under HIPAA. Furthermore, CHIME
members believe that a federal privacy law and a federal AI law that preempts state laws are
both needed to reduce burden and improve efficiency.
The amount of health data being created using AI is staggering in size. Advanced models
analyzing x-rays, magnetic resonance imaging (MRIs), clinical notes, and genomic data
contribute massive amounts of unstructured data. This data is in addition to the data being
created by smart devices that are continuously collecting health metrics. A single major hospital
can generate upwards of 50 petabytes of data annually which translates to approximately 170
years of HD video footage according to the World Economic Forum. The exponential growth of
health data requires heightened responsibility from healthcare providers to safeguard its
security, integrity, and responsible application.
The existing state patchwork of privacy laws has created an enormously complicated legal
landscape for healthcare providers and other stakeholders. Varying sets of state laws present
complexity, burden, and added costs to the healthcare system. Thus, the need for a national
data privacy law has become increasingly important. Five years ago, there were five states with
privacy laws enacted; today 20 states have privacy laws. As providers move to adopt AI and the
types of tools they are adopting are more complex, the privacy challenges are only mounting. A
federal law that preempts the myriad of state laws could bring greater efficiency to providers and
reduce administrative burden and complexity.
In the absence of a federal law on AI, and interest in AI at an all-time high, states continue
passing their own laws which have addressed mental health AI tools (i.e., chatbots), AI in
insurance and claims, clinical decision support systems, patient data privacy and algorithmic
transparency. Similar to the need for a federal privacy law that preempts state laws, a single
federal AI law that preempts state laws is needed.
AI Regulation, Reimbursement, and Research & Development
Regulatory Clarity and Flexibility will Spur Innovation
CHIME respectfully submits that a modernized, streamlined regulatory framework is essential to
unlocking the full innovative potential of AI in clinical care. While appropriate safeguards remain
critical to protect patients and maintain public trust, overly prescriptive or duplicative
requirements can inadvertently delay deployment, increase costs, and divert scarce resources
from patient care, and innovative cybersecurity investments. To foster meaningful innovation,
federal policy should emphasize regulatory clarity, and flexibility – creating an environment in
which healthcare providers can responsibly test, refine, and scale AI solutions without
unnecessary administrative friction. Thoughtful regulatory reform will not diminish accountability;
rather, it will enable providers to innovate more rapidly and effectively in service of better
outcomes, lower costs, and a more resilient healthcare system.
Navigating Patient Consent
Providers are using AI in a variety of ways. As AI becomes increasingly embedded in clinical
workflows, healthcare providers face mounting pressure to modernize patient consent policies.
4

A central challenge lies in distinguishing between explainable AI, which attempts to explain how
an algorithm arrived at its answer or output, and interpretable AI, which allows users to more
intuitively understand how an AI-driven decision was reached. While both frameworks aim to
enhance trust and transparency, they present different implications for consent. Explainable AI
often requires complex, technical justification that may overwhelm or confuse patients, whereas
interpretable AI lends itself to clearer communication but may be limited in scope.
As more providers adopt AI, they must determine how to handle informing the patients they treat
about its use in their care. Determining how and when to inform patients, and to what degree it
is being used is still an evolving area for providers. The integration of AI into clinical workflows –
ranging from ambient listening tools (a voice recognition technology that captures a patient
interaction with a clinician) to traditional clinical decision support systems to chatbots – raises
difficult questions about what, when, and how to best disclose its use.
As AI tools such as clinical decision support systems, ambient listening technologies, and
scheduling assistants become more prevalent in healthcare, it’s increasingly important to clarify
how patient consent applies under HIPAA’s Treatment, Payment, and Healthcare Operations
(TPO) framework – particularly since many of these tools may operate in the background or
across multiple functions. For example, ambient listening used for clinical documentation may
fall under ‘treatment’ or ‘operations’ depending on its use, while revenue cycle AI tools clearly
support ‘payment.’ Clarifying these distinctions helps ensure that patients understand when their
data can be used without explicit consent under TPO, and when additional authorization may be
required.
In some organizations, consent is obtained at the beginning of the patient’s care. The challenge
lies in determining how much detail to disclose about AI and machine learning (ML) tools that
can impact patient care. While some argue that disclosing every automation would overwhelm
patients, others believe that complete transparency is crucial. To address this, some institutions
have built an inventory of AI tools and share summaries with internal teams, with plans to make
this information available to patients in the future. They use platforms, including their websites,
to inform patients about AI usage rather than providing notifications for every instance. Surveys
indicate that patients do not want to be notified every time AI is used. This underscores the
need to consider whether a single consent form should be used to inform patients about
AI usage.
Providers continue to evaluate how extensively AI should be integrated into their organizations,
as they are cautious about increasing the discoverability of sensitive information and are
carefully considering the best approach to ensure patient consent and data privacy. As AI
continues to permeate the healthcare delivery landscape, one member reflected, “getting
consent for each and every use of AI will be wildly impractical.”
Balancing Innovation with Appropriate Oversight and Support for Providers
Current regulatory frameworks provide an important baseline for oversight of health information
technology; however, the 21st Century Cures Act2 includes exemptions that have led to
inconsistencies in the regulatory treatment of third-party AI tools and applications. One CHIME
member – a practicing emergency physician – emphasized that these inconsistencies can allow
certain AI-enabled functionalities to evade FDA oversight, creating gaps in safety and
effectiveness assurance as generative and agentic AI increasingly blur the line between

administrative and clinical use and strain traditional intended-use frameworks. HHS could
further clarify and modernize these exemptions to ensure appropriate oversight of high-risk AI
tools while preserving flexibility for low-risk innovation. For example, the January 2026 update to
the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices
guidance demonstrates a thoughtful approach to delineate when certain digital health and
software functions fall outside traditional medical device oversight based on intended use and
risk. The guidance reiterates that software functions intended solely to maintain or encourage a
healthy lifestyle and are unrelated to the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention, or treatment of
disease may fall outside the statutory device definition, while other low-risk products may be
subject to enforcement discretion.
Given that FDA operates within HHS, CHIME encourages the Department to further promote
greater cross-agency alignment and clarity regarding how risk-based oversight frameworks
apply to AI-enabled technologies across the continuum of wellness and clinical care. Doing so
would help ensure that regulatory boundaries are sufficiently clear for providers, support
appropriate accountability for higher-risk third-party AI tools, and reduce ambiguity that can slow
responsible and innovative adoption.
A recent peer-reviewed analysis published in JAMA highlights significant ambiguity in
accountability for evaluating the health effects of AI tools in healthcare. The article observes
that, while developers of AI tools subject to FDA review bear initial responsibility for evaluation,
such assessments may not extend to real-world clinical outcomes. Developers of tools exempt
from FDA clearance often design evaluations around product claims – such as subscriber
engagement for direct-to-consumer applications or financial performance for revenue cycle
management solutions – without systematically assessing potential health impacts. Healthcare
delivery organizations (HDOs) may conduct independent evaluations, yet many lack sufficient
funding or specialized expertise to do so comprehensively. Federal support through grants
exists but is limited, leaving patients and communities – critical stakeholders whose insights are
essential to understanding real-world effects – routinely excluded from these assessments.1
Mitigating Risk and Liability for Providers
AI use in healthcare is mostly voluntary at present, but as evidence of its effectiveness grows,
not using AI could be seen as falling below the standard of care, potentially exposing providers
to malpractice claims. Conversely, when AI is used and an adverse outcome occurs, liability
could involve the clinician, the health system, or the AI developer. Although relevant case law is
currently limited, all parties must have strategies to address potential liability risk.1 Healthcare
providers and systems face well-established risks – including malpractice liability, reputational
harm, regulatory or board sanctions, loss of medical licensure, credentialing restrictions, and
financial exposure – whereas AI developers are generally far better positioned financially to
withstand potential legal challenges.
Therefore, CHIME members believe that one of the most urgent policy needs is a clear
and workable framework for patient consent and liability related to the use of generative
AI in clinical care delivery. As generative AI tools become increasingly embedded across
clinical, administrative, and operational workflows, providers anticipate that such tools will soon
be ubiquitous and integral to routine care delivery.
It is neither feasible nor clinically appropriate for patients to opt out of all AI-enabled functions
within modern health systems, and that highly granular disclosures for each individual AI use
case would be operationally impractical and could undermine care delivery. At the same time,
6

the absence of clear federal guidance creates uncertainty regarding consent obligations and
litigation risk.
Accordingly, CHIME recommends the establishment of a standardized, technology-
neutral patient consent framework and a legal safe harbor for the use of generative AI in
healthcare, analogous and aligned to existing informed consent and privacy frameworks
(i.e., HIPAA), to reduce legal ambiguity, support responsible innovation, and protect both
patients and providers. Such a framework could include model language, baseline disclosure
principles, and liability protections for good-faith use of AI consistent with federal standards,
thereby mitigating the risk of fragmented state-level requirements and opportunistic litigation
while preserving patient trust and transparency.
Removing Cost Barriers
CHIME members report that, although AI-enabled tools are improving care delivery and patient
engagement, current reimbursement structures do not adequately account for the associated
costs. One member described deploying an AI-driven agent to proactively contact patients with
hypertension and other chronic conditions to improve monitoring and adherence. While the
organization anticipates downstream clinical and financial benefits from improved outcomes, it is
currently absorbing the upfront and operational costs because no direct reimbursement pathway
exists for these services.
Similarly, another member described comparable challenges during telehealth implementation,
where the costs of equipment, infrastructure, and staffing facilitators exceeded available
reimbursement, despite measurable improvements in access and quality. These experiences
underscore a persistent misalignment between innovation that advances patient care and
payment models that fail to recognize or support the associated investments. In the absence of
sustainable reimbursement mechanisms, providers are effectively required to subsidize
technologies that enhance quality, safety, and patient engagement – an approach that is not
scalable over time.
One CHIME member – a regional safety-net/anchor system – shared that emerging
technologies, including AI-enabled clinical tools, are evaluated through structured governance
processes based on defined clinical use cases, business need, return on investment, and the
level of autonomy and associated patient safety risk. A core principle of these governance
frameworks is education and accountability – recognizing that, in the absence of comprehensive
regulatory oversight, providers bear primary responsibility for determining whether a technology
is safe, effective, and appropriate for clinical deployment.
To operationalize risk-based decision-making, this organization classifies AI and other emerging
technologies into categories such as novel, platform, or commodity, with each category
informing tailored acceptance criteria and risk-benefit thresholds. This tiered approach reflects
the need for differentiated regulatory and oversight expectations based on maturity, autonomy,
and clinical impact, and underscores the importance of aligning federal policy with provider-
driven risk governance models.
CHIME members cite AI-enabled radiology tools as illustrative of the current reimbursement
gap. These technologies are increasingly integrated into clinical workflows to perform
preliminary computational image analysis, support clinical decision-making, and algorithmically
prioritize radiologist worklists based on predicted acuity. Despite their measurable impact on
7

workflow optimization, timeliness of interpretation, and patient safety, existing payment
methodologies do not clearly recognize or account for these technology-enabled services.
In practice, these systems rapidly analyze routine imaging studies upon acquisition, flag
potential abnormalities, and elevate high-risk cases for expedited clinician review. One member
indicated that this AI-driven triage can reduce turnaround times for routine films from
approximately 20 hours to roughly one hour, materially improving clinical workflow efficiency and
patient care timeliness. These tools are used as an adjunct to, rather than a replacement for,
clinician review, with radiologists continuing to perform final reads and clinical validation.
Importantly, this workflow introduces a dual-layer interpretive process: an initial algorithmic
assessment for triage and prioritization, followed by the radiologist’s definitive clinical
interpretation and diagnostic reporting. Both steps represent separate yet distinct contributions
to the diagnostic process and require governance, validation, and integration into clinical
operations.
Such workflows effectively create a dual-review paradigm – an initial AI-assisted prioritization or
preliminary assessment followed by clinician interpretation – yet current payment models do not
recognize the clinical value, operational efficiency, or quality improvement associated with AI-
enabled workflow optimization. At this time, the absence of a reimbursement mechanism or
established payment pathway for providers that have adopted this AI-driven triage model results
in providers bearing all of the associated costs.
HHS has an opportunity to advance its stated goals of reducing administrative burden,
accelerating responsible innovation, and improving patient access to timely, high-quality care by
providing clear, flexible policy guardrails for AI-enabled clinical workflows. Thoughtful
clarification regarding the regulatory and reimbursement treatment of tools including AI-assisted
triage and clinician interpretation would promote adoption of technologies that demonstrably
increase efficiency, support clinician decision-making, and improve patient outcomes, while
avoiding prescriptive requirements that could slow innovation or impose unnecessary
compliance costs on providers. We encourage the Department to pursue a risk-based,
outcomes-focused approach with reimbursement that enables providers to integrate AI
tools into clinical practice in a manner that is scalable, interoperable, and aligned with
patient safety and quality objectives.

Members have observed that as trust and reliance on AI tools increase, associated costs are

reimbursement policy.
CHIME encourages HHS to expand the range of incentives available to promote the use
of AI in clinical care, as such incentives are essential to accelerating adoption.
Additionally, CMS should consider implementing enhanced reimbursement mechanisms
or bonus payments for healthcare providers who deploy AI in clinical care, recognizing
the operational costs, efficiency gains, and measurable improvements in patient care
that such technologies can deliver.

Frameworks & Standards
As providers chart their AI course, they are looking for ways to deploy and use AI in an ethical
manner. Many hospitals and healthcare systems have AI councils, acceptable use, and/or
governance policies established within their organizations, though rural, safety-net, and smaller
providers are either just getting started or have not yet taken the leap. Providers may also be
served by having structures and processes to report potential safety events related to the AI
tools. In the beginning, this could be an internal process. There are emerging AI frameworks
and standards that serve as a good starting point for HDOs embarking on or refining their AI
adoption journey. They are intended to enable responsible, ethical, and effective advancements
in health AI on behalf of the patients and communities they serve. Resources exist to help
providers navigate the AI journey; CHIME’s AI Principles offer a detailed overview of existing
standards and frameworks, and below we offer a high-level overview of just a few.
The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), which was developed in conjunction with
the private sector, and “intended for voluntary use and to improve the ability to incorporate
trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI products,
services, and systems.” America’s AI Action Plan calls for revising the existing Framework.
Providers are already deeply familiar with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) and have
voluntarily adopted it. Released in 2023, the NIST AI RMF offers a complementary framework to
better manage risks to individuals, organizations, and society associated with AI. NIST is also
developing a way to reach consensus faster around AI standards development – typically a
lengthy process – through their “Zero Drafts” pilot project.
11

The National Academy of Medicine’s Artificial Intelligence Code of Conduct is “aimed at
providing a guiding framework to ensure that AI algorithms and their application in health, health
care, and biomedical science perform accurately, safely, reliably, and ethically in the service of
better health for all.”
The Health AI Partnership (HAIP) is a multi-stakeholder, provider-driven effort spearheaded by
Duke University, and offers tools to help providers navigate the purchase and deployment of AI
including designing and testing clinician workflow and disseminating information to end users.
Their Health Equity Across the AI Lifecyle (HEAAL) Framework is community-informed
framework developed through extensive collaboration with an interdisciplinary team of
developers and inputs from seventy seven practitioners involved in various functions of HDOs.
HEAAL is a comprehensive measure intended to be used whenever an HDO considers
implementing an AI solution – and is empowering providers to safely and fairly use AI through
the use of standards to focus on procurement, development, clinical integration, and lifecycle
management.
Privacy, Security, & Third-Party Solutions
AI’s integration into healthcare heightens the need for robust cybersecurity and privacy
protections. Providers face increasing regulatory burdens, fragmented policies, and rising
threats from AI-driven cyberattacks; therefore, the critical and inextricable domain of
cybersecurity as it intersects with the clinical deployment of AI cannot and should not be
ignored. CHIME’s AI Principles recognize that trustworthy, scalable, and clinically responsible AI
cannot exist apart from a resilient cybersecurity posture, given the uniquely targeted threat
environment facing healthcare providers and the sensitivity, volume, and interoperability of
clinical data assets.
We therefore urge the Department to evaluate clinical AI policy within the broader
operational reality confronting hospitals and health systems – namely, a persistently
hostile cyber landscape marked by ransomware, third-party supply chain vulnerabilities,
and adversarial nation-state activity – while aligning federal expectations for AI
governance with existing statutory and regulatory obligations under the Health Insurance
Portability & Accountability Act (HIPAA), the Health Information Technology for Economic
and Clinical Health Act (HITECH), and related frameworks. As further detailed in our
principles, cybersecurity must be treated not as an ancillary safeguard, but as a foundational
prerequisite to safe, ethical, and sustainable clinical AI adoption.
Vendors Routinely Shift More Risk to Providers
HIPAA privacy protections extend only to HIPAA covered entities (CEs) which include providers,
payers and healthcare clearinghouses, as well as any business associates (BAs) of these
entities. Companies that have access to PHI and do business with a HIPAA CE are required by
law to safeguard the data. However, some vendors try to avoid risk by refusing to agree to
certain provider terms and provisions in BAAs, shifting more risk to providers. As providers ink
agreements with AI vendors, they have reported vendors attempting to shift liability by refusing
to accept key terms in the BAA or overriding restrictions in the BAA – exposing them to
additional risk.
Our members also report vendors using contracts that append the BAA as an attachment and
use language in the main contract that overrides all other language as it pertains to data use.
12

Some AI vendors have refused to sign BAAs with providers, while others have elected to train
their models using de-identified data instead. Although HIPAA requires a standard BAA, the
regulatory language sets only a minimum threshold—stating that a BA may use or disclose PHI
solely as permitted by the agreement with the CE. The challenge arises when vendors embed
broad data use rights into the main service agreements, effectively circumventing the intent of
the BAA. Many vendors assert that de-identified data falls outside HIPAA’s scope, using this
claim to justify expansive data use, often in ways that make providers uncomfortable. However,
de-identification is highly subjective and context-dependent, and vendor language—such as
assurances that “de-identified data will not be used in any way that could identify a natural
human”—raises serious concerns about enforceability and the potential for re-identification. As
HHS’ OCR has emphasized, the Privacy Rule recognizes two permissible de-identification
methodologies: (1) a formal determination by a qualified expert, or (2) the removal of specified
identifiers coupled with the absence of actual knowledge that the remaining information could be
used to identify an individual. Importantly, even when these methodologies are properly applied,
the resulting data retains a residual risk of identification; while the probability is very small, it is
not zero, and de-identified data may, in certain circumstances, be linked back to the individual to
whom it pertains.
In a perfect world, the original intent of HIPAA would be reinforced: data should be used solely
for the purposes outlined in the agreement and only in ways that directly support the services for
which the provider has contracted. Unfortunately, many providers cannot afford to walk
away from certain vendors, placing them in an impossible bind—underscoring the urgent
need for increased oversight by the Office for Civil Rights (OCR) to ensure that the spirit
of HIPAA is upheld.
Healthcare providers are increasingly constrained when purchasing and procuring applications,
medical devices, AI solutions, and other digital technologies. Vendors often deliver products with
insufficient baseline security assurances, forcing providers to shoulder the burden of conducting
individual risk assessments that are costly, duplicative, and resource-intensive. This fragmented
approach undermines efficiency, slows adoption of innovative tools, and can leave critical
vulnerabilities unaddressed across the ecosystem.
Third-Party Risk and Burden for Providers
A marketplace should be established for certified apps – including those leveraging AI –
which have demonstrated their ability to meet a baseline set of security requirements.
Establishing consistent, transparent, and enforceable security standards for third-party
technologies is essential to reducing provider burden, strengthening supply chain
resilience, and protecting patients from escalating cyber threats.
Providers continue to grapple with substantial vulnerabilities inherent in third-party solutions,
underscoring the need for remedial action to safeguard the confidentiality, integrity, and
availability of Americans’ health data. Providers need confidence that the technologies they
purchase are secure; however, in the absence of a uniform set of standards that must be met,
our members are forced to conduct their own risk assessments when procuring essential
medical devices, apps, and AI solutions.
A rigorous review of third-party devices and applications is needed to ensure compliance and
security best practices. Providers are saddled with these time-consuming and costly processes
as they conduct their own assessments on the same technology being purchased by thousands
of other providers, which is inefficient, redundant, and a waste of precious healthcare resources.
13

When delivering updates to software that deliver new AI capabilities or updates or changes AI
components, vendors should be required to disclose those AI components, the type of AI and
the data used, as well as disclose their related security assessment including addressing
relevant threats and vulnerabilities. CHIME firmly believes that shifting risk and burden onto
providers is slowing AI adoption in clinical care.
Conclusion
In closing, CHIME appreciates the opportunity to provide input in response to this RFI and to
contribute to HHS-wide policy development concerning the clinical use of AI. On behalf of our
members, we offer practical, provider-informed recommendations grounded in real-world
implementation experience.
Our comments are intended to assist the Department in determining how its regulatory,
reimbursement, and research and development authorities can be most effectively calibrated to
support the responsible and timely adoption of AI in clinical care; foster public trust and
confidence in modern health technologies; reduce regulatory and liability uncertainty that may
impede innovation; and better align federal incentives so that AI deployment enhances
productivity, alleviates administrative burden, lowers healthcare costs, and improves health
outcomes for patients, caregivers, and communities.
To realize AI’s full potential, providers must be equipped with the knowledge, resources,
technical infrastructure, and policy certainty necessary to overcome adoption barriers. CHIME
and its members remain steadfast in their commitment to leveraging technology to deliver high-
quality, patient-centered care and to advancing interoperability and secure, appropriate access
to health information across the care continuum. At the same time, we respectfully urge HHS to
ensure that future rulemaking or guidance does not inadvertently impose duplicative or
prescriptive requirements that could divert limited resources away from patient care,
investments in digital technology such as AI tools, or cybersecurity investments.
CHIME and our members look forward to continuing to be a trusted stakeholder to the
Department, and stand ready to serve as an ongoing resource as HHS advances its AI policy
objectives. Collaborative engagement between the public and private sectors will be essential to
achieving our shared goals and ensuring that AI strengthens – rather than complicates – the
delivery of healthcare in the United States.
