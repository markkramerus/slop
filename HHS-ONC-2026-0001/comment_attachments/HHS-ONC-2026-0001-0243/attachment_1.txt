Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and
Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care
I. Executive Summary
ALIGNMT AI is an AI governance platform provider that works with health systems, payers,
regulatory bodies, and AI developers to operationalize AI governance frameworks, risk
assessment, and accountability structures for healthcare AI. We offer these comments from the
perspective of practitioners who see daily the gap between the theoretical promise of healthcare
AI and the governance and oversight realities on the ground.
We are broadly supportive of HHS's ambition to accelerate AI adoption in clinical care. We are
equally convinced that acceleration without robust governance is not a shortcut - it is a liability.
The history of health IT is replete with examples of rapidly deployed technology that generated
unintended harm, eroded clinician trust, and ultimately set back adoption rather than advancing
it.
Our comments focus primarily on two areas where we believe HHS can take the most
consequential action:
● Standards and oversight: Establishing a coherent, risk-proportionate, and enforceable
governance framework for clinical AI - particularly for the large and growing category of
non-medical device AI tools - that reduces uncertainty for developers while providing
meaningful accountability for patients.
● Patient safety and equity: Ensuring that the imperative to accelerate AI adoption does
not come at the cost of the populations most likely to be harmed by poorly governed AI,
including patients from underserved communities, those with disabilities, and those with
limited health literacy.
We urge HHS to resist the false choice between speed and safety. The best governance
frameworks are enabling, not obstructive - they create the predictability and trust that
sustainable AI adoption requires.
Our core recommendation: HHS should establish a tiered, risk-proportionate oversight
framework for clinical AI that is coherent across FDA, ONC, and CMS - backed by quality
and equity requirements, transparency standards, and meaningful post-market
surveillance - and that applies to all AI tools used in consequential clinical decisions, not
only those that meet the FDA's definition of a medical device.
II. Barriers to Innovation (Question 1)

From our experience advising health systems and developers, the most consequential barriers
to responsible AI adoption in clinical care are governance-related, not technical:
Regulatory Fragmentation and Ambiguity
The fundamental governance problem facing clinical AI in the United States is jurisdictional
incoherence and incompleteness. FDA regulates AI that meets the definition of Software as a
Medical Device (SaMD) under 21 C.F.R. Part 880. ONC shapes health IT through certification
requirements under 45 C.F.R. Part 170. CMS influences behavior through payment policy. Each
operates on a different timeline, with different requirements, and for different subsets of the AI
ecosystem.
The result is that a large and clinically consequential category of AI tools - non-device clinical
decision support (CDS) that does not meet FDA's criteria under 21 U.S.C. §399d - operates in a
near-regulatory vacuum. These tools may analyze patient records, generate differential
diagnoses, recommend treatment pathways, flag patients for escalation, guide provider or
patient queries, suggest care plans, or provide clinical reference information. This category also
includes AI tools offered directly to patients and consumers — whether through patient portals,
mobile health applications, or direct-to-consumer platforms — that generate symptom
assessments, triage recommendations, care navigation guidance, or health coaching, and tools
offered by providers to patients as an extension of the clinical encounter. These tools occupy
the same regulatory vacuum as provider-facing non-device CDS, despite often operating with
less clinical oversight and reaching populations with greater health literacy variability. They are
used in life-affecting decisions. Yet they face no mandatory evaluation requirements, no
performance standards, no transparency obligations, and no post-deployment surveillance or
performance measurement requirements.
This is not primarily a barrier to innovation. It is a patient safety gap that also, secondarily,
creates business risk for health systems that deploy these tools and reputational risk for
developers whose products underperform in ways they did not anticipate.
Liability Ambiguity Creates Perverse Incentives
The current liability environment simultaneously discourages adoption by cautious health
systems or clinicians and fails to adequately incentivize responsible development by AI vendors.
When a clinical AI tool contributes to a harmful outcome, there is no settled legal framework
governing how responsibility is allocated across the developer, the deploying health system,
and the treating clinician. This ambiguity causes legal departments to counsel non-adoption,
even when the clinical evidence for a tool is strong - and it means that developers face limited
accountability when their tools cause harm.
Clear governance frameworks - including defined standards of care for AI deployment,
transparency requirements, and appropriate safe harbors for compliant adopters - would benefit
both sides of this equation.
Inequitable Access to Governance Infrastructure

Smaller health systems, rural providers, federally qualified health centers (FQHCs), community
health centers (CHCs), physician and clinician practice groups, generally lack the internal
capacity to conduct meaningful AI governance: they cannot independently validate vendor
claims, audit models for quality or bias, or establish robust post-deployment surveillance. This
creates an equity paradox: the organizations that serve the most vulnerable patients are least
equipped to protect those patients from poorly performing AI - and least able to capture AI's
benefits from a position of informed decision-making.
III. Regulatory Priorities (Question 2)
We recommend HHS treat the following regulatory actions as the highest-priority interventions
for accelerating responsible AI adoption:
3.1 Establish a Unified, Tiered Federal Framework for Clinical AI Governance
The most impactful single action HHS could take is to develop - in collaboration across FDA,
ONC, and CMS - a unified risk-tiered framework that applies consistently to all AI tools used in
consequential clinical decisions, regardless of whether those tools qualify as FDA-regulated
medical devices.
We recommend a three-tier structure for classifying healthcare AI tools based on their purpose
of use for risk management:
● Tier 1 — Autonomous or high-stakes decision AI: Tools that make or substantially
determine clinical decisions with limited clinician override capacity. These should be
subject to the most rigorous evaluation requirements, including prospective clinical
validation, mandatory demographic performance disaggregation, and active post-market
surveillance.
● Tier 2 — Clinical decision support with clinician review: Tools that generate
recommendations reviewed and acted upon by a clinician. These should require pre-
deployment validation documentation, transparency disclosures, and periodic
performance reporting. Patient and consumer-facing AI tools — including those offered
through providers via patient portals or care management platforms and those offered
directly to consumers outside a clinical relationship — require explicit placement within
this framework. Where such tools generate clinical recommendations, triage decisions,
or care guidance that a patient acts upon without an intervening clinician review, they
should be classified at minimum as Tier 2 and evaluated accordingly, notwithstanding
the absence of a traditional provider-patient workflow. Direct-to-consumer tools that
operate fully outside any provider relationship warrant additional consideration given the
reduced safety net available when recommendations are misunderstood or misapplied.

● Tier 3 — Administrative and workflow AI: Tools that do not directly affect clinical
decision-making (e.g., scheduling, coding, documentation assistance). These warrant
lighter oversight focused primarily on data privacy and security.
This framework would not require new statutory authority for many of its elements - it could be
implemented through ONC certification criteria (45 C.F.R. §170.315), CMS conditions of
participation, requirements for AI developers under the CMS Health Tech Ecosystem, and HHS-
wide guidance. It would also provide the clarity that developers and health systems need to plan
for compliance.
3.2 Mandatory Transparency and Disclosure Standards for Tier 1 and Tier 2 Tools
Health systems currently evaluate AI vendors with almost no standardized information required
from the developer. Vendors make performance claims using their own metrics, their own test
sets, and their own definitions. There is no equivalent of the FDA's Summary of Safety and
Effectiveness Data (SSED) for non-device clinical AI.
HHS should require, as a condition of use in any health systems, hospitals, physicians, or
clinicians funded by or in receipt of federal funds related to the provision of designated health
services as defined by 42 CFR 411.351, that developers of Tier 1 and Tier 2 clinical AI tools
provide standardized transparency documentation including:
● Training data characteristics: source, date range, demographic composition, and any
known gaps or biases.
● Validation methodology: whether validation was prospective or retrospective, internal or
external, and the characteristics of the validation population.
● Performance metrics: disaggregated by race, ethnicity, sex, age, primary language, and
insurance status at minimum.
● Known limitations and contraindications: specific patient populations or clinical contexts
where the tool has demonstrated degraded performance.
● Update and versioning history: what has changed in each model version and how
changes have been validated.
This standard should be developed in collaboration with NIST (building on the AI Risk
Management Framework), standards development organizations (HL7, IEEE), and industry, and
should be codified as an ONC certification criterion under 45 C.F.R. §170.315.
3.3 Post-Market Surveillance Requirements
The FDA's real-world performance monitoring framework for AI/ML-based SaMD represents a
sophisticated and practical model for post-market surveillance. HHS should develop an
analogous framework for non-device clinical AI that requires health systems and developers to
monitor and report on:
● Model drift: changes in AI tool performance over time as patient populations, clinical
practices, or data inputs change.

● Disparate impact monitoring: ongoing tracking of whether performance gaps across
demographic subgroups are emerging, widening, or narrowing post-deployment.
● Adverse event reporting: a mechanism analogous to MedWatch by which clinicians and
patients can report suspected AI-related clinical errors.
This surveillance infrastructure should be administered through a multi-agency accountability
structure that reflects the actual reach of federal authority over the AI developer community.
ASTP/ONC is a logical administrative home for surveillance reporting — building on existing
infrastructure such as the Health IT Feedback and Inquiry Portal — and reporting obligations
should be linked to any standing an HIT developer holds under ONC's certification program.
However, ONC's reach is limited to certified HIT developers, and certification itself is voluntary
except where it conditions provider participation in Promoting Interoperability programs. Pure AI
developers and direct-to-consumer health IT vendors have little practical motivation to seek
ONC certification and therefore fall outside that accountability structure. To achieve the reach
necessary for meaningful post-market surveillance across the full AI developer community, CMS
should establish parallel reporting obligations tied to participation in CMS's Health IT Ecosystem
as a recognized vendor or developer — conditions that carry direct market consequences for a
broader class of AI developers than ONC certification alone. Additionally, HHS should engage
OIG as an enforcement backstop, recognizing that OIG holds investigative and enforcement
authority over certified HIT developers in matters involving Information Blocking and fraud, and
that this authority could extend meaningfully to material misrepresentations in AI performance
disclosures. No single agency has sufficient reach to cover the full landscape; a coordinated
ONC-CMS-OIG framework is necessary to ensure that surveillance obligations have genuine
teeth across the developer community.
3.4 HIPAA Modernization for Responsible AI Development
The current HIPAA Privacy Rule (45 C.F.R. Parts 160 and 164) was designed before large-
scale machine learning was foreseeable. Its application to AI training and validation creates
genuine compliance uncertainty. HHS should issue guidance clarifying:
● That training and validating AI models on de-identified data meeting the standards of 45
C.F.R. §164.514 does not constitute impermissible use or disclosure, and that a
business associate covered entity's AI governance program constitutes a legitimate
"healthcare operations" function under §164.501.
● That federated learning approaches — in which model training occurs at the site of the
data without raw data leaving institutional control — satisfy the Privacy and Security
Rule's requirements for minimum necessary and authorized use requirements when
implemented with appropriate technical security and administrative security controls.
● That patients must be meaningfully informed when AI plays a material role in their
diagnosis or treatment, consistent with the principle of informed consent, and that this
notification requirement applies regardless of whether the AI tool is FDA-regulated.
IV. Novel Legal and Implementation Issues (Question 3)

From our governance advisory practice, we identify the following as the most consequential
unresolved legal and implementation challenges for non-device clinical AI:
4.1 The Accountability Gap
The central legal problem with non-medical-device clinical AI is the accountability gap: there is
no party who is clearly responsible for ensuring the tool performs safely across the full range of
patients and clinical contexts in which it is deployed. Developers disclaim liability through
contractual terms of use. Health systems and clinicians rely on vendor performance
representations they lack the expertise to verify. Clinicians are expected to exercise
independent judgment but are rarely given the information they need to calibrate their trust in an
AI tool. This problem is made materially worse by ASTP/ONC's proposed HTI-5 rule, which
removes requirements for exactly this kind of performance and transparency information for
developers certifying to 170.315(b)(11) — apparently on the mistaken assumption that such
information is not useful to end users in their clinical workflow. Transparency documentation
about model training, validation methodology, known limitations, and demographic performance
is not primarily a point-of-care reference tool for end users; it is essential infrastructure for health
systems and clinicians as buyers and implementers of clinical AI — necessary for responsible
evaluation, procurement, governance, and ongoing oversight. Removing these requirements
from the certification framework at the moment when clinical AI deployment is accelerating most
rapidly compounds the accountability gap rather than addressing it, and HHS should treat the
HTI-5 proposal on this point as a cautionary example of how regulatory action can inadvertently
erode the governance foundation that responsible AI adoption requires.
HHS should take the following steps to close this gap:
● Establish, through CMS Conditions of Participation (42 C.F.R. Parts 482 and 485), that
accredited hospitals and health systems bear primary accountability for ensuring that AI
tools deployed in clinical care have been appropriately evaluated, monitored, and
documented. This places responsibility with the deploying institution — which has the
most context about its patient population — rather than exclusively with developers.
● Clarify, through OIG guidance modeled on existing safe harbor provisions at 42 C.F.R.
§1001.952, that health systems that deploy AI tools in compliance with HHS-established
governance standards have an affirmative defense in regulatory proceedings arising
from AI-related adverse events. This creates a compliance incentive without eliminating
accountability.
● Require AI developers as part of condition of use in any health systems, hospitals,
physicians, or clinicians funded by or in receipt of federal funds related to the provision
of designated health services as defined by 42 CFR 411.351 to provide contractual
representations regarding model performance, update notification, and post-deployment
support, with HHS enforcement authority for material misrepresentations.
● Direct ASTP/ONC to retain and strengthen — rather than reduce — the transparency
and risk management documentation requirements currently embedded in the
certification program under 170.315(b)(11) for predictive decision support interventions.
As discussed above, the proposed HTI-5 rule would eliminate requirements that are

foundational to responsible AI procurement, evaluation, and governance by health
systems and clinicians. These requirements should be preserved as a floor, not
removed, and should be treated as a governance asset of the certification program
rather than an administrative burden on developers.
○ HHS should further require that any HIT or AI developer seeking recognition as a
participating vendor or developer within CMS's Health IT Ecosystem meet
equivalent transparency and risk management documentation standards as a
condition of that participation — ensuring that developers who have not pursued
or do not hold ONC certification are nonetheless subject to comparable
disclosure obligations when they seek access to the federal market through
CMS-recognized channels.
4.2 Informed Consent and Patient Notification
There is currently no federal requirement that patients be informed when AI plays a material role
in their clinical care. This is a meaningful gap: patients have a right to understand how decisions
about their care are made, and that right is not contingent on whether a tool is FDA-regulated.
HHS should work with OCR to develop a practical patient notification standard — not a consent
form, but a clear notification obligation — that applies to Tier 1 and Tier 2 AI tools.
A distinct but related issue arises with patient and consumer-facing AI tools, where the patient
or consumer is the direct user rather than the subject of an AI-assisted provider decision. In
these contexts, disclosure obligations run directly to the individual using the tool, and must
address not only that AI is involved but the nature of its limitations, the absence of a clinical
relationship, and the appropriate role of the tool's output relative to professional medical advice.
HHS should clarify that notification standards apply with equal force to consumer-facing tools
offered through CMS's Health IT ecosystem, including those offered by providers to patients
and those offered directly to consumers through app marketplaces or digital health platforms.
4.3 Civil Rights and Algorithmic Discrimination
Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act (42 U.S.C. §18116) prohibits discrimination in HHS-
funded health programs on the basis of race, color, national origin, sex, age, and disability. AI
tools that systematically underperforms for protected groups — due to non-representative
training data, proxy variables that correlate with protected characteristics, or differential
deployment across patient populations — may constitute discriminatory practices under this
statute.
The HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) should issue enforcement guidance clarifying that health
systems have a proactive obligation to evaluate AI tools for disparate impact on protected
groups before deployment and on an ongoing basis. This is not a new legal standard — it is an
application of existing law to a new technology context. Clear guidance would provide health
systems with the framework to comply and would create meaningful accountability for
discriminatory AI deployment.

Governance Principle: Accountability for AI in clinical care must be distributed across
the development, deployment, and monitoring lifecycle — not concentrated at any single
point. HHS governance frameworks should reflect this by placing complementary
obligations on developers, deploying health systems, and ongoing monitoring processes.
V. AI Evaluation Methods (Question 4)
Evaluation of non-device clinical AI should occur across three phases: pre-procurement, pre-
deployment, and post-deployment. HHS can play a meaningful role in supporting all three.
5.1 Pre-Procurement Evaluation
Health systems and providers, particularly smaller organizations and physician/clinician
practices, lack standardized tools for evaluating AI vendor claims. HHS should support the
development of:
● A publicly available AI procurement checklist and evaluation rubric, developed by
ASTP/ONC in collaboration with CMS under their Health Tech Ecosystem and health
system associations, that provides health systems with a minimum standard of due
diligence to apply before contracting with AI vendors.
● A national AI tool registry (modeled on ClinicalTrials.gov and administered by NIH or
ASTP/ONC) where developers register clinical AI tools, submit transparency
documentation, and post validation evidence. This allows health systems, researchers,
and payers to compare tools on a common informational basis.
5.2 Pre-Deployment Validation
Before deploying a clinical AI tool in a live clinical environment, providers should conduct local
validation on a representative sample of their own patient population. HHS should:
● Develop and publish standardized validation protocols for common clinical AI use cases
(sepsis prediction, imaging analysis, medication management, etc.) that providers can
apply without needing to design evaluations from scratch.
● Require, as a condition of use in CMS-funded programs, that Tier 1 AI tools undergo
prospective shadow mode validation — in which the AI generates recommendations that
are tracked but not acted upon — for a defined period before live deployment, with pre-
specified performance thresholds as go/no-go criteria.
● Fund, through AHRQ grants, independent evaluation support for FQHCs, rural health
clinics and independent clinician groups, and safety-net providers that lack internal
validation capacity.
5.3 Post-Deployment Monitoring
Post-deployment monitoring is the most underinvested phase of AI governance. HHS should
encourage that providers deploying Tier 1 and Tier 2 AI tools maintain and periodically review:

● Model performance dashboards tracking accuracy, alert acceptance rates, clinician
override rates, and demographic performance disaggregation on an ongoing basis.
● Structured incident reporting processes for suspected AI-related clinical errors, with a
defined escalation pathway to the AI developer and, for serious adverse events, to HHS.
This reporting regime should be modeled after the Patient Safety Organization (PSO)
program established under the Patient Safety and Quality Improvement Act of 2005 and
administered by AHRQ, which provides a proven federal framework for the voluntary
collection and analysis of patient safety event data reported by healthcare providers,
with legal protections for reported information that encourage candid disclosure. Under
that model, AHRQ certifies and lists PSOs that receive provider-reported safety data,
aggregate it through standardized Common Formats, and contribute non-identifiable
data to the Network of Patient Safety Databases (NPSD) for systemic learning. HHS
should direct AHRQ to extend the PSO program's Common Formats and reporting
infrastructure to expressly capture AI-related adverse events and near-misses as a
discrete event category, enabling health systems to report suspected AI-related clinical
errors through existing PSO relationships with the same legal protections that apply to
other patient safety work product — while ensuring that serious adverse events trigger a
defined escalation pathway to the AI developer and to HHS for regulatory follow-up. This
approach leverages existing infrastructure rather than creating a parallel reporting
bureaucracy, and it connects AI safety surveillance to the broader patient safety learning
system in a way that can generate actionable evidence at scale.
● Annual re-validation against updated local data to detect model drift.
VI. Patient Safety and Equity (Questions 3, 9)
We want to be direct: AI governance that does not explicitly center on patient safety and health
equity is incomplete governance. The populations most likely to be harmed by poorly governed
AI - patients from racial and ethnic minority communities, patients with disabilities, patients with
limited English proficiency, patients in underserved rural and urban settings — are also the
populations least likely to have advocates within health systems' AI procurement and
governance processes.
6.1 Mandatory Equity Evaluation as a Governance Standard
Patient and consumer-facing AI tools present a compounded equity risk. These tools are
increasingly marketed to and used by patients managing chronic conditions, navigating care
access, or seeking health information — populations that disproportionately include individuals
with limited health literacy, limited English proficiency, and limited access to professional clinical
guidance to contextualize AI-generated recommendations. HHS equity standards should
explicitly apply to direct-to-consumer clinical AI and to tools offered by providers through patient-
facing channels, with particular attention to whether these tools have been validated for the
literacy levels, languages, and access contexts of the populations likely to use them.

HHS should establish, as a federal standard applicable to all AI tools used in Tier 1 and Tier 2
clinical decisions in healthcare providers in receipt of federal funds for provision of designated
health services" that are overseen by the FDA as SaMD, by ASTP/ONC as certified products to
170.315(b)(11), or as are provided by AI developers participating in CMS's Health Tech
Ecosystem, a mandatory equity evaluation requirement that includes:
● Pre-deployment demographic performance disaggregation: vendors must provide
performance data broken down by race, ethnicity, sex, age, primary language, and
insurance status. Tools that cannot provide this data should not be deployed in federally
funded settings until they can.
● Local equity audit: health systems must evaluate whether AI tool performance in their
own patient population reflects the performance claimed by the vendor, with particular
attention to any disparities for demographic subgroups overrepresented in their patient
panels.
● Ongoing disparate impact monitoring: post-deployment monitoring must include
demographic disaggregation, with a defined threshold for performance disparity that
triggers mandatory review and corrective action.
This is not a burdensome standard — it is a baseline that any responsible developer should
already be meeting. Making it mandatory ensures that the market cannot compete on the basis
of ignoring equity.
6.2 Structural Inclusion of Patient and Community Voices
HHS AI governance processes — both at the federal policy level and in the local governance
frameworks that health systems establish — currently lack meaningful patient and community
representation. This is a structural deficit that leads to governance frameworks designed
primarily by and for the interests of developers and deploying institutions.
We recommend:
● HHS establish a permanent Patient and Caregiver AI Advisory Council with a formal
advisory role to ASTP/ONC, FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence, and CMS's
Innovation Center. Council membership should reflect the diversity of the patient
population, with particular representation for communities historically underserved by
health care.
● ASTP/ONC incorporate community representation requirements into the AI governance
standards it develops for certified health systems — analogous to community benefit
requirements for nonprofit hospitals — recognizing that communities affected by AI
deployment have a legitimate interest in how those tools are governed. These
community representation requirements should extend beyond the health systems that
deploy AI tools to the developers who create them. HHS should require that any AI
developer seeking or maintaining recognition as a participating vendor or developer
within CMS's Health IT Ecosystem demonstrate meaningful engagement with the

communities their tools are designed to serve — including communities defined by race,
ethnicity, primary language, disability status, and geography — as a condition of that
participation. This is particularly important for developers whose tools are deployed in
safety-net, rural, or underserved settings, where the gap between the populations
represented in model development and the populations affected by model deployment is
most likely to be consequential. Community engagement in this context does not require
a prescribed structure, but should at minimum include documented processes for
incorporating community input into tool design, validation, and ongoing governance —
with HHS establishing minimum standards for what constitutes meaningful engagement
as distinct from nominal compliance.
6.3 Equitable Access to AI Governance Capacity
The equity problem in clinical AI is not only about whether AI tools perform equitably for diverse
patients. It is also about whether the organizations that serve those patients have the capacity
to govern AI responsibly. Safety-net providers, FQHCs, rural health clinics, and Critical Access
Hospitals disproportionately serve vulnerable populations and are disproportionately under-
resourced for AI governance.
HHS should address this through:
● Dedicated AHRQ and HRSA funding for AI governance capacity-building at FQHCs,
rural health clinics, and safety-net providers — including training, shared evaluation
resources, and access to standardized procurement tools.
● A shared services model, potentially administered through regional health information
exchanges (HIEs) or ASTP/ONC's Trusted Exchange Framework and Common
Agreement (TEFCA) infrastructure, through which smaller organizations can access AI
governance and validation support without bearing the full cost independently.
● CMS reimbursement consideration for AI governance activities — including model
validation, equity auditing, and post-deployment monitoring — as legitimate quality
improvement expenditures under existing value-based care frameworks.
VII. Interoperability for Governance (Question 8)
From a governance perspective, interoperability is not only a technical enabler for AI — it is also
the foundation for meaningful AI accountability. Tools that operate on fragmented, inaccessible,
or non-standardized data are impossible to audit, validate, or monitor effectively.
HHS should prioritize the following interoperability investments to support AI governance:
● Standardized FHIR-based AI model cards: HL7 should develop and ONC should adopt
as federally recognized interoperability standards FHIR implementation guides for AI
model documentation — standardized, machine-readable disclosures of model training
data, validation evidence, performance metrics, and intended use — that can be
embedded in health system AI registries and queried by governance tools.

● Demographic data standardization: The ability to conduct meaningful equity audits
depends on the availability of standardized, structured race, ethnicity, language, and
disability data in EHR systems. HHS should strengthen requirements for collection and
exchange of this data under ONC certification criteria and ensure USCDI includes SDOH
and demographic variables sufficient for equity auditing.
● Federated audit infrastructure: ASTP/ONC, AHRQ and CMS should invest in the
development of federated audit tools that allow regulators, researchers, and
accreditation bodies to evaluate AI tool performance across health systems without
requiring raw patient data to leave institutional control. This is technically feasible and
would dramatically improve post-market surveillance capacity.
VIII. Supporting Private Sector Governance Activities
(Question 5)
Private sector governance activities - including accreditation, certification, and industry-driven
standards - have an important role to play, but HHS should be clear-eyed about their limitations.
Voluntary industry standards, unmoored from regulatory requirements, tend toward the least-
common-denominator. For high-risk clinical AI, self-regulation is insufficient.
We recommend a complementary model:
● HHS-anchored minimum standards: ASTP/ONC should establish minimum governance
standards for Tier 1 and Tier 2 clinical AI as certification criteria under 45 C.F.R.
§170.315. These represent the regulatory floor.
● Accreditation as a market signal above the floor: HHS should recognize and support
independent accreditation programs — from bodies such as the Joint Commission,
NCQA, or a new AI-specific accreditation body — that evaluate health systems' AI
governance practices against standards above the regulatory minimum. Accreditation
status should be visible in CMS quality reporting and usable by payers in network
contracting decisions. Beyond its role as a market signal, HHS should give serious
consideration to making demonstrated AI governance accreditation a Condition of
Participation (CoP) under 42 C.F.R. Parts 482 and 485 for hospitals and health systems
that deploy Tier 1 or Tier 2 clinical AI tools in their care delivery operations. Establishing
accreditation or equivalent governance standards as a CoP would transform AI
governance from a voluntary best practice into a baseline accountability requirement for
participation in Medicare and Medicaid — giving it the same structural weight that
existing CoPs carry for infection control, patient rights, and quality assurance. HHS
should initiate rulemaking to explore this pathway, with particular attention to how
accreditation standards could be designed to be achievable by smaller and safety-net
health systems without creating compliance burdens that effectively disadvantage the
organizations that serve the most vulnerable patient populations. In the interim, prior to
any CoP rulemaking, accreditation status should function as a meaningful market signal

through its visibility in CMS quality reporting and its usability by payers in network
contracting and value-based care arrangement decisions.
● Developer certification programs: ASTP/ONC or a designated third party should operate
a voluntary AI developer certification program that evaluates clinical AI tools against
published transparency and equity standards. Certification should not be required as a
standalone matter but should confer meaningful and concrete market obligations and
advantages. Specifically, certification — or demonstrated compliance with equivalent
transparency and equity standards — should be established as a condition of
participation in CMS's Health IT Ecosystem as a recognized AI vendor or developer,
ensuring that the federal market is not accessible to developers who have not met
minimum governance standards. Beyond ecosystem participation, certification should be
tied to the use of health information technology in CMS reimbursement pathways that
already carry HIT requirements — including CMS Innovation Center models that require
the use of Certified Electronic Health Record Technology (CEHRT) as a condition of
participation, and CMS Advanced Alternative Payment Models (Advanced APMs) under
the Quality Payment Program — such that AI tools used by providers operating under
these models must meet the same certification or equivalent standards as a condition of
their use in those payment arrangements. This approach gives certification genuine
regulatory consequence rather than treating it as a purely voluntary market signal. This
certification program should explicitly extend to developers of patient and consumer-
facing clinical AI tools, including those distributed through app marketplaces or offered
directly to consumers, recognizing that these developers are a material part of CMS's
Health IT Ecosystem and that the absence of a clinical intermediary makes governance
standards more — not less — important for protecting consumers. Preferred status in
federal procurement should apply to certified developers across all of these categories.
IX. Research and Development Priorities (Question 10)
We recommend HHS prioritize the following research investments through NIH, AHRQ, and
ASTP/ONC:
● Governance science: Rigorous research on what AI governance frameworks and
practices actually work — what evaluation methods predict real-world safety, which
organizational governance structures improve outcomes, and how to design
accountability mechanisms that do not inadvertently impede beneficial adoption. This
field is nascent, and federal investment would have high leverage.
● Equity research: Studies specifically examining AI performance disparities across
demographic subgroups, the mechanisms by which bias enters clinical AI systems, and
the effectiveness of bias mitigation techniques in real-world clinical settings. NIH should
create a dedicated funding mechanism for clinical AI equity research.
● Implementation science: Research on the organizational, workforce, and cultural factors
that determine whether clinically effective and responsibly governed AI is actually
adopted and used appropriately in practice — with particular focus on community health
settings.

● Longitudinal outcomes research: Long-term studies evaluating the population health
effects of widespread clinical AI adoption, including effects on health equity, total cost of
care, and the patient-clinician relationship. Without this evidence base, the field cannot
make credible claims about AI's societal value.
X. Conclusion
AI has genuine and significant potential to improve clinical care. Realizing that potential requires
governance frameworks that are rigorous enough to protect patients — particularly the most
vulnerable — while predictable enough to support responsible investment and innovation.
The United States does not currently have such a framework. The choice facing HHS is not
between acceleration and safety: it is between building the governance infrastructure now, in a
deliberate and coherent way, or encountering it reactively, after patient harm has eroded public
trust in ways that would be far more damaging to AI adoption in the long run.
We urge HHS to invest in the governance foundation that durable AI adoption requires: a
unified, tiered oversight framework; mandatory transparency and equity standards; meaningful
patient voice; and post-market surveillance with teeth. These are not obstacles to innovation.
They are the conditions under which innovation can earn and sustain public trust.
