Medical Information Technology, Inc. (MEDITECH) is pleased to offer a response to
the Department of Health and Human Services’ (HHS) Request for Information on
accelerating the adoption and use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) as part of clinical care.
MEDITECH empowers healthcare organizations to expand their vision of what’s
possible with Expanse, the intelligent EHR platform. Around the globe, Expanse
transforms care and ushers organizations into the future with AI-infused solutions,
personalized workflows, next-level interoperability, and predictive analytics — all
working together to drive better outcomes.
MEDITECH strongly supports the HHS initiative to accelerate AI innovation and
adoption in clinical care. We recognize its potential to enhance efficiency, reduce
clinician burden, and improve outcomes while maintaining patient safety and data
privacy. As a leading EHR vendor, we believe a balanced federal approach is
crucial—one that provides clear, risk-based guidance while minimizing undue
regulatory burdens that stifle innovation. We advocate for leveraging existing EHR
infrastructure as a primary vehicle for safe and effective AI deployment.
MEDITECH is aligned with and supports responses being submitted by the HIMSS
Electronic Health Record (EHR) Association. We have incorporated some of the EHR
Association’s statements directly within our response with an attributable reference.
Below are our responses to the specific questions outlined in the RFI.
Barriers to Private Sector Innovation
What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and
its adoption and use in clinical care?
Response:
1

As a leading EHR vendor, we believe that the private sector's ability to innovate with
AI is primarily hindered by several intersecting issues:
1) Regulatory Uncertainty and Inconsistency: The lack of a consistent, shared
framework for risk management and transparency has led to a patchwork of
state legislation. This forces vendors to focus on complying with multiple,
diverse requirements and drains resources from actual innovation.
2) Lack of Risk-Tiered Approach: Effective risk management is vital for safe AI
development and establishing a consistent industry understanding of residual
risk as AI technology advances. However, risk levels are not universal across all
use cases. A tendency toward a uniform assessment of risk imposes a
disproportionate compliance burden on low-risk applications and diverts
critical resources and focus away from risk mitigation and testing needed by
more novel, complex use cases.
3) Federal Regulation Redundancy: Vendors are potentially subject to
overlapping oversight from both ASTP/ONC and the FDA. This regulatory
redundancy, often termed "double regulation," means software must adhere
to both the ASTP/ONC Health IT Certification standards and FDA Medical
Device standards. This compliance burden slows speed-to-market.
4) Access to Data: Innovation requires access to large, diverse datasets to
develop, train, and test models. Currently, the complexity of aggregating
real-world data across multiple systems creates challenges with developing
fully generalizable AI models.
Regulatory, Policy, and Programmatic Changes
What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS
regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to
develop or use AI in clinical care? Please provide specific changes and applicable
Code of Federal Regulations citations.
Response:
MEDITECH encourages HHS to prioritize the following changes:
1) Establish a Federal Framework: Develop a unified, federally led framework
for healthcare AI that aligns with international standards.
● Address State Patchwork: Conflicting and duplicative state frameworks
have emerged through a lack of federal guidance. If a vendor meets
stringent federal transparency requirements (e.g., ONC HTI-1), this should
satisfy state-level requirements.
2

● Ensure Global Competitiveness: Many developers operate in both the U.S.
and international markets. Adopting federal standards that harmonize with
global frameworks (e.g., ISO/IEC 42001:2023 and ISO/IEC 23894:2023) will
position US developers for success and growth in the global market.
2) Harmonize ONC and FDA Oversight: Regulatory redundancy stifles
innovation. We encourage HHS to harmonize ONC Decision Support
Intervention Transparency with the FDA Pre-market Review through the
following actions:
● Transparency Reciprocity: Compliance with the streamlined ONC
Decision Support Intervention (DSI) criteria should satisfy specific FDA
pre-market transparency requirements for low-to-moderate risk software.
● “Developer-based” Reviews: Consider FDA’s transition from
"product-based" reviews to "developer-based" excellence appraisals for
low-risk EHR modules. If an EHR vendor has a documented track record of
safe development, their integrated AI tools should qualify for expedited
clearance.
● Expanded Enforcement Discretion: Consider broadening enforcement
discretion for AI tools that optimize workflows (e.g., auto-scribing,
administrative triage, scheduling) without providing definitive diagnoses.
This aligns with the deregulatory goals of HTI-5 by exempting modules that
already satisfy ASTP/ONC’s Real World Testing requirements.
3) Adopt a Risk-tiered Approach: A risk-tiered framework aligns regulatory
requirements with use-case risk, enabling developers to allocate resources
more effectively. This approach allows innovation to begin with lower-risk
applications, while providing a clear pathway for scaling to higher-risk use
cases. For healthcare organizations, this approach also offers a practical
roadmap for adoption, starting with low-risk, high-value use cases, while
gradually building governance and monitoring capabilities.
● Categorize Use Cases: We encourage HHS to categorize AI use cases,
differentiating them as clinical, operational, or other, to serve as a
foundational step for establishing appropriate risk tiers.
● Apply Proportional Oversight: We encourage HHS to consider an
approach that ensures regulatory requirements and oversight are
proportional to potential clinical impact and safety. This allows developers
to pursue a diverse portfolio of AI use cases without facing uniform
compliance burdens.
3

4) Address Transparency: Current regulation focuses on the benefit of
information "to help guide decisions at the time and place of care" (see Health
Data, Technology, and Interoperability: Certification Program Updates, 89 FR
1192, p. 619). However, even with plain language descriptions, the volume of
“Source Attribute” information may not be useful to the end-user.
● Define Minimum Essential Information: We recommend HHS support
stakeholders in reaching a consensus on the minimum essential
information required to ensure safe and accountable use. Focusing on
essential, actionable AI documentation reduces developer burden and
costly updates while ensuring critical information for AI use is available.
5) Create Reimbursement Incentives: We encourage HHS to create specific
CPT codes or value-based payment models that reward providers for using
evidence-based AI tools that demonstrably improve efficiency, reduce costs,
and improve outcomes.
Novel Legal and Implementation Issues
For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise
novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and
accountability structures (e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and
security). What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any,
should HHS play to help address them?
Response:
The use of AI in clinical care introduces liability and maintenance challenges that
current frameworks do not address.
1) Establish a Liability Framework: Liability is a complex legal issue when
AI-generated insights inform clinical decision-making.
● Duty of Care: MEDITECH is in alignment with the EHR Association’s
statements regarding the need for a clearer definition of accountability for
AI tools that are not regulated by FDA as medical devices: “A central issue
is the allocation of liability among developers, implementers, and clinicians
when AI-generated recommendations inform care decisions. Because
these tools usually operate in advisory or assistive roles and do not
constitute regulated medical devices, the boundaries of responsibility can
be ambiguous. To support safe deployment and reduce legal uncertainty,
the industry would benefit from model ‘duty of care’ frameworks and
safe-harbor practices for documenting AI-informed clinical decisions,
recognizing that the ultimate responsibility for patient care ultimately rests
with the authorizing clinician. Such structures would help ensure that
accountability aligns with real-world roles and capabilities while preserving
clinicians’ independent judgment.”
4

● Clarify Third-Party Liability: Clearer rules are needed to address liability
when an AI intervention relies on a third-party model that degrades due to
unforeseen circumstances beyond the vendor's control.
2) Leverage Transparency for Legal Clarity: We encourage HHS to utilize the
modifications proposed by HTI-5 to reorient focus toward useful transparency
rather than exhaustive documentation.
● Presumption of Diligence: HHS should consider establishing a
presumption that clinicians employing AI tools that satisfy the Decision
Support Intervention (DSI) criteria–specifically, the transparency
requirements outlined in 45 CFR 170.315(b)(11)–have adhered to the
"standard of care."
● Empower Clinician Review: Prioritizing the usability of transparency
information over volume supports a clinician’s ability to “independently
review and understand the basis for recommendations,” which constitutes
the statutory prerequisite for a tool to maintain its designation as a
non-device under Section 520(o) of the Cures Act.
● Privacy and Security Requirements: There is uncertainty around
interpretations of HIPAA privacy and security requirements when AI
models are trained or updated using clinical data. HHS can play a
constructive role by clarifying expectations for AI transparency and
auditability, and aligning privacy guidance with real-world AI development
and deployment practices.
3) Ongoing Maintenance: AI models require ongoing monitoring and
maintenance to detect degraded performance caused by underlying changes
in input data (known as “data drift”). This introduces a new, resource-intensive
burden that creates disparity between organizations with different levels of
resources. To address this, MEDITECH recommends the following:
● Standards Version Advancement Process: HHS should consider
adopting the proposed HTI-5 deregulatory approach to Real World Testing
for non-device AI. Rather than mandating prescriptive, annual testing
plans, HHS could authorize a "Standards Version Advancement Process"
(SVAP) methodology. HHS could permit developers of EHR-integrated AI to
voluntarily adopt more contemporary interoperability standards (e.g.,
FHIR-based APIs) to monitor model robustness without awaiting formal
federal regulatory updates.
● Support Equitable Capacity: We encourage HHS to consider using grant
programs and offering technical assistance to support under-resourced
healthcare organizations. Funding should focus on enabling them to
adopt, maintain, and evaluate AI tools. This support could also facilitate the
publication of more diverse, formal evaluations of AI's impact on varying
specific workflows and patient populations.
5

4) National Framework for Workforce Proficiency: AI implementation is
ineffective without a proficient workforce to deploy and maintain it. Without
appropriate training, AI tools may be underutilized, misapplied, or deployed in
ways that fail to achieve maximal impact. This capability includes being able to
clearly explain AI applications, potential risks, and necessary precautions to
patients. Investments in clinical workforce AI training and literacy at a national
level are essential for fostering more equitable integration of AI across the
healthcare landscape. MEDITECH encourages HHS to help set standards for
baseline AI competencies and enable national workgroups to support the
creation of a common set of best practices.
5) Clarify the Legal Status of Agentic: The legal status of "agentic" or
autonomous AI systems within EHRs remains ambiguous. HHS should finalize
the HTI-5 proposal to revise the definitions of "access" and "use" in 45 CFR
171.102 to explicitly incorporate automated and AI-driven processes. This
modification ensures that autonomous AI systems can retrieve and exchange
Electronic Health Information (EHI), thereby facilitating the adoption of
non-device AI agents for administrative and clinical support functions.
Evaluation Methods
For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods
(pre- and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and
human-centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support
these processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most impactful (e.g., contracts,
grants, cooperative agreements, and/or prize competitions)?
Response:
For successful and trusted AI adoption, solutions must seamlessly integrate into
current clinical workflows and address real-world problems. Current evaluation
approaches are fragmented, making it difficult for users and developers to
consistently assess safety, effectiveness, and usability. Greater alignment around
evaluation methods would support responsible adoption.
1) Support the Development of Standardized Metrics: HHS could play an
important convening role in encouraging clear, standardized measurement
indicators tailored to distinct categories of clinical care use cases. These
metrics should assess clinical effectiveness, safety, equity, and workflow
impact to ensure AI tools deliver meaningful value in real-world settings.
Greater consistency in evaluation methods would allow results to be more
easily interpreted across settings.
2) Prioritize User-Centered Evaluation: User-centered evaluation methods are
vital for assessing the impact of AI on factors like cognitive burden, user
6

satisfaction, and perceived time savings. Evaluating human-AI interaction can
help identify areas where AI functions best as a complement to human
expertise, and where it may inadvertently degrade human decision-making.
MEDITECH jointly published a study, User-Centered Delivery of AI-Powered
Health Care Technologies in Clinical Settings: Mixed Methods Case Study, that
describes methods for applying user-centered research frameworks across the
design and development, and post-deployment stages.1
3) Encourage Structured Feedback: Users are best positioned to identify bias or
inaccuracies in AI output and surface any issues or concerns. Methods that
enable users to assess and respond to AI outputs are essential for ongoing
performance monitoring. Structured feedback channels can surface these
insights early and support continuous improvement while reinforcing human
oversight.
Private Sector Activities
How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation,
certification, industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and
effective AI use in clinical care?
Response:
To promote the innovative and effective use of AI in healthcare, MEDITECH proposes
three key strategic recommendations:
1) Establish Workforce Training Programs: To enhance AI literacy and
competency across the healthcare industry, we recommend a coordinated
partnership between regulatory bodies, professional organizations, and the
private sector.
● Standardized, Role-Based: Programs should focus on developing
baseline, role-based workforce training programs and standardized
credentialing mechanisms. Training modules should be customized for
varying clinical and administrative roles, ensuring every member of a care
team understands concepts like data quality, bias, fairness, and validity.
● Public Funding: We encourage HHS to prioritize funding for these
programs to ensure all healthcare professionals have access to
foundational AI education. Publicly supported AI training would promote
equity, improve safety and trust, and enable organizations to better achieve
measurable outcomes.
2) Equitable Access to Accreditation/Certification: Access to voluntary
accreditation and certification processes must be kept affordable and

Enhanced Interoperability
Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research,
and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific
data types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
Response:
To accelerate AI adoption and foster research, we encourage HHS to prioritize the
following interoperability advancements:
1) Advance National Interoperability Frameworks: HHS should continue to
advance interoperability through the TEFCA framework, CMS-aligned data
exchange networks, and standards-based APIs.
● Scalable AI Platforms: Enhanced interoperability enables EHRs to
function as scalable platforms for various AI-enabled workflows. This
broadens market opportunities, particularly for AI tools driven by
longitudinal, multi-source data, such as risk predictions, population health,
and care management.
● Standardize Context: Adoption of common standards further expands
these markets. US Core and the International Patient Summary enable
consistent clinical context, while HL7 FHIR APIs support model
development and real-time AI workflows.
2) Improve Data Quality for AI Readiness: High-quality, comprehensive data is
essential for developing robust, generalizable, and bias-free AI tools.
● Support Industry Collaboratives: We encourage HHS to continue to
support industry initiatives, such as the Sequoia Project’s “Taking Root
Movement,” which enhance AI readiness by improving data quality,
metadata consistency, and patient matching, thereby reducing
downstream deduplication and model bias.
8

3) Leverage Patient-Generated Data: Strengthening interoperability around
patient access, contribution, and bidirectional exchange of patient-generated
data is critical for equity.
● Create Representative Datasets: Bidirectional exchange would enable
more representative and continuous datasets, improving AI training,
evaluation, and deployment.
● Promote Patient-Centered AI: This shift would support the development
and adoption of AI that is context-aware and effective across diverse
populations and care environments.
4) Expand Data Standards for AI Outputs and Monitoring: We encourage HHS
to continue to reinforce and expand data standards that support the use of AI
within clinical workflows and develop standards that define how AI predictions
and output are formatted in FHIR. We encourage HHS to consider ways to
help build mechanisms to test, collaborate, and evolve AI use cases. As use
cases move beyond pilot phases, standardized methods for users to share
real-world performance data will become more crucial for ongoing
monitoring, maintenance, and re-training processes.
Prioritized Research
Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the
adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
Response:
We recommend HHS prioritize AI research that addresses the growing lack of privacy
assurance, transparency, and trust associated with AI integration into clinical care. As
automated, AI-driven data processing expands, there is a high likelihood that
patients and clinicians will have limited visibility and control over how protected
health information is collected, analyzed, and shared, leading to misalignment with
HIPAA privacy expectations. This trust deficit represents a central barrier to
widespread adoption of clinical care that needs to be prioritized to enable the safe
and scalable use of AI in clinical care.
Thank you for your time and consideration. We appreciate the opportunity to share
our perspective and support HHS’ efforts to accelerate responsible AI adoption. We
look forward to continuing to work with HHS to promote equitable adoption of
innovative technologies across the healthcare landscape.
