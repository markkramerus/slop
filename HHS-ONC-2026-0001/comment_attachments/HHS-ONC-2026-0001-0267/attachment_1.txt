2. AI in Clinical Care: Value and Scope
AI-enabled CDS tools that support information retrieval, contextualize, and summarization
already play a meaningful role in clinical care by:
• Improving clinician efficiency and reducing administrative and cognitive burden,
• Supporting consistency with evidence-based practice,
• Enhancing access to up-to-date medical knowledge within clinical workflows.
Importantly, not all AI used in clinical care serves the same function or carries the same risk. AI
systems that inform clinical decision-making are distinct from systems that automate, direct,
or execute clinical decisions. Federal policy should continue to recognize and preserve this
distinction.
3. The Need for Proportionate Evidence Standards
A central issue raised by this RFI—appropriately—is how evidence should be generated and
evaluated for AI used in clinical care.
Elsevier encourages HHS to adopt proportionate, risk-based evidence expectations that align
with an AI system’s intended use and level of clinical impact. In particular:
• AI tools that retrieve, summarize, or contextualize existing medical knowledge should
not be held to the same evidentiary standards as tools that diagnose, predict, or prescribe.
• Evidence frameworks should recognize the value of real-world evidence, post-market
performance monitoring, and continuous improvement—especially for AI systems that
evolve over time. Overly rigid or one-size-fits-all evidence requirements risk slowing
adoption of beneficial tools without commensurate gains in patient safety.
For CDS tools such as ClinicalKey AI, trust is built through transparent sourcing, traceability
to underlying evidence, clinician reviewability, and clear limitations, rather than through
automation or prescriptive outputs.
4. Regulatory Clarity and CDS Boundaries
Regulatory uncertainty remains a significant barrier to responsible AI adoption in healthcare.
Elsevier supports continued efforts by FDA, ONC, and HHS to:
• Provide clear, consistent guidance on the boundary between non-device CDS and
regulated Software as a Medical Device (SaMD),

• Reinforce the importance of human oversight, explainability, and the clinician’s role as
the final decision-maker,
• Avoid regulatory approaches that inadvertently push low-risk, informational AI tools into
medical device frameworks solely due to the use of advanced AI techniques.
Clarity in this area enables healthcare organizations to adopt AI confidently, while allowing
developers to invest responsibly in safety, quality, and innovation.
5. Adoption, Trust, and Real-World Deployment
From Elsevier’s experience deploying CDS at enterprise scale, successful AI adoption depends
on more than technical performance. Key enablers include:
• Integration into existing clinical workflows and health IT ecosystems,
• Alignment with clinical governance, training, and change management,
• Clear communication of intended use, limitations, and appropriate reliance,
• Organizational trust in the provenance and independence of clinical content.
Federal policy can support adoption by reinforcing these principles and avoiding incentives that
prioritize speed or novelty over safe, clinically meaningful use.
6. Closing
Elsevier supports HHS’s goal of responsible AI adoption in clinical care and appreciates the
opportunity to contribute to this dialogue. We believe that policies grounded in risk-based
regulation, proportionate evidence standards, and respect for clinical judgment will best
enable AI to help improve care quality, clinician experience, and patient outcomes.
We welcome continued engagement with HHS, FDA, CMS, and ONC as these policies evolve.
