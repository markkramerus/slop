Barriers to Private Sector Innovation and Adoption of AI in Health Care
Regulatory and Reimbursement Barriers
Innovation in AI for health care faces obstacles due to unclear regulations and a lack of
specific safeguards for clinical decisions and patient needs. This uncertainty hinders
growth, investment, and commercialization, as concerns persist in meeting proper patient
care standards.
The clinical landscape of wound management is uniquely vulnerable, functioning in an
environment critically devoid of standardized specialization, unified clinical pathways, and
established industry guardrails. This baseline instability is further compounded by a deeply
fragmented reimbursement system that varies drastically by state, care setting, and payer
type. Consequently, clinicians are forced to navigate severe logistical obstacles, resulting
in profoundly broken care transitions and compromised patient outcomes. It is into this
already fractured ecosystem that Artificial Intelligence (AI) is now being rapidly introduced.
While AI holds immense potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy, deploying these
powerful tools without a rigorous, mandated oversight framework will only amplify
systemic uncertainty. Practitioners are already struggling to advocate for evidence-based
care within the current chaotic climate; without decisive regulatory intervention, the
unguided integration of AI threatens to multiply clinical risks and introduce unprecedented
liability into the market
The current use of AI in reimbursement processes, including prior authorization, tends to
apply uniform approaches to areas such as wound care. Wound care requirements are
highly individualized, depending on factors like a patient's medical history, comorbidities,
and social determinants of health—information which is typically not included with claim
submissions. This omission can result in service denials. Such outcomes may negatively
impact patients, potentially leading to increased hospitalization, amputations, or mortality.
Workflow, Liability, and Clinician Adoption
Clinicians have raised concerns about medico-legal risks, especially when AI-generated
recommendations or claim reviews conflict with clinical judgment. Effective governance
frameworks are needed to address these scenarios. In November 2023, a claim alleged
UnitedHealth improperly denied Medicare Advantage patients’ necessary care by relying on
an AI model with a 90% error rate, overriding physician decisions.1 These denials affected
Medicare beneficiaries, many of whom have chronic wounds.

With AI already integrated into healthcare, the focus shifts from adoption to the need for
clear guidance on specific platforms, as well as assurance regarding clinical input and the
capability for clinical override.
Regulatory, Payment Policy, and Programmatic Recommendations for HHS to
Incentivize Effective AI Use in Clinical Care
1. Establish Clear Medicare Payment Pathways for AI Wound Services
PAWSIC strongly believes the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) should
develop clearly defined coverage and reimbursement mechanisms for algorithm-based
wound care services. This initiative would involve integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into
current quality and alternative payment programs, as well as updating data and
information technology regulations to support the secure use and sharing of wound images
and outcome data for the development and deployment of AI models. It is important to
note that PAWSIC sees the importance of clinical decision support and input from
practitioners with capability to override what an AI model may see as the best course of
care. Due to the uniqueness of wound care, specific to the patient, all AI technologies and
models should allow for clinical interference.
2. Expand AI Payment Models for Home Health and SNF Wound Care
Under 42 CFR Part 484 (home health) and Parts 413/409 (SNF consolidated billing and
PPS), PAWSIC recommends that HHS formally clarify the eligibility of AI-enabled wound
assessment and remote monitoring as reimbursable expenses. Where appropriate, these
services should be recognized as distinct categories for payment, contingent on
documented reductions in hospitalizations and amputations.
Given that chronic wound management frequently takes place within home health and
SNFs, the explicit inclusion of AI-enabled wound care as allowable costs or value-based
payment enhancements may significantly improve adoption in populations at elevated
risk.
3. Integration of AI into Quality Programs and Models for Wound Care
AI-assisted wound care should be incorporated into value-based healthcare programs. In
accordance with 42 CFR Part 414, Subpart O (Merit-based Incentive Payment System) and
Part 425 (Shared Savings Program), it is recommended that the Department of Health and
Human Services revise quality measures to specifically acknowledge validated AI tools
which demonstrate improvements in chronic wound healing rates, amputation rates, and
infection-related hospitalizations.

For populations with a high incidence of wounds, such as individuals with diabetes or
peripheral artery disease, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services could develop “AI-
enabled wound care” improvement activities within MIPS and ACO quality frameworks.
This approach would grant recognition to clinicians utilizing certified AI tools that meet
established standards for performance, equity, and governance. Aligning AI utilization with
payment adjustments serves to incentivize the effective application of AI, rather than
treating its adoption as an unfunded requirement.
Under 42 CFR Part 510 and demonstration waivers permitted by Section 1115A (CMMI
models), CMS could pilot AI-enabled chronic wound bundles, integrating AI-supported
assessment, triage, and remote monitoring as essential components within standardized
care pathways. These models may provide explicit funding for AI licenses and
infrastructure as part of care redesign expenses, with shared savings or bonuses tied to
outcomes such as improved wound healing, reduced sepsis/readmissions, and increased
limb salvage rates. The Center for Medicare & Medicaid Innovation has already
implemented models aligning payments with technology-enhanced care, making wound
care a suitable area given its measurable outcomes and significant downstream costs.
4. Clarification of Regulatory Treatment of AI in Wound Care
The Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) should establish clear guidelines
regarding the regulatory treatment of clinical AI and non-device AI within wound care.
Utilizing guidance from the Office of Inspector General (OIG) and Centers for Medicare &
Medicaid Services (CMS), as referenced in 42 CFR Parts 1001–1003 concerning program
integrity, alongside cross-references to FDA regulations pertaining to medical devices and
Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), HHS is encouraged to issue targeted, AI-specific
guidance. This guidance should address criteria delineating when wound AI qualifies as a
regulated device versus a non-device clinical decision support system, outline
requirements for performance monitoring, and specify documentation standards for
medical records. Most importantly, there needs to be guidelines and barriers associated
with AI decision making without clinical oversight from a trained practitioner.
In accordance with 45 CFR Part 92 (Section 1557, nondiscrimination) and HIPAA
regulations (45 CFR Parts 160 and 164), HHS should release guidance on evaluating wound
AI applications for potential bias related to skin tones, comorbidities, and environments, as
well as best practices for maintaining documentation to ensure compliance. The
establishment of clear and consistent expectations will promote thorough evaluation while
supporting the responsible integration of advanced solutions.

Priority Areas for HHS-Funded AI Research in Wound Care
To accelerate the adoption of AI as part of clinical care, PAWSIC sees several specific
research priorities that HHS should consider, particularly within the context of wound care.
These priorities emphasize establishing real-world clinical value, promoting equity and
usability, and clarifying the impact of AI on cost and workflow outcomes, especially
regarding chronic and post-surgical wounds.
1. Real-World Effectiveness in Chronic Wounds
HHS should support prospective, multi-site trials of AI-enabled digital wound care
solutions across various care settings such as home health, skilled nursing facilities (SNF),
long term care facilities, and outpatient wound centers. These studies should
comprehensively measure outcomes including accuracy of wound type diagnosis, time to
heal, number of amputations, hospitalizations, and clinician time, focusing on wound
types like diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), venous leg ulcers (VLU), pressure injuries, arterial
ulcers, atypical ulcers, and surgical sites.
Comparative effectiveness studies are also needed to evaluate AI-supported wound care
pathways versus standard care. These investigations should assess how standardized AI
assessments influence adherence to evidence-based protocols and escalation decisions
in the management of complex wounds. This includes AI-based models associated with
prior authorization from Medicare and Medicare Advantage. We see concerns raised
surrounding denials of clinical needed services due to AI technology, creating major gaps in
care and lag times that create further wound care needs and higher expenses.
2. Imaging, Prediction, and Advanced Sensing
Priority should be given to research on robust, device-agnostic wound imaging
technologies, including 2D, 3D, multispectral, and fluorescence imaging, as well as tissue
oxygenation. AI models developed in this context should be capable of accurately
segmenting wounds, classifying tissue types, and predicting healing trajectories in diverse
care environments.
Furthermore, the development and clinical testing of “smart dressings” and sensor-
enabled bandages that leverage AI to detect early infection or clinical deterioration is
essential, particularly in high-risk settings such as surgical and diabetic foot wounds.
These technologies have the potential to trigger timely interventions and improve patient
outcomes.

3. Equity, Skin Tone, and Population Diversity
Studies should be intentionally designed to ensure that AI wound tools perform effectively
across diverse skin tones, body sites, and comorbidity profiles. Research protocols should
include stratified performance reporting and prioritizing bias mitigation as a primary
endpoint.
In addition, investigations should explore how AI-driven wound assessment and remote
monitoring impact access to care, adherence, and clinical outcomes for rural populations,
homebound individuals, and those with low digital literacy, including the role of caregivers
in these contexts.
4. Workflow, Usability, and Human Factors
Implementation science is needed to understand how patients, nurses in all
settingsinterprofessional clinicians, and wound specialists access use, and perceive AI
tools in real-world settings. Research should address optimal user interface design,
patterns of user trust and override behavior, alerting thresholds, and the balance between
automation and clinical judgment required for effective, context-aware wound care.
Additional studies should focus on the integration of AI documentation into electronic
health records (EHRs) and wound registries with the goal of reducing documentation
burden and supporting seamless team communication and handoffs across different care
settings.
5. Economic and Value-Based Outcomes
Health economic analyses are crucial to linking AI-enabled wound care programs to
episode-of-care costs, advanced therapy utilization (such as skin substitutes and negative
pressure wound therapy), and potential downstream savings associated with avoided
complications.
Research should also test the effectiveness of AI-supported wound care bundles or care
pathways within value-based care models, such as home health, accountable care
organizations (ACO), or Medicare Advantage (MA). The objective is to quantify the potential
for shared savings and provide evidence to inform the design of future CMS payment
models.
Impact of AI Tools in Clinical Wound Care
Published Findings on Clinical Outcomes
Recent studies have investigated the effects of adopting AI tools in clinical wound care
settings. A large descriptive study of a digital wound care solution implemented across 59

U.S. home health agencies, encompassing approximately 196,000 wound assessments,
reported a 38.2% reduction in average healing time. Specifically, healing times decreased
from 62.5 to 38.6 days between 2022 and 2023, with statistically significant improvements
observed across various wound types, including diabetic ulcers, pressure injuries, and
burns.2
Additionally, the same dataset revealed that more wounds were healing within three
months, along with substantial reductions in time to improvement and area reduction for
chronic wounds. These findings suggest that sustained use of the AI-enabled digital wound
care solutions is associated with faster healing and improved wound trajectories.
Measurement Accuracy and Workflow Efficiency
Implementation studies summarized by Wounds International indicate that mobile apps
utilizing deep learning–based wound measurement achieved sub-millimeter mean error
(~0.3 mm) and reduced assessment time by approximately 54%. First-attempt imaging
accuracy was around 92%, surpassing manual measurement and simplifying
documentation processes.3
