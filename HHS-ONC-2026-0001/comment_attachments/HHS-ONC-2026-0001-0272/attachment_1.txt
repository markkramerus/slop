WHO IS RESPONDING
I am a physician with 30 years of clinical practice in psychiatry and medical education, a
current AI developer and experimenter building tools to facilitate health creation and
maintenance, and President of the Institute for Salutogenesis — a non-profit
organization with a mission to empower individuals and communities to thrive by
focusing on the origins of health rather than just disease.
THE PERSPECTIVE I BRING
The Salutogenic Reframe
The RFI focuses on how AI can make current healthcare more efficient: faster diagnosis,
optimized scheduling, reduced administrative burden, streamlined billing. These
applications matter. But they accept a fundamental assumption: that healthcare's
primary purpose is managing disease.
I propose a different starting point.
Salutogenesis — the study of what creates and maintains health — asks a fundamentally
different question than pathogenesis, which studies what causes disease. This is not
semantic. It changes everything about what we ask AI to accomplish.
The current approach asks: How can AI help us diagnose and treat disease more
efficiently?
The salutogenic approach asks: How can AI help everyone get what they need to stay
healthy and flourish?
One optimizes disease processing. The other creates conditions for health. Both are
necessary. Current policy supports only the first.
Why This Matters Now

America spends nearly twice as much per capita on healthcare as peer nations, yet has
shorter lifespans, higher rates of chronic disease, a growing mental health crisis, and
widening health disparities.
This is not an efficiency problem. We are very efficient at managing disease.
This is a priority problem. We lack systematic support for health creation. AI offers
unprecedented opportunity to address both — enhance disease management and create
conditions for health — but only if policy enables it.
WHAT SALUTOGENIC AI REQUIRES
To realize AI's potential for health creation, four foundational elements must be
addressed:
1. Shared Decision-Making as Core Design Principle
Current Reality:
Most AI in healthcare serves providers and administrators. Patients are data sources
and intervention targets, not participants.
Salutogenic Requirement:
AI tools must support genuine shared decision-making — not automated
recommendations patients passively accept, but systems that help people understand
their options, articulate their values, and make informed choices aligned with their lives.
This means:
• Transparent explanation of how AI reaches recommendations
• Clear, patient-appropriate presentation of alternatives and tradeoffs
• Support for patients to question and challenge clinician and AI guidance
• Integration of patient preferences and values into decision-making
• Tools that strengthen rather than replace the patient-provider relationship
Policy Implication: Federal funding and reimbursement should require AI systems to
demonstrate meaningful patient engagement, not just clinical accuracy.
2. Access to Integrated Data Across Silos
Current Reality:
Health is determined by clinical factors, social determinants, environmental exposures,
community resources, and behavioral health — all tracked in separate, incompatible
systems.
Salutogenic Requirement:
AI cannot support whole-person health creation with fragmented data. We need
privacy-protected integration across:
• Clinical records (EHR systems)
• Social services (housing, food assistance, employment support)
• Community resources (nonprofits, local programs, support networks)

• Behavioral health (mental health and substance use treatment)
• Environmental data (housing quality, neighborhood safety, pollution exposure)
The Barrier:
As an AI developer actively building these tools, I cannot create effective health creation
systems without this integration. Current data silos make whole-person AI technically
impossible, not just difficult.
Policy Implication: HHS should establish frameworks for privacy-protected data
integration across health and social services. Without this, AI will remain limited to
narrow clinical applications.
3. Clinician Competence in Using AI Tools
Current Reality:
AI is being deployed in clinical settings where providers have minimal training in
whole-person health approaches, AI capabilities and limitations, appropriate
interpretation of AI recommendations, and patient communication about AI-influenced
decisions.
Salutogenic Requirement:
Health creation AI will be used by diverse professionals — physicians, nurses,
community health workers, care navigators, social workers — who need competence in:
• Using AI to support rather than replace clinical judgment
• Identifying when AI recommendations enhance versus undermine shared
decision-making
• Explaining AI guidance in patient-comprehensible terms
• Coordinating AI-enabled interventions across disciplines
Policy Implication: HHS should fund development of AI competency curricula for all
health professions and require AI literacy as part of continuing education.
4. Focus on Social Determinants and Community Resources
Current Reality:
Multiple commenters on this RFI have noted the importance of addressing social
determinants of health (SDOH). I strongly agree — and want to emphasize what this
actually requires. Screening for food insecurity, housing instability, transportation
barriers, and social isolation is valuable only if it connects people to resources. Most
current SDOH screening ends with documentation in the medical record. The patient
still faces the same barriers. Nothing changes.
Salutogenic Requirement:
AI tools must:
• Screen for social determinants systematically
• Automatically match people with available community resources
• Generate referrals to social services and community organizations
• Track whether connections were successful

RESPONDING TO QUESTION 6: GREATEST POTENTIAL AI TOOLS
Given this salutogenic perspective, what AI tools have the greatest potential to
improve outcomes and reduce costs?
The Answer: Integrated Whole-Person Support Systems
AI tools with greatest potential will address the whole person — integrating across
dimensions current systems treat separately:
1. Support Health Creation Alongside Disease Management
Not replacing current clinical AI, but complementing it with systems that help people
understand what creates health in their specific circumstances, connect them to
resources that promote flourishing, support prevention before disease develops, and
make healthcare comprehensible and navigable.
2. Address the Whole Person
Integrating across dimensions current systems treat separately:
• Physical health (clinical care, prevention, health behaviors)
• Mental and emotional wellbeing (stress, resilience, psychological health)
• Social determinants (housing, food, economic security, community connection)
• Environmental factors (neighborhood conditions, exposures, safety)
• Spiritual factors (promoting evidence-based practices and community connection
where desired)
3. Enable Shared Decision-Making
Tools that translate complexity into comprehensible information, present options and
tradeoffs clearly, support informed choice aligned with patient values, and strengthen
rather than supplant the provider-patient relationship.
4. Connect Clinical Care with Community Resources
Systems that identify social barriers to health, match people with available community
resources in real time, generate and track referrals, and close feedback loops that
identify gaps requiring community investment.
5. Focus on Critical Developmental Windows
Especially the first thousand days (pregnancy through age 2), when:

WHY THESE TOOLS HAVE GREATEST POTENTIAL
Making America Healthy requires creating health — not just managing disease more
efficiently. These tools operationalize that vision. The cost reductions they generate are
not the goal; they are the consequence of doing something more fundamental: building
the conditions under which people actually thrive.
Salutogenic Outcomes: What Health Creation Looks Like
The metrics that matter for health creation are not simply the absence of pathology.
They are the presence of the conditions that allow people to flourish. Drawing on Aaron
Antonovsky's foundational framework, these outcomes include:
Sense of Coherence
People's capacity to understand their situation, believe they have the resources to
manage it, and find meaning in doing so. Research consistently shows this is one of the
strongest predictors of sustained health across the lifespan. AI tools designed around
health creation should be measured in part by whether they strengthen this capacity —
not just whether they route people to clinical services.
Agency and Navigational Capacity
Not simply connecting people to resources, but building their capacity to navigate
complex systems on their own terms over time. This is a fundamentally different
outcome metric than reduced utilization. A person who understands how to advocate for
themselves, interpret clinical information, and access community support is not just
avoiding illness — they are accumulating health.
Community Health Assets
These tools identify and strengthen what already exists in communities — treating local
assets as generative resources rather than their absence as the primary problem. The
goal is a map of abundance, continuously updated, that connects people with the social
infrastructure of health that surrounds them.
Developmental Trajectories
Particularly through the First 1000 Days lens, salutogenic outcomes compound. A child
born into a family with strong coherence, social support, and navigational capacity
doesn't merely avoid adverse outcomes — they develop differently. The neurological,
relational, and emotional foundations laid in those first years shape a lifetime of health.
This is the highest-return investment in health creation available.
System Intelligence: Generating New Knowledge

These tools do not merely deliver existing knowledge more efficiently. They generate
new knowledge — about how social determinants, community assets, and clinical care
interact in specific populations; about which interventions actually build coherence and
capacity rather than just managing symptoms; about where systematic gaps in
community resources require investment.
This adaptive capacity — a learning system that improves its understanding of what
creates health — represents a qualitatively different contribution than optimization of
existing workflows.
Disease Burden Reduction as Consequence
When health creation succeeds, reductions in disease burden follow. Emergency
department visits for conditions manageable in community settings decline — not
because we redirected people, but because those conditions didn't develop. Hospital
readmissions driven by social barriers fall — not because we managed transitions better,
but because the underlying barriers were addressed. Chronic conditions are prevented
or substantially delayed — not through screening and surveillance, but through the
early, sustained support that allows people to maintain health.
These are not marginal efficiency gains. They represent a fundamental reduction in
disease burden — durable, compounding, and equitable in a way that disease
management optimization can never be.
CRITICAL BARRIERS FROM DEVELOPMENT EXPERIENCE
As someone actively building these tools, I face specific barriers that have clear policy
solutions:
Data Silos: Cannot integrate across clinical, social service, community resource, and
behavioral health systems.
Regulatory Uncertainty: Unclear whether health creation tools are medical devices,
wellness products, or something requiring new classification.
No Reimbursement Pathway: Current payment models reimburse disease
management, not health creation. No viable path to sustainability for tools that prevent
the conditions reimbursement depends on.
Lack of Standards: No national standards for community resource data,
clinical-community referral protocols, or outcome measurement for social interventions.
Each barrier has a policy solution. What is required is recognition that health creation
AI faces different obstacles than disease management AI — and deserves deliberate
support.

CONCLUSION
AI in healthcare stands at a choice point.
One path accelerates adoption of tools that make current disease management more
efficient. The other enables tools that create health alongside managing disease. The
first path is well-supported by current policy. The second requires deliberate
intervention.
As clinician, experimenter, developer, and salutogenesis advocate, I am actively building
tools for the second path. I know they can work technically, and I know they can
contribute meaningfully to the health and wellbeing of Americans.
The perspective I bring — grounded in clinical reality, informed by development
experience, and guided by salutogenic principles — suggests that AI's greatest potential
lies not in processing disease-related information faster, but in creating health more
effectively.
This aligns with HHS's mission, serves MAHA's vision, and addresses what
patients actually need.
The question is whether policy will enable it.
