RE: Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part
of Clinical Care
Dear Dr. Keane:
On behalf of the Board of Directors of the Community Oncology Alliance (“COA”), we are
submitting this comment letter regarding the Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption
and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care (“RFI”).
COA is an organization dedicated to advocating for the complex care and access needs of patients
with cancer and the community oncology practices that serve them. COA is the only nonprofit
organization in the United States dedicated solely to independent community oncology practices,
which serve the majority of Americans receiving treatment for cancer. Since its grassroots
founding close to 25 years ago, COA’s mission has been to ensure that patients with cancer
receive quality, affordable, and accessible care in their own communities where they live and
work.
COA is committed to providing solutions to the challenges that community oncology patients
and physicians face throughout the care journey. We seek to ensure that cancer care is delivered
with the utmost quality and in an accessible and affordable manner.
Recognizing the role artificial intelligence (“AI”) plays in clinical care and the ongoing
momentum around its use, COA has established an AI and Digital Transformation Task Force.
Our feedback and recommendations below are informed by the community oncologists and
practice leaders who are actively adopting AI within their practices.
Background and Summary of Feedback and Recommendations
Independent community oncology practices are the backbone of cancer care delivery in the
United States, caring for most patients close to home across urban, suburban, rural, and
underserved communities. Because this is where the majority of treatment decisions and
longitudinal follow-up occur, supporting digital transformation in community settings is essential
to achieving national goals for quality, health outcomes, access, and affordability in cancer care.1

The digital transformation required to integrate AI into clinical care is substantial, necessitating not only financial
investment but also significant time and human resources. Implementing AI solutions involves a steep learning curve
and the adaptation of existing workflows, which can overwhelm small practices with limited personnel and financial
bandwidth. Without proper incentives, the risks may outweigh the potential benefits, making it especially challenging
for community practices to engage in this essential evolution.
Historically, the introduction of electronic health records (“EHRs”) provides a fitting precedent. Incentives in that
case enabled practices to invest in the necessary technology and training while offsetting the initial transition costs.
To facilitate the adoption of AI tools, we urge the Department of Health and Human Services (“HHS”) to consider a
similar incentive structure. This could include financial reimbursement models for implementing AI applications that
drive improved patient outcomes, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness. By promoting access to high-value AI clinical
interventions, we can ensure that community practices remain viable and do not face pressures to consolidate into
higher-cost hospital service settings.
Failing to effectively transition into a digital-first approach could jeopardize the sustainability of smaller practices.
As pressures mount from larger health care systems that can leverage advanced technologies to reduce costs and
enhance patient care, the risk of community oncology practices falling behind is real. This would ultimately lead to
reduced patient choice and increased health care costs for the communities we serve.
Our responses focus on questions 1, 2, 6, and 9 in the RFI. Next are our summary responses, followed by more
detailed responses.
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption and
use in clinical care?
The biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption and use in clinical care include
financial barriers and resource constraints; uncertainty regarding regulatory expectations, accountability, and legal
liability for non-device AI tools; and limited access to interoperable data across care settings.
1. Financial Barriers and Resource Constraints
Up-front and non-recoverable implementation costs may limit practices’ adoption of AI tools and, once
implemented, a lack of reimbursement for AI-enabled clinical or administrative functions places pressure on
practices’ long-term and sustained AI use. For practices to fully leverage AI in clinical care, the financial
incentives must account for the full range of costs associated with AI use, including additional investment in
technical infrastructure and expertise. Even when AI tools are clinically promising, practices may be unable to
deploy them safely or effectively without significant external support. This creates a widening gap between large,
well-resourced health systems and community-based practices, potentially exacerbating disparities in access to
innovation.
2. Uncertainty Regarding Regulatory Expectations, Accountability, and Legal Liability
For providers, most professional liability policies were not designed with AI-assisted clinical workflows in mind.
Providers must also be able to understand the limitations, training data sources, and intended use of AI systems
– providers cannot reasonably assess risk or ensure appropriate clinical oversight without transparency.
Malpractice risk is another concern, as providers lack clarity on whether the use of AI tools may affect
malpractice coverage or exposure, particularly when tools influence documentation, clinical reasoning, or
treatment decisions. On the vendor side, many AI vendors offer limited service-level agreements (“SLAs”)

Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or programs could
be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
HHS should consider several strategies to incentivize the adoption of AI and support digital transformation in clinical
care. We recognize that congressional action may be required to enable HHS to set up these initiatives. However,
our hope is that HHS will adopt these initiatives whenever possible. COA recommends a multi-faceted approach that
includes:
1. Financial Incentives and Infrastructure Development
i. Grants and Funding Opportunities: Establishment of grant programs specifically for community
practices to fund AI implementation projects and require transparent reporting of safety, performance,
and health outcomes impact.
ii. Tax Incentives: Working with the Internal Revenue Service (“IRS”), HHS should provide tax incentives
for qualifying AI technologies and investments in digital infrastructure.
iii. Pilot Programs: Launch a pilot program to incentivize early adopters of AI technologies, including
through the Center for Medicare & Medicaid Services (“CMS”) Innovation Center Authority at Social
Security Act (“SSA”) § 1115A.
2. Training and Partnerships
i. Data Sharing Initiatives: Enhanced interoperability standards and government-led initiatives to
encourage data sharing among practices and AI systems would make it easier to demonstrate the efficacy
of AI tools through shared insights and benchmarks. CMS could also leverage the voluntary CMS
Health Technology Ecosystem pledges to further accelerate AI adoption.
ii. Recognition Programs: Establish awards or recognition for practices that successfully implement AI-
driven solutions, boosting visibility in the community and providing positive reinforcement for
innovative efforts. HHS’s Million Hearts Hospitals & Health Systems Recognition Program could be
used as a model, and the Health Technology Ecosystem pledges could include an AI-specific pledge.
iii. Public-Private Partnerships: Collaborations between government entities and private sector
technology developers could create tailored AI solutions for small practices. This could also include
shared investment in research and development. Partnerships should be paired with shared
implementation playbooks, model contracts, and standardized governance requirements to reduce

Question 9: What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the adoption
and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and

Patients and caregivers generally support the use of technology that improves access, reduces delays, and enhances

clinical oversight. Policies that reinforce these expectations will be essential to sustaining trust in the use of AI in

Following are further detailed responses to questions 1,2, 6, and 9.
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption and
use in clinical care?
There are several practical barriers that limit the adoption of AI in clinical care, including financial barriers and
resource constraints, uncertainty regarding regulatory expectations and accountability for non-device AI tools, and
limited access to interoperable data across care settings. Without targeted federal guidance, incentives, and technical
assistance, AI adoption risks becoming concentrated in only the largest health systems – undermining broader policy
goals of scalable innovation in health care delivery.
1. Financial Barriers and Resource Constraints
Financial barriers are a significant hurdle to the adoption of AI in clinical care. Up-front and non-recoverable
implementation costs may limit practices’ adoption of AI tools, and once implemented, a lack of reimbursement
for AI-enabled clinical or administrative functions places pressure on practices’ long-term and sustained AI use.
For practices to fully leverage AI in clinical care, the financial incentives must account for the full range of costs
associated with AI use.
Limited technical infrastructure and expertise at the practice level are critical and often overlooked barriers,
particularly within small and midsized community oncology groups. Many practices lack the internal technical
capacity needed to implement, validate, and govern AI tools responsibly. Many practices lack dedicated data
engineering or informatics staff; AI governance or model evaluation frameworks; infrastructure to monitor
performance, bias, or drift over time; resources to conduct vendor due diligence or technical validation; and
expertise to integrate AI tools safely into clinical workflows. As a result, even when AI tools are clinically
promising, practices may be unable to deploy them safely or effectively without significant external support. This
creates a widening gap between large, well-resourced health systems and community-based practices, potentially
exacerbating disparities in access to innovation.

Uncertainty regarding regulatory expectations, accountability, and legal liability for non-device AI tools used in
clinical workflows is a major barrier to adoption. While the FDA has established regulatory pathways for
Software as a Medical Device (“SaMD”), many AI tools currently being deployed in health care – such as clinical
decision support, ambient documentation, summarization tools, and workflow automation – fall outside
traditional medical device classifications. While the FDA recently released updated Clinical Decision Support
Software – Guidance for Industry and FDA Staff, uncertainty remains for providers in several critical areas8:
i. Accountability and liability: It remains unclear how legal responsibility is apportioned among clinicians,
health systems, and vendors when AI tools contribute to clinical decision-making errors. This uncertainty
is particularly concerning given the potential for AI hallucinations, data bias, or incomplete context in large
language models.
ii. Malpractice risk: Most professional liability policies were not designed with AI-assisted clinical
workflows in mind. Providers lack clarity on whether use of AI tools may affect malpractice coverage or
exposure, particularly when tools influence documentation, clinical reasoning, or treatment decisions.
iii. Vendor accountability and transparency: Many AI vendors offer limited SLAs related to model
performance, uptime, data provenance, or error rates. This creates a mismatch between the clinical risk
assumed by providers and the contractual protections offered by vendors.
iv. Trust and defensibility: Clinicians must understand the limitations, training data sources, and intended
use of AI systems. Without transparency into model behavior and performance characteristics, providers
cannot reasonably assess risk or ensure appropriate clinical oversight. The potential for bias, hallucinations,
and errors underscores the need for transparency about the underlying assumptions of AI tools. Ultimately,
if a data set being used in an AI model does not match a patient, it introduces biases and limits the
applicability of the tool and findings to the patient.9
3. Limited Access to High-Quality, Interoperable, and Clinically Usable Data
Limited access to high-quality, interoperable, and clinically usable data across care settings is another barrier to
AI adoption. Even where interoperability standards exist, such as those promulgated through the Health Data,
Technology, and Interoperability rules, access to usable, high-quality data remains a major barrier to effective AI
deployment. Clinical data across care settings is often incomplete, inconsistently structured, poorly normalized,
and fragmented across multiple systems, vendors, and care sites. Key challenges include:
i. Data quality and heterogeneity: Real-world clinical data frequently contain missing values, inconsistent
coding, unstructured text, and variable documentation practices. AI models trained on such data may
produce unreliable or biased outputs.
ii. Interoperability gaps: While standards such as Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (“FHIR”) have
improved data exchange, real-world implementation remains inconsistent. Many data elements critical for
oncology care – such as staging, biomarker data, treatment intent, or outcomes – are not reliably structured
or exchanged.

Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or programs could
be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
HHS should consider several strategies to incentivize the adoption of AI and support digital transformation in clinical
care. We recommend a multifaceted approach that focuses on financial incentives and infrastructure development,
training and partnerships, regulatory streamlining, and reimbursement. We recognize that congressional action may
be required to enable HHS to stand up some of these initiatives. However, our hope is that HHS will adopt these
initiatives whenever possible.
1. Financial Incentives and Infrastructure Development
i. Grants and Funding Opportunities: We recommend that HHS establish grant programs specifically for
community practices to fund AI implementation projects, including funding non-recoverable start-up costs
(integration, data governance, model validation/bias testing, cybersecurity), and require transparent
reporting of safety, performance, and health outcomes.
ii. Tax Incentives: Working with the IRS, HHS should provide refundable tax credits, deductions, or
accelerated depreciation for qualifying AI technologies and investments in digital infrastructure (including
cybersecurity and interoperability upgrades), helping lower the financial burden of implementation,
particularly for smaller independent practices.
iii. Pilot Programs: We support the launch of pilot programs that incentivize early adopters of AI technologies,
allowing practices to test AI applications in a controlled environment while providing funding and support.
These could be conducted by CMS using its CMS Innovation Center Authority at SSA § 1115A – we provide
specific examples in other sections of this letter below.

data sharing among practices and AI systems would make it easier to demonstrate the efficacy of AI tools
through shared insights and benchmarks. This includes alignment with existing interoperability
requirements under 45 CFR Part 171.

to deliver results in the first quarter of this year, and we look forward to seeing how those results positively

Question 6: Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost expectations and
where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the greatest potential to improve health
care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
Tools that provide interactive clinical decision-making support have the potential to improve health care outcomes,
provide new insights into quality, and reduce costs. For example, treatment guidelines, such as the National
Comprehensive Cancer Network (“NCCN”) Guidelines, are a key resource for evidence-based and consensus-driven
cancer care. These guidelines are updated at least annually by independent expert panels and play a critical role in
the oncology space because they are often used by payers to determine coverage, and by providers to inform treatment
pathways. However, these guidelines are typically published as static PDFs.25
More dynamic implementations – such as interactive decision trees or AI-enabled query tools – have the potential to
improve access to guideline-based recommendations at the point of care. When thoughtfully designed, such tools
could support a more consistent application of evidence-based medicine while reducing cognitive burden on
clinicians.
AI tools aimed at reducing administrative burden – such as ambient documentation, chart summarization, coding
assistance, and patient identification for clinical trials – have shown promise in improving clinician experience and
operational efficiency. Several community oncology practices have piloted these tools with encouraging qualitative
results.
For example, one COA member practice has onboarded about 500 doctors using an AI scribe, which provides chart
summarization and ICD-10 coding suggestions. The practice also uses AI to identify patients for clinical trials, who
might otherwise be overlooked, through a pilot that assesses patient eligibility and screens them for suitability,
ultimately providing more patients with access to clinical trials. Talking to a patient about a clinical trial can take
four times as long as talking to a patient about standard of care therapy, so having these AI tools provides an
opportunity to increase clinical trial access. Importantly, the AI scribe provides clinical decision support and does
not supplant providers’ autonomous decisions.26

These are only a few examples of the many ways in which AI has the potential to support cancer care – applications
also include cancer detection, screening, diagnosis, tumor analysis, biomarker assessment, and drug discovery.27
Cancer researchers continue to investigate new use cases for AI, expanding the potential for its adoption in clinical
care.28
That said, most implementations to date remain early in maturity, and widespread evidence demonstrating measurable
cost savings or improved outcomes remains limited. Emerging evidence suggests that the financial ROI for these
tools remains modest and highly variable, particularly in community-based practice settings.
Recent peer-reviewed literature supports this observation. A 2024 study from the University of California, San
Francisco, published in JAMA Network Open, evaluated the impact of ambient AI scribes on clinician productivity
and billing, finding improvements in documentation and work relative value units (“RVUs”). The study did not find
evidence of an increase in billing denials with AI scribe adoption.29 However, the resulting financial gains may be
insufficient to generate substantial net ROI to support practice implementation after accounting for licensing,
implementation, and operational costs.
Similarly, an analysis from Kaiser Permanente published in The New England Journal of Medicine highlighted both
the promise and limitations of ambient AI tools, noting that while clinician experience may improve, financial and
operational returns remain uncertain and highly context dependent.30
These findings align with the experience of many community oncology practices, including COA members, where
AI pilots have demonstrated technical feasibility and quality-of-life improvements but have not yet produced
consistent or scalable financial returns.
Collectively, these experiences suggest that while AI holds significant potential to improve care delivery, most current
applications are still in an early stage of development. Real-world implementation challenges observed in community
oncology must be addressed to facilitate sustained adoption. Across multiple AI pilot programs conducted by
community oncology practices, several consistent themes have emerged:
• Data quality and preparation remain significant barriers: AI tools that rely on clinical documents,
scanned records, or faxes require extensive human oversight and data normalization. Even well-performing
tools often require prolonged quality assurance efforts to achieve acceptable accuracy. In practice, accuracy
rates approaching, but not reaching, 100 percent remain common, limiting the extent to which automation
can replace manual review.
• Operational lift is frequently underestimated: The effort required to vet vendors, negotiate contracts,
implement integrations, train staff, monitor performance, and manage ongoing quality control is substantial.
These indirect costs are often not reflected in vendor ROI projections.
• Quality-of-life improvements do not always translate into financial ROI: Many AI tools deliver value
through reduced cognitive burden, improved clinician satisfaction, or streamlined workflows. While
meaningful, these benefits do not always translate into measurable cost savings or revenue generation –
particularly in fee-for-service or hybrid reimbursement environments.

Question 9: What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the adoption
and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and
use of AI in clinical care?
Patients and caregivers generally support the use of technology that improves access, reduces delays, and enhances
coordination of care. At the same time, they value transparency, strong privacy protections, and continued human
clinical oversight. Policies that reinforce these expectations will be essential to sustaining trust in the use of AI in
clinical care.
1. Concerns Regarding AI Use in Utilization Management, Including Prior Authorization
AI use in utilization review is of great concern to COA. We have long expressed concerns about how PA places
a significant burden on providers and patients.31,32 A survey of physicians found that 94 percent of physicians
surveyed stated that PA had a “somewhat or significant negative impact” on patient clinical outcomes, and more
than a quarter of physicians reported that PA led to a serious adverse event for a patient in their care. Sixty-one
percent of physicians surveyed indicated that they are concerned that AI increases or will increase PA denial
rates.33 In turn, denials delay patient access to treatment, which is particularly pressing for patients with cancer,
as delays in treatment initiation have been associated with increased mortality across several cancer types.34
These findings underscore the risks of AI use in an immensely burdensome process that adversely impacts patient
care, particularly as insurers’ use of AI grows. A survey from the National Association of Insurance
Commissioners (“NAIC”) found that 84 percent of responding health insurers use AI/machine learning.35,36
Utilization management is a primary use, with 71 percent reporting they currently or are exploring using AI for
utilization management practices.37 While we acknowledge that AI may speed up utilization review, providers
and patients need confidence that wrongful denials will not occur because of a lack of human oversight.
Unfortunately, at this time, many patients and providers are not even aware in cases where AI is involved in
utilization review; NAIC’s survey found that less than a quarter of insurers tell providers when AI is used.38
While CMS has taken some steps to regulate Medicare Advantage organizations’ (“MAOs”) use of AI in making
coverage determinations and has stated that it is the responsibility of the MAO to ensure that AI complies with

To add further guardrails regarding plans’ use of AI and to promote transparency, we recommend CMS expand
PA reporting requirements to require health plans to also report when AI was used in PA decisions, stratified at
the item or service level for both drugs and services, with a breakdown of the percentages of whether requests
were approved or denied. CMS is already implementing public reporting requirements for PA of services, but
these are likely to mask trends in PA decisions since they are in the aggregate and are for services only.43 We
believe that data collection and public reporting from health plans that use AI in coverage decisions are vital for
providers, patients, and CMS, and support strategies that facilitate the collection of accurate, transparent data.
However, increased transparency and data collection are not a substitute for policies that make the role of human
oversight clear.
Recent research from Stanford University highlights several concerns and risks related to the use of AI: doubts
regarding the thoroughness of human review for AI-made decisions; users’ lack of familiarity with AI and how
it works; lack of information regarding what drives an AI prediction; underperformance, where models miss the
mark; unintended consequences, such as the creation of perverse incentives related to denials; and variance in
governance practices across health care organizations.44
While all of these issues are of significant concern, COA is particularly worried about how AI’s role in utilization
management might impact the use of precision medicine, which plays a vital role in oncology. Precision medicine
and targeted therapies enable physicians to tailor treatments to each patient’s cancer based on its genetic and
molecular characteristics. Restrictive insurance policies already often fail to keep pace with these innovations,
and the use of AI in utilization management for these treatments may further delay patient access to needed cancer
care.45
2. Concerns Regarding Patient Privacy and Security
Patient privacy and security are also critical in facilitating AI adoption. HHS itself has reported a 256 percent
increase in large breaches involving hacking and a 264 percent increase in ransomware over five years, making
data and security concerns particularly pressing.46 Patients must be able to trust that their information will be used
in a HIPAA-compliant manner, that they will be informed of when their data will be used within an AI tool, and
that safeguards are in place in the event of a data breach.

Conclusion
Structured incentives tailored to support community practices through the adoption of AI are vital for fostering
innovation, ensuring access to advanced treatments, and maintaining the competitive edge necessary to thrive in an
evolving health care landscape. We propose that these incentives be modeled after successful strategies used during
the EHR rollout, with the aim of encouraging engagement and investment in AI integration across the board. A multi-
faceted approach, focusing on financial incentives and infrastructure development, training and partnerships,
regulatory streamlining, and reimbursement, is critical to accelerate AI adoption. At the same time, policies that
expand AI use must incorporate transparency, strong privacy protections, and continued human clinical oversight.
