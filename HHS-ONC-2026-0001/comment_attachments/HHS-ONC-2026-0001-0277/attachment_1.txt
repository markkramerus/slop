1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care
and its adoption and use in clinical care?
The most significant barrier to AI innovation, adoption, and use in clinical care that we seek to
highlight in this response is the lack of a clear, predictable reimbursement pathway under
Medicare to account for the costs of these technologies. AI tools used in clinical care have
the potential to reduce overall health care costs by improving clinical efficiency, supporting
more timely and accurate clinical decision-making and enabling high quality care in the face
of healthcare workforce shortages. Additionally, many AI tools used in clinical care improve
patient outcomes through more accurate and earlier disease detection, and better treatment
recommendations. At the same time, adoption of these technologies imposes real and often
significant upfront and ongoing costs on hospitals and physician practices that are not
adequately recognized under current reimbursement frameworks.
2

As HHS and CMS consider barriers to adoption of AI tools for clinical care, it cannot be
understated the importance that adequate reimbursement will play to support future
adoption. CMS has long recognized that improvements in efficiency mean less work for
physicians and have accordingly adjusted Physician Fee Schedule payment to account for
that work reduction. These increases in efficiency are due in part to improvements in
technology including use of AI tools for clinical care; however, there is no corresponding
payment to support adoption of these technologies by hospitals and physician practices.
Under prevailing fee-for-service payment models, many of these savings accrue outside the
provider setting that bears the cost of adopting and maintaining the AI technology.
For providers, implementation of AI tools used in clinical care requires meaningful
investment, including but not limited to:
• Acquisition or subscription costs for AI software and/or enabled hardware;
• Integration with existing clinical IT systems, including EHRs, PACS, and device
infrastructure;
• Cybersecurity, data governance, and compliance resources;
• Training of clinicians and staff and ongoing workflow optimization; and
• Maintenance, updates, validation, and performance monitoring over time.
These costs are incremental to existing operating expenses and are often incurred without a
corresponding increase in reimbursable services. In some cases, AI may even reduce billable
volume by preventing unnecessary interventions, creating a financial disincentive for
adoption despite clear clinical and operational value. As a result, the current reimbursement
environment may unintentionally slow adoption of high-value AI technologies, particularly for
smaller hospitals, rural providers, and independent physician practices with limited capital
and IT resources. Without targeted reimbursement pathways or value-based incentives,
providers may be unable to justify investment in AI even when such tools demonstrably
improve efficiency, safety, and patient outcomes.
Aligning reimbursement policy with the economic realities of AI implementation is essential
to ensure that providers are not penalized for adopting technologies that improve care
delivery and system-wide efficiency. Thoughtful reimbursement strategies can accelerate
responsible AI adoption while promoting sustainability for hospitals and physician practices.

2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What
HHS regulations, policies, or programs should be revisited to augment your
ability to develop or use AI in clinical care? Please provide specific changes and
applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
As CMS further considers the establishment of a reimbursement framework for software as a
service (SaaS), we believe that future policies should strike an appropriate balance, by easing
financial barriers to hospital and physician adoption without providing incentives for over-
utilization. In the context of Medicare’s Outpatient Prospective Payment System (OPPS) for
hospitals, CMS has relied upon temporary New Technology Ambulatory Payment
Classifications (APCs) and transitional pass-through (TPT) payments for devices to provide
this balance. However, there is currently no pathway for physicians to receive reimbursement
for utilizing SaaS in their practices. We urge HHS to direct CMS to adapt these mechanisms to
enable adoption of AI in clinical care in hospital outpatient, ambulatory surgical center (ASC)
settings, and physician practices under fee-for-service Medicare, while in parallel pursuing
value-based payment mechanisms for long-term sustainability, as outlined in detail below.
New Technology APCs
The use of procedural C-codes with payment established through New Technology APCs,
enables patient access during the initial period of clinical adoption, while simultaneously
facilitating the collection of hospital cost data to inform permanent APC assignments in the
future. CMS could effectively utilize this approach for AI to increase hospitals’ ability to invest
in innovative technologies to advance the speed, accuracy, and quality of diagnostic and
therapeutic decision making in health care, ultimately enabling improved standards of care.
More specifically, we support the payment pathway outlined in The Health Tech Investment
Act (S. 1399) for algorithm-based healthcare services (ABHS) using New Technology APCs,
which we believe CMS could adopt using existing authorities. The payment pathway outlined
in this legislation provides a dedicated framework for Medicare to reimburse for ABHS,
defined as services provided with an FDA approved or cleared medical device that utilizes AI,
machine learning (ML), or other similar technology to produce clinical outputs or generate
clinical conclusions with physician or other qualified health care professional involvement in
the screening, detection, planning, diagnosis, or treatment of an individual’s condition or
disease. Under this pathway, CMS could adapt the New Technology APC application process
4

and criteria to ensure that all ABHS, including those that are performed in conjunction with an
underlying service, are eligible for payment under the OPPS. In addition, ABHS could be
assigned to a New Technology APC for no less than five years until there is sufficient data on
costs to assign the service to a clinical APC or create a new APC.
The New Technology APC pathway is also a suitable approach with respect to coding for
ABHS, because it allows CMS to create procedural C-codes as needed and outside the
American Medical Association (AMA) CPT Editorial Panel process. CPT codes are established
by the AMA primarily to describe physician services, and therefore may not be sufficient for
hospital and ASC billing purposes in all cases. For procedures involving sophisticated clinical
software, including AI, acquired on a subscription or per-use basis, CPT codes may not reflect
facilities’ costs to perform the procedure, and therefore fail to comprehensively describe the
service from the standpoint of the facility. In such cases, the creation of procedural C-codes
by CMS may be necessary to facilitate appropriate billing and payment for ABHS by hospitals
and ASCs.
In the absence of finalized legislation, we believe CMS should propose a unified and
predictable reimbursement framework leveraging the New Technology APC pathway for
software technologies based on CMS-defined categories and existing statutory authority, to
help address the current uncertainty around valuation and to encourage consistent adoption
of these technologies in hospital outpatient and ASC settings of care.
TPT Payment for Devices
Under the OPPS, temporary TPT payments are used to enable hospital adoption of
implantable medical devices that are new, costly, and that have either evidence
demonstrating substantial clinical improvement compared to available treatments, or
Breakthrough Device status as designated by the FDA. The current regulation at 42 CFR
419.66(b)(3) requires devices to come into contact with human tissue, and therefore excludes
AI technologies. We urge CMS to consider adapting the TPT requirements, or consider
developing a TPT-like framework, to accommodate high-value AI innovations that would
otherwise not be accounted for under the cost-based prospective payment system
methodology, in order to reduce hospital barriers to adoption.

Physician Fee Schedule
At present, there are no established payment pathways for physicians to be reimbursed for
the acquisition or use of SaaS solutions in the office setting. As medical technology advances,
particularly in the area of implantable devices, physicians lack a predictable mechanism (and
in some cases, any mechanism) to recover investments associated with these innovations. For
example, emerging implantable devices are being designed with the capability for future
FDA approved software “unlocks,” enabling enhanced therapeutic functionality over time.
These new, enhanced software-based capabilities will allow patients and clinicians to benefit
from technological advancements without requiring full device replacement, thereby
eliminating clinical risks of reoperation, and improving outcomes while preserving the value
of the original implant.
Value-Based Payment
While improvements to Medicare fee-for-service reimbursement methodologies are vital in
the short-term to ensure AI innovation in clinical care does not stagnate, we encourage HHS
to maintain an eye toward a future in which payers’ and providers’ incentives are aligned to
provide the best possible care to patients at the lowest possible cost. In doing so, CMS
should explore how stronger incentives for high-value AI clinical interventions can be created
through existing quality measurement programs and through CMMI payment models. For
example, outcomes associated with the use of AI in clinical care could be studied and
rewarded through a framework similar to that of the upcoming ACCESS model.
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and
cost expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI
tools would have the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give
new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
As indicated above, AI tools used in clinical care can improve patient outcomes through
more accurate and earlier disease detection, and better treatment recommendations. At the
same time, adoption of these technologies imposes real and often significant upfront and
ongoing costs on hospitals and physician practices that are not adequately recognized under
current reimbursement frameworks. We offer two examples of AI technologies – AI-assisted
6

colonoscopy and fractional flow reserve angiography (FFRangio) – that exceed performance
expectations yet face challenges in terms of their deployment in clinical care to illustrate the
barriers that inadequate and unclear reimbursement pathways can present.
AI-Assisted Colonoscopy
Medtronic’s GI Genius™ CADe Intelligent Endoscopy Unit enhances patient care through the
use of AI to identify suspicious lesions in real time for evaluation during a colonoscopy, and is
clinically proven to increase the adenoma detection rate (ADR). In a randomized controlled
trial, the GI Genius™ system resulted in an average absolute ADR improvement of 14.4%
compared with standard colonoscopy,1 increasing the odds that patients with lesions will be
detected and treated earlier. Importantly, each 1% improvement in ADR has been shown to
lead to a 3% reduction in interval colorectal cancer over a 10-year follow-up period.2 GI
Genius™ has been further shown to reduce the Adenoma Miss Rate.3 Despite the
tremendous benefit the use of GI Genius™ can provide – both to individual patients and at a
population health level – there are currently no coding and payment mechanisms to account
for the costs of facilities and physicians adopting this technology.
FFRangio
Evidence shows that when fractional flow reserve (FFR) is used to help classify coronary
lesions there is a significant reduction in stenting, lower costs, and improved outcomes.4,5
Traditional FFR is a wire-based procedure, but FFRangio provides a less invasive method of
evaluating hemodynamic relevance of coronary artery stenosis by using AI analysis of routine
angiography images to create three-dimensional functional models of the coronary tree.
FFRangio is shown to have a comparably high degree of diagnostic accuracy to wire-based
