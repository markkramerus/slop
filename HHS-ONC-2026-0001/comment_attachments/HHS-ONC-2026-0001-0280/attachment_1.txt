Regulatory Frameworks
Access to high-quality, interoperable clinical data is foundational to effective Al

development and deployment. HHS should promote policies that enable secure data
sharing and interoperability while maintaining strong, practical privacy protections under
HIPAA. Overly prescriptive, duplicative or rigid privacy controls risk limiting the
availability of data needed to train, validate, and monitor AI systems. A balanced
regulatory framework is essential to supporting responsible data use while
accelerating innovation and preserving patient trust.

Payment and Incentives
We support payment policy changes that ensure payers have the incentive and ability to

promote access to high-value AI clinical interventions, foster competition among clinical
care AI tool builders, and accelerate access to and affordability of AI tools for clinical
care.

Current reimbursement models often fail to recognize the value created by Al-enabled
clinical care, particularly where AI improves outcomes, efficiency, or clinician workload
without increasing service volume. HHS should explore reimbursement pathways that
reward demonstrated value, including new or updated codes, bundled payments, or value-
based payment models that account for Al-driven improvements. Aligning
reimbursement with innovation incentives is critical to scaling AI adoption across health
care systems.

Research, Evidence and Learning Systems
Recognizing that HHS supports one of the world’s largest health research ecosystems,

HHS should support structured public-private partnerships to generate real-world
evidence on AI performance, safety, and equity impacts. HHS should invest in federated
research networks, shared testbeds, and pilot programs which would enable scalable
evidence generation and development while protecting proprietary intellectual property.
These public stakeholder collaborations would accelerate learning, support responsible
adoption, and provide regulators and providers with credible, real-world insights into AI
effectiveness.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to meaningfully improve patient care,
enhance safety, and strengthen clinical and regulatory decision-making across the health
care system. Realizing this potential depends not on replacing existing regulatory or
clinical frameworks, but on modernizing the data and evidence infrastructure that
supports them.

The recommendations outlined below are not intended to shortcut FDA review,
weaken standards for medical device quality, or displace the role of clinicians,
regulators, or other professionals entrusted with protecting patients. Rather, they are
intended to enable existing oversight frameworks to function more effectively in an
increasingly data-driven health care environment.

From our experience across the medical technology ecosystem, there are three
interrelated areas where improved interoperability and policy alignment are
essential to support responsible AI adoption in health care: 1) adoption of the medical
device Unique Device Identifier (UDI) in data capture, 2) use of real-world evidence
(RWE) for regulatory decision-making, 3) timely, evidence-aligned coverage and
payment decisions, and 4) governance, accountability and liability clarity.

1. Adoption of UDI Data Capture by Health Care Providers and CMS

e UDI capture is foundational to trustworthy AI in health care. Improved
interoperability can enable reliable device-level evidence through structured UDI
capture within electronic health records, registries, and claims systems. While
UDI requirements are well established at the point of device labeling, downstream
capture within clinical systems and administrative data remains inconsistent.

e UDI data capture is particularly important for higher-risk devices used across
diverse patient populations and care settings.

2. Use of Real-World Evidence for Regulatory Decision-Making

e Real-world evidence can complement traditional regulatory pathways when data
are relevant and reliable. Use of real-world evidence does not reduce regulatory
rigor; it supports decisions grounded in real-world clinical performance.

e Interoperability enables scalable, representative evidence generation across
clinical records, imaging systems, registries, and administrative data sources.

e Al-enabled evidence generation can also serve existing FDA programs designed
to expedite patient access to transformative technologies. For example, the FDA’s
Breakthrough Devices Program provides an established framework for early
engagement and iterative evidence development for devices addressing unmet
medical needs.

e Al-supported real-world data analysis can strengthen the Breakthrough Devices
Program by enabling more efficient evidence generation, supporting postmarket
learning, and accelerating the translation of technological innovations into clinical
practice without compromising regulatory rigor.

3. Enable Timely, Evidence-Aligned Coverage and Payment Decisions

Misalignment between regulatory evidence, coverage decisions, and payment
policy is a known limitation of our current systems that often delay by several
years patient access to beneficial technologies.

Enhanced interoperability and reliance on real-world evidence can support more
timely and informed coverage and reimbursement decisions while maintaining the
highest standards of safety, quality, and effectiveness.

Our shared objective as a health care ecosystem should be to ensure that
beneficial technologies reach patients in time—not only to improve quality of
life and manage chronic disease, but in many cases to save lives—while
preserving the rigor, accountability, and trust that underpin public confidence in
medical innovation.

4. Governance, Accountability, and Liability Clarity

Ongoing uncertainty around responsibility for Al-enabled tools across
developers, deploying organizations, and clinicians continues to slow responsible
adoption. HHS can support innovation and patient safety by issuing practical
governance guidance that clarifies accountability across the AI lifecycle, rather
than creating new liability regimes.

Effective oversight of artificial intelligence must enable innovation while ensuring patient
safety, public trust, and regulatory clarity across the health care ecosystem.

Appropriate Regulation

Al regulation should focus on the specific use and application of AJ rather than
the underlying technology itself. Oversight should be risk-based, proportionate to
potential patient impact, and capable of adapting over time as technologies and
clinical use evolve.

United States Leadership in AI

The U.S. should lead the world in responsible Al development by establishing a
clear, coherent federal regulatory framework that supports innovation and can be
harmonized internationally. Global alignment is critical to advancing patient
access, fostering innovation, and avoiding conflicting requirements. A federal
approach to AI oversight in health care is essential to avoid a fragmented
regulatory landscape that could impede innovation and limit patient access.
Inconsistent or conflicting state-level requirements risk creating uncertainty,
duplicative compliance obligations, and uneven adoption across the health

Regulation of AI in medical technology and health care applications should align
