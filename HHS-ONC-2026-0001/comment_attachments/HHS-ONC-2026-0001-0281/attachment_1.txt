HHS Health Section AI RFI Response
1.0 Executive Summary
This document provides a comprehensive examination of the challenges and opportunities
associated with the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into clinical care and outlines proactive
measures that HHS can take to accelerate responsible and ethical adoption. AI has the potential to
be transformative in modernizing healthcare systems, improving operational efficiencies, reducing
clinician burnout, enhancing patient outcomes, improving diagnostic accuracy, and expanding
access to underserved populations. However, any strategy to accelerate AI in clinical settings will
need to address barriers to adoption, including fragmented data governance, low trust in AI
systems, liability questions, demographic bias, misaligned incentives, and workflow friction. As a
trusted partner in government transformation and digital enablement, LMI advocates for forward-
thinking strategies that promote the benefits of AI adoption and scalability while maintaining a
robust framework for safety, equity, and long-term sustainability.
Key Points:
Challenges to Adoption
• Trust & Liability: Clinicians and patients require confidence in AI accuracy and fairness,
while liability ambiguities complicate adoption.
• Outdated Infrastructure and Data Fragmentation: Fragmented and biased healthcare
data undermines the scalability and reliability of AI systems across diverse populations.
Additionally, aging infrastructure in large healthcare systems often proves incompatible
with modern AI platforms, limiting real-time data exchange and promoting the use of
standalone AI tools instead of seamless integration into existing systems.
• Operational Friction: Lack of human-centered design and clinician/patient perspectives
during development results in AI tools that fail to integrate seamlessly into workflows,
leading to "friction-filled" systems rather than optimized productivity.
• Economic Misalignment: Outdated fee-for-service models do not incentivize value-based
outcomes achievable through AI capabilities.
• Administrative Barriers to Adoption: Fragmented governance and oversight, lack of
resources or understanding around change management processes, complex regulatory
requirements, lack of standardized evaluation frameworks, and uncertainty around funding
and reimbursement all contribute to delayed AI deployment.
AI in Healthcare: Success so Far
• Reduced Administrative Burnout: Research shows that AI scribes have resulted in
decreased clinician burnout and increased patient capacity. A survey by the American
Medical Association in 2024 found that the percentage of medical practices using AI
almost doubled from 2023 and that clinicians are most comfortable with using these tools
to assist with administrative tasks, rather than direct patient care.
• Imaging and Diagnostics: Several emerging AI tools for radiology and pathology
demonstrate accuracy that rivals or exceeds human accuracy in detecting disease (Jones et
al., 2026; Phan et al., 2025).

LMI Recommendations for Strategic AI Integration
Recommendations for accelerating AI adoption span multiple modalities and are outlined below.
• Regulatory Modernization: To address barriers related to trust in AI, HHS can focus on
the establishment of risk-based oversight frameworks, standardized data privacy
requirements, and algorithm transparency and performance documentation.
• Governance Frameworks: Introduce guidelines for or creating standardized contracting
templates, transparency protocols, and model-lifecycle controls to address accountability
gaps in non-medical AI tools.
• Private Sector Collaboration: Foster public-private partnerships for scalable testing
infrastructure and enable third-party certification frameworks to encourage independent
validation of AI solutions.
• Research and Development Priorities: Focus on explainable AI outputs, robust
standards for data quality, performance benchmarks, and post-deployment monitoring and
drift detection to ensure AI systems evolve alongside dynamic healthcare practices without
undermining reliability.
• Surfacing Patient Perspectives: Patient perspectives have not been adequately
researched, but among those studies that have been done, the need for patient stakeholders
in AI development, human oversight, transparency, and importance of data privacy have
all been identified as key components for addressing patient trust in AI technologies.
• Workflow Integration and Organizational Change Management: Prioritize human-in-
the-loop models to incorporate clinician oversight, usability optimization, and cognitive
burden reduction for seamless adoption. Combine usability testing, training strategies, and
integrated change management to drive trusted adoption and scalable tools.
• Innovative Payment Models: Transition from fee-for-service to value-based care models,
like the model used by the CMS Advancing Chronic Care with Effective, Scalable
Solutions (ACCESS) initiative, to create incentives for adopting AI systems that
measurably improve patient outcomes and hospital efficiencies. Develop reimbursement
pathways that align with AI's unique capacity to achieve long-term operational and clinical
efficiencies.
AI holds immense promise for revolutionizing clinical care by addressing clinician burnout,
improving diagnosis, and increasing access for underserved populations. However, realizing this
potential requires coordinated regulatory reforms, better data governance, clear reimbursement
frameworks, human-centered implementation strategies, and alignment among clinicians, patients,
vendors, and healthcare systems. By addressing these barriers collectively, the U.S. healthcare
system can leverage AI to deliver safe, equitable, and high-quality care at scale.

2.0 Defining AI
Before discussing strategies for promoting the development and adoption of AI tools it is important
to understand what “AI” means. The term “Artificial Intelligence” or “AI” is frequently used
inconsistently and interchangeably to describe different capabilities and use cases, particularly in
vendor communications and popular discourse. This ambiguity complicates evaluation of actual
system behavior, associated risks, and appropriate oversight.
For the purposes of this document, AI is defined as falling into one of the following two categories:
Machine Learning (ML)
ML techniques are already widely deployed in healthcare and include both traditional statistical
learning and modern deep learning methods. These systems typically operate on structured or
semi-structured data and produce bounded, task-specific outputs.
Examples include:
• Risk prediction models (e.g., sepsis risk, readmission risk)
• Medical imaging and signal analysis (e.g., radiology, pathology, ECG interpretation)
• Population health stratification and resource optimization
• Clinical decision support rules enhanced with learned parameters
These systems are often deterministic at inference time, evaluated against predefined performance
metrics, and more amenable to validation within existing regulatory and quality frameworks.
Foundation models and Large Language Models (LLMs)
Foundation models, such as LLMs and multimodal models, are pre-trained on large, heterogeneous
datasets and adapted for downstream tasks and generation of ‘new’ content.
Examples include:
1. Clinical documentation assistance and summarization
2. Natural language interfaces to EHR data
3. Patient-facing chatbots and triage assistants
4. Multimodal models combining text, images, and signals
Unlike many traditional ML systems, these models:
• Generate open-ended outputs
• Exhibit probabilistic and context-sensitive behavior
• Produce fluent but sometimes incorrect or misleading responses
• Introduce new risks such as hallucination, automation bias, cognitive load, and alert fatigue
As a result, LLMs and other foundation models require distinct evaluation methods, workflow
testing, and human-centered safeguards. In the document below, LMI will speak to both categories

3.0 Regulation and Adoption
Existing Barriers to AI Adoption in Clinical Settings:
With a solid foundation in the scope and capabilities of AI, the focus now shifts to the critical
challenges and opportunities that influence its integration into clinical care. Private sector
innovation in AI for health care and its adoption in clinical care is shaped by a range of barriers
that differ depending on the stage of development, implementation, and the stakeholders involved.
These include:
Trust in AI: Clinicians and patients alike must have confidence in the safety, accuracy, and overall
performance of AI tools. Clinicians remain liable for patient care, meaning AI must meet high
standards of reliability, while patient trust depends on accurate, unbiassed tools for decision-
making about their care.
Liability concerns: Both clinicians and AI vendors face risks related to clinical errors caused by
AI systems. While clinicians are legally accountable for patient outcomes, AI vendors are
increasingly being called upon to share liability for faulty algorithms and design defects.
AI System Biases: AI systems may unintentionally prioritize certain variables or identify invalid
correlations, leading to biased outcomes. The lack of transparency in decision-making ("black box"
issues) further complicates efforts to address these biases. AI tools are often more accessible in
higher-resource settings, potentially widening healthcare gaps for underserved populations. Over-
reliance on AI without human oversight may overlook critical individual and contextual factors as
well. Mitigating such biases requires diverse datasets, regular auditing, human oversight, and
inclusive collaboration to ensure these technologies improve care for all populations equitably
(James, 2024). There are several examples of biased AI models resulting in discrepancies in patient
care and outcomes. An independent study found that black patients were not identified with
respiratory illness due to pulse oximeters being less accurate on darker skin. (UK Department of
Health and Social Care, 2024) In a review of an algorithmic model to identify liver disease, AI
missed it in 44% of female patients vs 23% of males. (Straw & Wu, 2022).
Data Access: Access to high-quality, diverse, non-fragmented data is critical for developing robust
AI models. Health data is often siloed across multiple systems and formats, making it difficult to
aggregate and standardize. Also, privacy and security constraints such as HIPAA and PHI make
data sharing and reuse complex. Even when data can be accessed, concerns over breaches,
confidentiality and patient consent slow down innovation.
Legacy Systems and Interoperability: Large healthcare systems often rely on aging
infrastructure that cannot easily communicate with modern AI platforms. Incompatible data
systems and lack of standardized data formats (e.g., FHIR) hinder real-time data exchange. In
addition, many AI tools are implemented as separate, “bolted-on” applications rather than being
seamlessly integrated into systems such as the electronic health record. This forces clinicians and

Analysis of the Barriers to AI Adoption in Healthcare
Early machine learning–based AI applications have exhibited model drift, algorithmic errors,
unintended biases, and overfitting, leading to decreased accuracy, classification or prediction
errors, and reduced ability to generalize. Generative AI applications can experience all these
problems as well as “hallucinations,” and “sycophantic behavior” leading to blatantly incorrect or
difficult-to-detect false information that can harm patients (Christophe et al., 2026; Peng et al.,
2026; Sittig & Singh, 2025).
Together, these barriers form a multi-layered ecosystem problem — not just a technical one.
Overcoming them requires coordinated action across:
• Policy and regulations to clarify standards and liability
• Data governance that balances privacy with ethical access
• Clinical partnerships to build trusted evidence and workflow fit
• Funding and incentives to support innovation and long-term ROI
• Education and change management to build clinician confidence
Only by addressing these factors in tandem can private-sector AI innovation truly translate into
reliable, safe, and widely used tools in clinical care.
While tools incorporating AI are rapidly making their way into clinical practice, mechanisms to
ensure their safety and effectiveness remain underdeveloped. For instance, there is little guidance
for health care organizations (HCOs) and clinicians as they navigate the complexities of
implementing AI into routine care. To ensure that integrating AI-enabled systems into health care
transforms clinical decision-making and improves patient outcomes, it is essential to
proactively address known risks and establish safeguards to manage potential unknown risks as
they emerge.
Clinicians are still liable for patient care quality and outcomes and therefore need to feel that the
AI input is reliable and accurate. For example, documentation tools such as Deep Scribe or Freed
reduce administrative workload and empower providers to focus on patient care by automating
transcription. However, the Scribe doesn’t always get the transcription 100% right and human
involvement is needed to ensure accuracy and maintain patient safety. To integrate AI safely, there
is an ongoing need for a “human-in-the-loop”. This applies not only to administrative clinical
applications, but also to diagnostic applications of AI. Algorithms have shown proficiency in
detecting conditions such as tumors, malaria, and prenatal issues, performing at or above expert
levels in certain cases (Fan et al., 2025; Faratisha et al., 2026; Isewon, Oba, & Oyelade, 2025;
Kumar et al., 2025; Nishida et al., 2022; Pai et al., 2025; Yeganegi et al., 2025). However,

efficiency, cutting costs, and expanding access, particularly in underserved rural areas. In an
overburdened healthcare system with significant shortage of nurses and physicians AI presents
opportunities for trusted scalable care.
LMI Policy Recommendations for Addressing Barriers to AI Adoption:
As HHS aims to shift the U.S. healthcare system toward "deflationary technologies"—tools that
improve health outcomes while simultaneously lowering long-term costs, the current regulatory,
payment policy and program design create an environment where AI adoption may remain limited
to "experimentation" rather than becoming a standard part of safe, high-quality clinical care.
To incentivize AI in clinical care, HHS should focus on modernizing outdated systems to ensure
they do not hinder innovation. Key priorities involve moving from rigid fee-for-service models
to outcomes-based incentives and establishing clear, risk-based regulatory guardrails.
Revising current Payment Policy to reflect a prioritization of efforts and programs aimed at
transitioning from episodic or fee-for-service payment models to alternative models that
incentivize and reward the long-term efficiencies possible through reasonable adoption of AI
technologies is one part of a strategy that will incentivize the adoption of AI in healthcare.
Prioritizing emerging models like the CMS ACCESS Model (CMS, 2025) which aims to provide
stable, recurring payments for technology supported chronic care rather than traditional fee-for-
service payment models is a start on this journey. Coupling the CMS ACCESS Model program

When examining the regulatory landscape, opportunities exist to remove the “bottlenecks” that
currently exist for developers and healthcare systems alike. Proposed regulatory changes to remove
these barriers and incentivize the effective use of AI in healthcare include a transition to a Risk-
Based Oversight model that employs rigidity on a sliding scale that is proportionate to the risk
itself. For example, low risk tools (e.g. administrative automation) would be subject to less
rigorous regulatory hurdles while high risk/impact tools (e.g. appeals and grievances and
utilization management) require increased scrutiny and a higher regulatory bar to clear.
Other regulatory design changes to consider would include:
1. Addressing Health IT Certification and Transparency by updating the ONC Health IT
Certification Program to include criteria for algorithm transparency and decision support,
ensuring clinicians understand and trust AI-generated recommendations. Specifically,
implement and refine the Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability (HTI-1)
requirements under 45 CFR Part 170 to strengthen decision support transparency that requires
certified health IT to provide users with a "consistent, baseline set of information" about
algorithms, including their fairness, validity, and safety as well as simplifying the certification
process for AI models to reduce development costs and administrative burdens.
2. Data Privacy and Data De-identification: Modernize 45 CFR § 164.514(b) to address the
risks of re-identification in the age of big data and AI. Specifically, move beyond the static
“safe harbor” list of 18 identifiers to as few as four pieces of non-exclusive data can now re-
identify an individual. Updates should provide more flexible, risk-based de-identification
methods that allow for richer datasets while maintaining high privacy standards.
3. Medicare Advantage and Prior Authorization: Update 42 CFR § 422.101(b)(6) and 42
CFR § 422.138 regarding utilization management and prior authorization to explicitly state
that Medicare Advantage (MA) Plans cannot use AI alone to deny coverage; decisions must
be based on an individual’s specific circumstance and a must retain a Human-in-the-Loop. MA
plans must make publicly available any internal coverage criteria used by the AI models.
4. FDA Lifecycle Management for AI Devices: Shift from “static” device approvals to dynamic
lifecycle oversight by formalizing the Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCP) within the
existing frameworks of 21 CFR Part 820 (Quality System Regulation) and marketing
pathways (510(k), De Novo, PMA) to allow a PCCP to pre-authorize specific future algorithm
updates (e.g., iterative machine learning improvements) without needing a new marketing
submission for every change, provided they maintain rigorous quality standards.
Other programmatic design changes to support AI adoption include:
1. Create AI Implementation Grants through the CMS Innovation Center (CMMI) to pilot "AI
Accelerators" that provide upfront technical assistance grants for training and workflow
integration.
2. Establish Interoperability Standards that ensure “data liquidity” is maintained for AI tools
to pull/push information across platforms with minimal technical barriers.

Administrative Influence on AI Adoption
Updated policy and regulation cannot drive AI integration in a vacuum. AI adoption in clinical
care is shaped by coordinated governance across clinical, technical, operational, and compliance
stakeholders. Administrative barriers, including fragmented oversight, procurement complexity,
regulatory requirements, and organizational change management, frequently present greater
challenges than technical limitations. Addressing these barriers through standardized governance
models, clear evaluation processes, and early multidisciplinary engagement will be critical to
advancing responsible and scalable AI adoption across healthcare organizations.
Adoption of new technology and processes is governed by a multidisciplinary decision structure
that balances patient safety, regulatory compliance, clinical effectiveness, operational feasibility,
and responsible data stewardship. Healthcare organizations typically require coordinated approval
across executive leadership, clinical governance bodies, technical authorities, compliance
functions, and operational stakeholders. Understanding these roles and the administrative barriers
they face is critical to enabling responsible, scalable AI adoption aligned with HHS priorities.
Key Decision Makers and Governing Bodies
• Clinical and Executive Leadership—including Chief Medical Officers (CMOs), Chief
Clinical or Nursing Officers (CCOs/CNOs), and executive leadership teams—determine
whether AI solutions align with clinical quality goals, workforce needs, patient safety
standards, and organizational risk tolerance. Clinical leadership endorsement is often the
most significant driver of institutional trust and adoption readiness.
• Information Technology, Data, and Security Leadership—including Chief Information
Officers (CIOs), Chief Digital or Analytics Officers, and Chief Information Security
Officers (CISOs)—serve as technical gatekeepers responsible for evaluating
interoperability with electronic health records, cybersecurity risks, infrastructure readiness,
and data governance compliance. Their approval is essential to implementation feasibility.
• Clinical Governance and Patient Safety Bodies—such as Clinical Informatics
Committees, Quality and Patient Safety Boards inclusive of patient advisory councils,
Clinical Decision Support governance groups, and Institutional Review Boards (IRBs)—
assess clinical evidence, bias mitigation strategies, monitoring requirements, and overall
patient risk. These bodies determine whether AI solutions meet standards for safe and
equitable clinical use.
• Finance, Compliance, and Acquisition Functions—including Chief Financial Officers
(CFOs), acquisition offices, legal counsel, and compliance teams—evaluate procurement
requirements, cost sustainability, reimbursement considerations, vendor risk, and
adherence to federal privacy and security regulations.

Primary Administrative Barriers to Adoption
Healthcare organizations face several administrative challenges that can delay AI deployment:
• Fragmented Governance and Oversight: Multiple committees and unclear approval
pathways can create lengthy evaluation cycles, particularly in organizations without formal
AI governance frameworks.
• Complex Regulatory and Compliance Requirements: Privacy protections, safety
oversight, liability considerations, and evolving regulatory expectations increase review
timelines prior to clinical use.
• Acquisition and Contracting Complexity: AI solutions introduce nontraditional
licensing models, data use agreements, and cybersecurity assessments that extend
procurement processes.
• Lack of Standardized Evaluation Frameworks: Organizations often lack consistent
methodologies for assessing clinical effectiveness, operational impact, and algorithm
performance across diverse patient populations.
• Operational and Change Management Demands: Implementation requires updates to
clinical workflows, policies, training programs, and documentation practices, which can
slow adoption without coordinated planning.
• Funding and Reimbursement Uncertainty: Misalignment between budgets, uncertain
reimbursement pathways, and competing organizational priorities can delay investment
decisions.
4.0 Reimbursement: Feedback on how HHS can approach reimbursement regulation to
drive AI adoption
One of the primary barriers to AI adoption and development is the uncertainty around funding and
reimbursement. To align payment policies with the goal of promoting high-value clinical AI,
fostering competition, and increasing affordability, the following key policy changes should be
considered:
• Establishing Predictable Reimbursement Pathways through proposals like Health Tech
Investment Act (S. 1399) which would require CMS to place qualifying AI services into a
"New Technology" Ambulatory Payment Classification (APC) for at least five years
and/or Transitional Add-on Payments, similar to the Transitional Drug Add-on Payment
(TDAP), which provide temporary extra funding for new AI tools, allowing CMS to gather
data on their value before incorporating them into permanent bundled payments
(“Medicare reimbursement pathway for AI-enabled medical devices considered in Senate's
Health Tech Investment Act”, 2025; Levins, 2022).
• Prevention of the “overuse" of risks associated with fee-for-service models by shifting
toward Value-Based and Outcome-Aligned Models such as the ACCESS Model and
continuing to encourage contracts where manufacturers share the financial risk. For
example, providers might receive rebates if an AI tool fails to deliver the promised clinical
or economic outcome.

Analysis of Reimbursement Challenges Facing AI Integration in Clinical Spaces
Many AI innovations improve or enhance workflows through workflow infrastructure (e.g.
automate components of a workflow) or as decision support (e.g. make prescriptive
recommendations). However, these capabilities don’t fit neatly into legacy health care billing
structures, which are traditionally designed around billable procedures and specific outcomes. For
example, an AI tool which assists in diagnosis or notes summarization may improve decision
support or save time, but that’s not a specific procedure or billable outcome. This misalignment
raises critical questions about reimbursement for AI-assisted or AI-enabled clinical functions.
Many AI tools provide indirect benefits, such as improving provider workflows or enhancing
decision-making, yet these contributions often involve broader infrastructure rather than discrete,
billable outcomes.
To address these complexities, health care leaders need to define clearer reimbursement
frameworks. Key considerations include establishing what aspects of AI are reimbursable—
such as AI as a service, AI-powered infrastructure, or AI-enabled outputs—while ensuring clarity
around safeguards like human oversight, documentation, and quality control. Additionally, it's
essential to outline standard criteria to justify reimbursement, which may involve demonstrating
measurable clinical value, operational efficiency, or patient safety improvements. Developing
a comprehensive checklist of dimensions for assessing AI reimbursement capabilities will be
crucial as the industry seeks to navigate this emerging area.
Payment models must evolve to reward measurable value rather than relying on traditional per-
use reimbursement structures. Value in health care should be defined by outcomes such as
improved patient care, enhanced clinical safety, or reduced administrative burden. For example, if
an AI-enabled tool significantly decreases diagnostic errors or reduces administrative workload,
these outcomes represent quantifiable improvements that could form the basis for reimbursement.
By incentivizing outcomes over isolated usage, payment models can more effectively align with
the capabilities and benefits of AI innovation. As AI continues to expand its role in health care,
these efforts will ensure its benefits are realized in a financially sustainable and equitable manner.

5.1 Research and Development: Recommendations for prioritization of AI research and
development subject areas
In addition to targeting policy changes for regulation, reimbursement, and oversight of AI adoption
and integration into clinical workflows, it is equally important to invest in strategic innovation to
address technical, operational, and ethical barriers. By aligning research priorities with the needs
of health care providers and patients, organizations can develop AI solutions that not only meet
policy requirements but also enhance practical usability and outcomes, paving the way for broader
and sustainable integration into health care systems.
There are several key areas of AI research that should be prioritized to support and accelerate the
adoption of AI in clinical care. These focus areas have been identified not only for their potential
to drive adoption, but also for their ability to strengthen trust, reduce uncertainty, and implement
guardrails for the responsible use of AI tools. These priorities are discussed below.
Criteria for AI prioritization. In addition to identifying application areas, it is crucial to define
criteria that guide AI prioritization. These criteria ensure that research and development efforts
align with core objectives for successful adoption. They include:
• Adoption: Facilitating widespread, rapid integration into clinical workflows with minimal
disruptions.
• Trust: Building confidence among clinicians, patients, and the public in the safety and
reliability and resiliency of AI solutions.
• Reducing uncertainty: Mitigating ambiguity around AI risks, outcomes, and
implementation challenges, including administrative and reimbursement questions.
• Guardrails: Establishing clear guidelines and safeguards to ensure ethical and responsible
use of AI technologies.
Research Target #1: Explainability for Accountability
HHS should prioritize research on AI systems that support clinical reasoning by making their
outputs interpretable and aligned with how clinicians make decisions in real-world settings. Care
providers are ultimately accountable for patient outcomes and must be able to understand,
validate and challenge AI generated care recommendations. AI systems are at risk of
conveying conclusions or diagnosis while not stating the confidence or communicating
uncertainty. AI should be able to clearly identify which patient features or clinical events drive a
recommendation in a way that is rooted in science and that the care providers can validate.
A multidisciplinary approach is essential in building healthcare AI to ensure safety, efficacy, and
ethical compliance, as AI models require the combined expertise of data scientists, clinicians,
patients, ethicists, and IT professionals to address complex patient data, regulatory requirements,
and technical challenges. HHS should ensure such collaboration is supported to prevent
algorithmic bias, build trust in clinical decisions, integrate AI into existing workflows, and
monitor, audit, and update AI models as medical knowledge advances (Quinn et al., 2020).
Further, HHS should support research into how accountability is shared and documented across
stakeholders and multidisciplinary teams (such as stakeholders, facilities, technical and medical

Research Target #2: Knowledge Management Capabilities
All data-driven approaches—from machine learning to generative AI to rules-based approaches
and retrieval—depend on what content is accessible to the model, how that content is captured
(e.g. database schema, unstructured document, etc.), and how it is stored. The success of any AI
model is directly tied to the quality and structure of the data it relies upon. Fragmented data
pipelines, such as siloed data sources, or unstructured, messy data, severely impair the
functionality and reliability of any AI model. Addressing these challenges is crucial to unlocking
the full potential of AI in clinical care. To this end, HHS should prioritize several key research
areas in knowledge management:
1. Digitalization, integration and structuring of content: One of the foundational priorities for
the effective adoption of AI in clinical care is the digitization and integration of health care
data into a structured format. Healthcare data exists in a myriad of formats, each with varying
levels of security and sensitivity. A focus on developing and implementing systems that
securely integrate health care content while adhering to rigorous security standards is vital for
building robust and reliable AI technology. This requires advancements in encryption,
regulatory safeguards, and collaboration between technology developers and regulatory
bodies. Work has been done in areas such as data security protocols, but further efforts are
needed to ensure sensitive health care data can be effectively stored and processed while
meeting privacy and compliance requirements.
2. Safe use of imperfect health care data: In real-world settings, health care data is often
incomplete, noisy, or reflective of biases, as seen in many EHR systems. While perfect data
may not always be attainable, HHS can prioritize research focused on the development of
methods to use imperfect data safely and effectively. Techniques such as data quality
assessments, validation protocols, and error detection frameworks can help mitigate risks
associated with unreliable or inconsistent datasets. Furthermore, AI systems can be designed
to operate with partial or fragmented information, with safeguards to prevent misinformation
or harmful conclusions.
3. Development of performance benchmarks and standards: Trust is one of the most
significant barriers to AI adoption in clinical care, and a lack of transparency regarding how
models perform only exacerbates uncertainty among users. Establishing clear performance
benchmarks and standards can address this issue by providing proof of performance that
users—whether clinicians, patients, or regulators—can rely upon. By supporting research to
develop standardized methods for measuring and reporting AI performance, HHS can ensure
consistent evaluation practices across AI solutions.

5.2 Review of Existing Literature on AI in Healthcare Settings
To best understand the most promising areas of Healthcare AI research and development, it is
important to review the academic landscape as it currently exists. A growing body of published
literature, including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and observational studies, has examined
how adopted AI tools influence clinical care, showcasing both potential benefits and emerging
risks.
One significant area of research into the benefits of AI in clinical spaces has surfaced evidence
showing that AI tools can achieve diagnostic performance comparable to human clinicians across
multiple medical domains. Takita et al. (2025) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis
comparing generative AI with physicians, finding that AI models performed similarly to clinicians
overall, though performance was slightly lower than expert-level physicians in some tasks.
Complementing this, McGenity et al. (2024) reviewed AI applications in digital pathology and
reported high diagnostic accuracy, with pooled sensitivity and specificity exceeding 90% across
diverse histopathological tasks. A growing collection of studies indicate that AI can serve as a
reliable adjunct to clinical judgment, supporting diagnostic decision-making while emphasizing
the need for continued validation and careful integration. Beyond diagnostic accuracy, studies have
also examined whether AI integration translates into tangible improvements in patient outcomes.

found that its use was associated with reductions in in-hospital mortality, length of stay, and 30-

healthcare (Wolff et al., 2020). Many studies report partial or isolated cost savings, and many
omit key costs such as implementation and maintenance.

than theoretical or pilot settings.

as a Medical Device (SaMD). While Wu et al. (2025) found that cost-utility and cost-effectiveness

licensing, maintenance fees, and app access costs from reporting. This inconsistency limits the
reliability of conclusions about AI’s true economic value and underscores the need for
standardized frameworks that capture all relevant costs associated with SaMD.
Recent literature also emphasizes equity, bias, and distributional concerns. It was found that
evaluations often fail to stratify results by demographic or socioeconomic subgroups, potentially
masking disparities in who benefits from AI and who may incur costs or harms (El Arab & Al

varied access to digital infrastructure, or unequal capacity to absorb training and integration costs
are underreported, highlighting the need for inclusive and context-sensitive economic frameworks
that consider both monetary and justice-related dimensions of AI adoption.
In summary, current literature approaches the costs and benefits of AI in clinical care through a
plurality of economic evaluation methods, with most evidence pointing to potential cost savings
and improved clinical value, especially when AI enhances diagnostic accuracy or streamlines care
delivery (Wolff et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2025). However, persistent methodological limitations (e.g.,
incomplete cost inclusion, static modeling), the early maturity of many technologies, and
underrepresentation of equity impacts, caution against simple cost-benefit conclusions. Robust,
transparent, and context-specific studies, especially those incorporating full cost components over
time and subgroup analyses, are essential for translating promising economic signals into reliable
guidance for policymakers, health systems, and payers (El Arab & Al Moosa, 2025).

6.1 AI Approach: Recommendations on how to support AI integration and responsible use

AI Evaluation and Oversight
The practical implementation of artificial intelligence (AI) tools in clinical care requires robust
evaluation methods to ensure effectiveness, reliability, and human-centered integration. Pre-
deployment assessments as well as post-deployment monitoring are both vital to ensuring AI
systems are trustworthy from the moment they enter the clinical workflow and continue to be over
time. Not only should evaluation focus on model function and accuracy, but it should also include
human centered metrics for usability, integration into workflows, and training. This multifaceted
approach paves the way for equitable and sustainable AI implementation across diverse healthcare
settings.
In pursuit of AI adoption in clinical care, leveraging standardized AI evaluation benchmark tests—
such as BIG-bench, Massive Multitask Language Understanding, GLUE and the Holistic
Evaluation of Language Models—serves as a powerful pre-deployment assessment strategy. These
tests provide valuable insights into the general reasoning, linguistic competence, and failure cases
of AI models and provide visibility into how the models follow instructions, conduct multi-step
reasoning, and ensure robustness across task types. AI models can be compared against each other
and human performance to identify which ones are worth investing in for specific use cases where
defined criteria are highly prioritized. A complete evaluation using these benchmark tests will
allow evaluators to assess where the model performs well and where performance is inadequate.
Pre-deployment robustness testing should be a high priority for HHS support as part of regulatory
policy. This includes perturbation testing and sensitivity analysis to minor data changes or missing
data. Models should be able to surface uncertainty and work with humans to review responses
when confidence is low. Before deployment, shadow runs and simulated deployment testing can
be very valuable in generating buy in with care providers without influencing care. Shadow runs
allow assessment of model decisions, output timing and error patterns, enabling gaps to be
identified and corrected before deployment.
Once deployed, AI systems must be continuously monitored to ensure that performance remains
stable as patient populations, medical knowledge, care practices and data evolve. Support for
evaluation approaches that enable early detection of model degradation and ensure timely updates
before patient care is compromised is important for ensuring long term success of AI tools. One
effective method to track post-deployment performance is input and output drift monitoring. Input
drift tracks shifts in patient demographics, diagnosis prevalence care provider patterns, and
documentation styles. This can be done using distributional distance metrics such as KL
divergence and maximum mean discrepancy. Distributional distance metrics measure how
different today’s data look compared to the data when the model was first deployed, providing
early signals when changes in patients, documentation, or workflows may threaten model
reliability—even before errors are visible in clinical outcomes. Output drift monitoring tracks
sudden changes such as an increase in high-risk flags or shift in recommendation thresholds.
Furthermore, humans should be constantly “in the loop” to ensure that the output drift reflects real
changes in care and not hallucinogenic spikes.

While it is important that AI tools are accurate, it is also important to ensure security, governance
and patient privacy and safety when implementing AI in healthcare settings. AI tools used in
clinical care which are considered non-medical devices (such as documentation assistants,
summarization tools, etc.) may still influence clinical judgement, prioritization, and work
processes in ways that create risks around patient safety, privacy, and accountability. These tools
often operate as infrastructure within workflows, which can place them outside traditional
regulatory categories. This creates risk in areas not well addressed by traditional compliance
frameworks.
• New legal and implementation issues introduced. AI tools can introduce legal and
implementation issues including influence on clinical decision-making, evolution in accuracy
over time (through model updates or retraining, which can impact repeatability or unexplained
differences in behavior), and probabilistic rather than deterministic operation. Existing
governance and accountability structures typically assume functionality which does not change
from one day to the next and assume deterministic outputs.
• Liability and accountability ambiguity. Non-device AI tools may shape decisions without
clear assignment of responsibility among clinician, health system, and vendor. This ambiguity
is amplified by automation bias and by AI outputs presented in persuasive or sycophantic
natural language.
• Indemnification and contracting gaps. Many organizations lack leverage or technical
capacity to negotiate appropriate vendor obligations related to model updates, monitoring,
incident response, and performance drift.
• Auditability and documentation limitations. AI tools may not log model versions, inputs,
outputs, or clinician interactions in a structured way, complicating quality assurance, root-
cause analysis, and legal defensibility. AI tools are often ‘black-box’, challenging auditability
requirements.
• Privacy and secondary data use concerns. Foundation model deployments raise uncertainty
around PHI handling, data retention, and whether patient data may be used for training or fine-
tuning, even when HIPAA compliance is asserted.
• Security risks from expanded integration. AI tools often require broad access to EHRs and
clinical systems, increasing the attack surface and introducing new vulnerabilities through
third-party services and model manipulation risks.
• Human factors risk (cognitive load, alert fatigue, automation bias). Even highly accurate
tools can increase clinician burden or encourage over-reliance when outputs are verbose,
confident, and timesaving. These risks are not captured by traditional performance metrics
alone. For example, if AI is implemented poorly, it can create more clicks, more alerts leading
to user fatigue and impeding adoption.

• Real-World Predictive Accuracy: While models often show "excellent" accuracy in
controlled trials, they frequently fail at the bedside. A March 2025 study found that
mortality-prediction models missed 66% of high-risk cases in real-world hospital settings
(Pias et al., 2025).
• Early Disease Detection (Sepsis): Despite massive investment, many sepsis-detection
models have underperformed. Research at the University of Michigan revealed a widely used
model was wrong more often than if it was right, missing two-thirds of actual cases while
triggering frequent false alarms (Lu et al., 2021).
• Complex Clinical Reasoning: Leading AI systems struggle when standard medical
questions are modified to include "none of the above" or require managing incomplete
information, with accuracy dropping by more than a third in some tests.
• Scalability and Workflow Friction: An estimated 80% to 95% of AI projects fail to scale
beyond the pilot phase. Failure often occurs not due to technical flaws but because tools
are not integrated into existing workflows, forcing clinicians to log into separate systems
and adding "friction" rather than reducing it.

• Bias and Cultural Nuance: Many models exhibit calibration drift or perform poorly for

• Failure to include patients in AI development: Healthcare has struggled to include
patients in AI development, often prioritizing technical, administrative, or efficiency goals
over user-centered design, leading to tools that disrupt clinical workflows and fail to
address actual patient needs. Key failures include opaque, non-inclusive development
processes, lack of patient input on, and inattention to, potential biases.
The use of AI in medical practices nearly doubled, from 38% in 2023 to 66% in 2024, highlighting
increased adoption of AI as an adjunct to patient care (American Medical Association, 2025).
However, providers are most comfortable with AI tools related to administration, such as billing,
transcription, discharge instructions, translation, research, and assistive diagnosis. A 2024 survey
of the American Medical Association (AMA) revealed that doctors want AI tools that save time,
improve accuracy, and strengthen patient care (AMA, 2025). In addition, they also want guarantees
such as transparency, oversight, and ethical guardrails. Integration is a key consideration. In this
survey, physicians emphasized the need for seamless EHR integration (84%), data privacy
assurances (87%), and feedback channels (88%) to drive adoption (AMA, 2025). There is a
recognized need to partner with AI not only for clinical input but for vendor liability when the AI
tool gets it wrong.
Furthermore, with respect specifically towards the use of LLMs for treatment, rather than
administrative purposes, research testing the use of LLMs for mental health treatment also raised
concerns for both privacy, safety, and for therapeutic outcomes (Moore et al., 2025). These
concerns included inappropriate chatbot responses to common and critical conditions as well as
expression of stigma towards mental health conditions (Moore et al., 2025). For this reason, in
most clinical settings, human interaction is still considered best practice for safe and adequate care.
While much of the discussion has been focused on the clinicians, administrators, vendors, and
regulators of AI, patient input into AI expectations is extremely underrepresented in research
(Moy et al, 2024; Nong & Ji, 2025). Studies, consisting mostly of smaller focus groups, have
investigated patient perspectives on AI use in healthcare and generally surfaced the following
priorities for AI:
1. That human oversight and decision making remains at the forefront of healthcare delivery.
2. That patients be involved in the AI development as stakeholders.
3. That the solutions be user friendly, easy to understand, and transparent in nature.
4. That data privacy remains of utmost importance.
(Foresman et al., 2025, Gundlack et al., 2025; Mikkelsen et al., 2023; Osnat, 2025)

many see the advantages of using these tools and hope they result in faster information delivery,

perspectives on patient engagement with AI in healthcare and found that patients felt strongly
about the importance of patient involvement in AI development as well as comprehensive AI
education. Inclusion of diverse demographics in AI development was emphasized in this focus

7.0 Conclusions
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has the potential to transform healthcare by enhancing efficiency,
reducing clinician burnout, improving diagnostic accuracy, and expanding access to underserved
populations. However, for this transformation to manifest, critical barriers must be addressed.
Trust by clinicians, patients, and administrators in the tools being used must be earned through
transparent algorithms, pre- and post-deployment monitoring, bias mitigation, clear standards and
regulatory requirement, and adoption of data governance frameworks for adoption, evaluation, and
maintenance. A comprehensive, human-centered approach that emphasizes safety, accountability,
equity, and long-term sustainability is essential to the successful integration of AI into clinical
workflows.
This paper identifies actionable strategies across the healthcare landscape to enable responsible
and scalable adoption of AI in healthcare. By transitioning to value-based payment models,
improving data interoperability, fostering public-private collaborations, and implementing robust
evaluation frameworks, AI can be integrated in ways that align with clinician trust, patient safety,
and organizational priorities. Additionally, emphasizing human-in-the-loop design principles,
transparency protocols, and dynamic post-deployment monitoring provides critical safeguards to
mitigate risks such as automation bias, performance drift, and inequities in patient care as well as
common issues with LLMs and hallucination.
The path forward must prioritize alignment across stakeholders, including clinicians, patients,
vendors, and policymakers, to ensure that AI tools meet the diverse and evolving needs of the
healthcare system. As AI continues to advance, the U.S. healthcare system has an opportunity to
leverage its transformative capabilities while mitigating risks to achieve improved health outcomes
at lower costs. Through strategic action, public-private collaboration, and innovative governance
mechanisms, AI can drive solutions to longstanding challenges such as workforce shortages,
systemic inefficiencies, and disparities in care.

innovation, LMI stands ready to partner with HHS and other stakeholders to shape the future of
healthcare.
