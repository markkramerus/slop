Executive Summary and Key Recommendations
We commend HHS for launching a “OneHHS” strategy on AI that explicitly links regulation,
reimbursement, and research & development, and for seeking experience‑based feedback from those
building and deploying AI in clinical care. From our vantage point as a leading post‑acute EHR and
interoperability platform, we see AI’s potential to improve care transitions, reduce avoidable
hospitalizations, and alleviate workforce burden, particularly in LTPAC and Medicaid‑serving settings.
To realize that potential and support HHS’s three major levers, we recommend HHS prioritize five
cross‑cutting actions:
1. Clarify regulatory expectations for non‑device AI in clinical workflows
Establish a unified, risk‑proportionate framework for AI used in triage, summarization,
documentation assistance, risk stratification, and other non‑device decision support. This
should include clear guidance on risk categories, clinician oversight, liability allocation,
documentation and audit expectations, and permissible development and deployment
practices under HIPAA and other HHS authorities, aligned with ONC’s decision support
transparency concepts.
2. Align reimbursement with measurable outcomes and burden reduction
Modernize payment policy so that providers deploying validated AI tools that demonstrably
improve outcomes, reduce avoidable utilization, or reduce documentation burden can share in
the value created. This could include add‑on payments, shared‑savings recognition, or quality
measure credit within Medicare fee‑for‑service and value‑based programs, including CMMI
demonstrations targeted to post‑acute and senior care.
3. Support a real‑world AI evaluation “playbook” and shared benchmarks
Promote standardized, human‑centered evaluation methods, including safety, drift, bias, and
workflow impact tailored to LTPAC, SNF, and senior living environments. HHS can seed
evaluation toolkits, shared reference datasets, and benchmark measures through grants,
cooperative agreements, and public–private testbeds that smaller providers and vendors can
use to assess AI tools consistently.
4. Advance pragmatic interoperability and data quality for AI readiness
Prioritize USCDI and USCDI+ data elements and FHIR‑based exchanges most relevant to
post‑acute and senior care (e.g., real‑time ADT, medication, functional and cognitive status, and
social risk data) and ensure LTPAC providers are included in FHIR API and TEFCA
implementation efforts. Pair this with minimum data quality expectations and clear guidance
on data provenance so AI models have reliable inputs across care settings.

Through CMMI models, NIH and AHRQ partnerships support testbeds that evaluate AI’s impact

Why Post‑Acute AI Policy Requires Distinct Consideration
Digital and interoperability starting point
Post‑acute and long‑term care providers were largely excluded from the federal HITECH incentive
framework that accelerated EHR adoption in hospitals and physician practices. As a result, skilled
nursing facilities (SNFs), assisted living communities, and other LTPAC providers entered the modern
interoperability era without the same level of federal capital support, technical assistance, or
infrastructure standardization. Many post‑acute organizations have since achieved significant digital
maturity, yet interoperability gaps, particularly around real‑time ADT notifications, structured
laboratory feeds, and standardized functional and cognitive status data, remain more pronounced than
in acute care.
AI policy that assumes uniform digital infrastructure across settings risks unintentionally reinforcing
these disparities. To meet the goals of the Health Sector AI RFI, a “OneHHS AI” strategy must explicitly
account for the distinct starting point of LTPAC providers and ensure that data standards, certification
pathways, payment models, and technical assistance all include post‑acute environments.
Financing and workforce realities
Financing and workforce realities further differentiate post‑acute care from other sectors. Medicaid is
the dominant payer for long‑term care services, and reimbursement rates often operate on thin
margins that limit flexibility for capital investment. Unlike many hospital systems, most SNFs and senior
care providers are not positioned to absorb significant upfront technology costs without clear, aligned
incentives.
The workforce structure in post‑acute settings, characterized by interdisciplinary teams led by medical
directors and heavily supported by licensed practical nurses, certified nursing assistants, and care
coordinators, shapes how AI must be designed and governed. Tools that assume constant physician
presence, always‑on specialist coverage, or large internal analytics teams may not translate effectively
into SNF environments. AI policy must therefore reflect staffing composition, scope‑of‑practice

avoid widening existing gaps in quality and equity.
Responses to Specific Questions
1. Biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care
In summary, the biggest barriers we observe in post‑acute and senior care are: (1) regulatory ambiguity
for non‑device AI, (2) misaligned payment incentives, (3) fragmented interoperability and state‑level
variation, and (4) capital and workforce constraints that limit the ability of LTPAC providers to evaluate
and govern AI tools.
First, regulatory uncertainty surrounding non‑device AI tools creates hesitation among providers. Many
AI‑enabled tools, such as readmission prediction models, documentation assistance systems, and risk
stratification tools, do not meet the definition of regulated medical devices but meaningfully influence
clinical workflows and reimbursement. Providers remain uncertain about how FDA, ONC, CMS, and
state regulators will interpret the use of these tools in audits, surveys, and enforcement contexts. In
risk‑averse post‑acute environments, ambiguity leads to defensive non‑adoption.
Second, payment incentives are frequently misaligned. Under fee‑for‑service Medicare, providers bear
the costs of AI implementation while savings from avoided hospitalizations or improved coordination
often accrue primarily to payers. Although AI adoption is more financially rational in capitated or
shared‑savings models (such as certain risk-bearing entities or CMMI arrangements), many post‑acute
providers remain outside such arrangements.
Third, fragmented interoperability and incomplete data flows constrain both model development and
deployment. Post‑acute providers were historically excluded from earlier federal health IT incentive
programs, and access to structured laboratory data, medication feeds, and real‑time ADT notifications
remains inconsistent. AI systems depend on reliable, cross‑setting data inputs; underrepresentation of
LTPAC and dual‑eligible populations in training datasets also limits generalizability.
Fourth, state‑level fragmentation presents an additional barrier. Because post‑acute care is heavily
regulated by state survey agencies and Medicaid programs, variation in privacy interpretations,
documentation standards, and oversight practices complicates multi‑state AI deployment. Federal
coordination and model guidance could reduce this variability while preserving state authority.
Finally, capital and workforce constraints are particularly acute in SNF and senior care environments.
Many facilities operate on thin margins and lack internal data science resources to independently
evaluate model performance, assess bias, or monitor drift. Without structured evaluation support,
providers may either avoid adoption or deploy tools without sufficient governance.

2. Regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes HHS should prioritize
HHS should prioritize development of a unified, risk‑proportionate framework for non‑device AI used
in clinical workflows. This framework should clarify expectations for clinician oversight, documentation
integrity, transparency when AI assists in drafting clinical notes or generating predictive insights, and
permissible training data practices under HIPAA. It should also reinforce expectations for bias testing
and equity impact assessment consistent with civil rights obligations and align with ONC’s emerging
transparency requirements for decision support interventions.
Rather than broad liability protections, HHS could consider structured accountability pathways when
validated AI tools are deployed within defined governance frameworks and with documented clinician
review. For example, guidance could clarify that AI‑assisted documentation where clinicians retain final
review and attestation does not by itself constitute fraudulent intent, while still requiring appropriate
monitoring and audit trails. Predictable oversight encourages responsible innovation without
weakening safeguards.
On the payment side, CMS should explore mechanisms that recognize validated AI‑enabled
interventions within value‑based programs such as the SNF Value‑Based Purchasing Program and
related Quality Reporting Programs. Transitional, time‑limited innovation add‑on payments,
shared‑savings recognition, or quality measure credit may help ensure adoption is not limited to large
risk‑bearing entities. Any payment recognition should be tied to measurable outcome improvement,
such as reduced avoidable hospitalizations or improved functional outcomes and include guardrails to
prevent inappropriate coding or utilization changes.
Cross‑agency coordination among ONC, CMS, FDA, and OCR would further reduce conflicting signals
and streamline expectations for developers and providers. A coordinated “AI in Clinical Workflows”
council or task force could align regulatory guidance, transparency expectations, and payment models
under the broader HHS AI Strategy.
3. Novel legal and implementation issues for non‑medical device AI and HHS’s role
Non‑device AI raises complex questions regarding responsibility allocation between developers and
providers, documentation expectations, audit discoverability, and contractual risk allocation.
Clarification is needed regarding how clinician review should be documented when AI assists in
drafting content, and how providers should approach governance and monitoring responsibilities over
time.
HHS can support responsible implementation by publishing model governance frameworks and
encouraging transparency in data stewardship practices, including disclosure of secondary model
training uses. Providers should understand how their data may be used to refine or commercialize
models and what safeguards apply.
AI‑specific cybersecurity risks also warrant attention. Model manipulation, prompt injection, and
third‑party dependency vulnerabilities represent evolving risks that should be incorporated into
existing health sector cybersecurity guidance and incident response expectations.

Implementation challenges are particularly acute for smaller and independent SNFs that lack in‑house
data science or advanced IT teams. Many facilities do not have the capacity to independently validate
models, audit for bias, or design governance processes. Standardized evaluation templates, shared
reference datasets, and technical assistance models tailored to LTPAC settings would help ensure
consistent safeguards and reduce “defensive non‑adoption.”
4. Evaluation methods, metrics, robustness testing, and human‑centered evaluation
Evaluation should scale to risk: assistive documentation tools require different oversight than
predictive triage systems or deterioration alerts that may influence clinical intervention. Outside of the
medical device context, there is currently no widely adopted U.S. standard for risk categorization of
non-device clinical AI tools, even as other jurisdictions develop risk-based governance approaches.
HHS could promote consistency by articulating a clear risk taxonomy for non-device AI used in clinical
care, such as high‑, medium‑, and low‑impact categories, to proportionally scale evaluation depth,
documentation expectations, and post-deployment monitoring requirements.
In doing so, HHS may wish to consider established international risk frameworks, including the impact-
based approach by the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) in its Software as a
Medical (SaMD) guidance, which stratefies oversight based on the significance of information provided
and the state of the healthcare situation. While non-device AI tools may fall outside formal SaMD
scopre, the IMDRF model provides a structured way to assess clinical impact and could serve as a
conceptual reference point. Other quantitative and qualitative approaches, such as impact scoring
based on workflow dependency, automation level, or potential oversight model. Referencing an
established risk framework would promote clarity and alignment without creating unnecessary
duplicative regulatory structure.
Because many post-acute providers lack internal analytic resources, HHS could fund development of
standardized evaluation playbooks, metric sets, and benchmarking tools tailored to LTPAC
environments through grants, cooperative agreements, and prize competitions. These toolkits would
help small SNF operators interpret performance metrics, assess impact categories, ask more informed
questions of vendors, and frame governance decisions around safety, bias, and reliability.
5. Supporting private sector accreditation, certification, and credentialing
Expansion of voluntary certification frameworks addressing AI transparency, explainability, risk
management, and monitoring would promote consistency across vendors. The ONC Health IT
Certification Program already incorporates required transparency criteria for decision support
interventions in certified EHR technology; this framework provides a useful foundation that could be
extended or adapted to address emerging AI-specific governance considerations such as model
monitoring, drift management, and documentation of training data provenance. Coordination with
accrediting bodies could further integrate AI governance readiness into organizational standards so
that oversight expectations are aligned across technology developers and provider organizations.

Additionally, clinician AI literacy should become part of continuing education expectations. Licensed
professionals must understand model limitations, potential bias, appropriate override practices, and
documentation responsibilities. HHS should work with licensing boards and professional societies to
integrate core AI literacy into continuing education and competency frameworks so that AI augments,
not replaces, licensed staffing requirements or professional judgment.
6. Where AI has met or fallen short; high‑potential tools
AI has demonstrated promise in readmission risk prediction, documentation summarization, and
medication reconciliation when embedded directly into workflows. In high‑volume SNF environments,
documentation summarization tools can reduce chart review time and allow reallocation of nursing
attention to resident care.
However, predictive tools that lack explainability or workflow integration often fail to produce
measurable impact. Standalone dashboards or alerts that are not aligned with existing team processes
or care pathways have limited real‑world value and can contribute to alert fatigue.
High‑potential areas for post‑acute and senior care include deterioration prediction, behavioral health
and cognitive status monitoring, automated quality measure abstraction, and AI‑assisted
patient‑to‑setting matching across acute and post‑acute transitions. HHS can help ensure that
evidence from these use cases is captured and linked to payment and regulatory policies that reward
proven value.
7. Decision makers, governing bodies, and administrative hurdles
In SNF and senior care settings, adoption decisions for AI‑supported tools are typically led by medical
directors, chief or director‑level nursing leaders, and IT leaders (such as a VP of IT or CIO), with
compliance, privacy, and finance leaders playing critical review roles. Governance bodies such as QAPI
committees often play a central role in evaluating and monitoring AI tools once deployed.
The primary administrative hurdles are the cross‑functional coordination required security and privacy
reviews, legal and contracting cycles, clinician training, workflow redesign, and change management,
layered on top of capital constraints and uncertainty about how regulators and surveyors will interpret
AI‑supported documentation and workflows. Model contracting templates, governance guidance, and
clear survey expectations would reduce friction and promote consistent safeguards.
8. Interoperability to widen market opportunities, fuel research, and accelerate AI
AI scalability depends on improved interoperability across the care continuum. Real‑time ADT feeds,
structured medication and laboratory data, harmonized functional and cognitive assessments and
standardized social needs data are critical inputs for predictive and care coordination tools. When
these data elements are incomplete or delayed at transitions between hospitals and post-acute care
settings, AI models lose reliability and care coordination suffers.
Expansion of USCDI and USCDI+ to reflect post‑acute and senior care data elements will strengthen
model accuracy and ensure inclusion of Medicaid‑serving populations. Inclusion of LTPAC providers

and their vendors in FHIR‑based API initiatives and TEFCA implementation is essential to ensure
cross‑setting data completeness. We encourage HHS to ensure that TEFCA exchange purposes, FHIR
API policies, and USCDI expansion explicitly include LTPAC‑relevant use cases so that cross‑setting AI

In addition to transactional interoperability, HHS could strengthen AI readiness through development
of controlled-access, de-identified reference datasets designed specifically for research, validation, and
benchmarking in post-acute and safety-net populations. These datasets should be structured to
support model evaluation and fairness testing, rather than commercial product replication, and should
include longitudinal cross-setting indicators such as Admission/Discharge/Transfer (ADT) events,
standardized assessment variables, medication categories, and outcome measures (e.g.,
rehospitalization, falls, pressure injuries) with consistent documentation of data provenance and
limitations.
Conceptually, this approach could resemble CMS Limited Data Set (LDS) files in its use of HIPAA-
compliant de-identification and governed data use agreements, but be purpose-built for AI validation
in LTPAC populations. Access should be tiered, subject to appropriate data use agreements, and
focused on benchmarking and methodological transparency rather than real-time operational
deployment. Such infrastructure would help smaller providers, researchers, and emerging vendors
assess subgroup performance, compare outcomes across representative populations, and strengthen
model accountability, while preserving the proprietary, workflow-integrated datasets that drive real-
world implementation and care delivery.
By clearly distinguishing benchmarking infrastructure from operational data exchange, HHS can
promote equity, reduce duplication of effort, and lower barriers to responsible AI development
without disrupting the competitive and innovation dynamics that enable vendors and providers to
invest in advanced data integration and care transformation.
9. Patient and caregiver priorities and concerns
Patients and caregivers in post‑acute and senior care settings prioritize fewer avoidable
hospitalizations, earlier detection of clinical decline, improved medication safety, and more clinician
time at the bedside. They want AI to help coordinate care across facilities and providers, reduce
paperwork, and make it easier to navigate complex care plans.
Concerns focus on privacy and security, transparency, potential algorithmic bias, and overreliance on
automation. There is legitimate worry that AI systems might treat older adults, people with disabilities,
or individuals with complex social needs unfairly or opaquely.
HHS can promote trust by encouraging plain‑language AI disclosures and embedding patient and
caregiver perspectives into evaluation and oversight frameworks—for example, through advisory
panels, user testing, or public comment on key guidance. Embedding these perspectives will help
ensure that AI tools respond to real‑world concerns about dignity, autonomy, and safety.

10. Priority AI research areas and literature considerations
HHS should prioritize research in LTPAC populations focusing on functional outcomes, falls,
readmissions, pressure injuries, medication‑related harm, and implementation science in
resource‑constrained environments. Research should also examine how AI can support workforce
augmentation and burden reduction without reducing necessary staffing or eroding human
relationships in care.
Emerging literature suggests that AI tools can reduce readmissions, improve early detection of
deterioration, and increase documentation efficiency when properly validated and embedded in
workflows, but that performance often degrades when models are deployed outside the populations
or settings in which they were trained. This reinforces the need for local validation, fairness testing,
and ongoing monitoring, particularly in Medicaid and dual‑eligible populations.
These research priorities can be advanced through targeted NIH and AHRQ funding calls and CMMI
demonstration models that include post‑acute and Medicaid‑serving providers as full partners, not just
downstream data sources.
Conclusion
Artificial intelligence can strengthen quality, reduce burden, and support value‑based care across the
continuum. In post‑acute and senior care settings, success will depend on regulatory clarity and
risk‑proportionate oversight, payment alignment that rewards measurable improvements,
interoperability inclusion and data quality, standardized evaluation support, transparent data
stewardship, and strong governance that maintains human oversight and protects equity.
PCC welcomes continued engagement with HHS to ensure AI improves care for older adults, people
with disabilities, and Medicaid‑serving populations in ways that are safe, equitable, and accountable.
We stand ready to partner with HHS and its agencies through pilots, testbeds, and advisory efforts to
help make the HHS AI Strategy and Health Sector AI RFI goals a reality.
