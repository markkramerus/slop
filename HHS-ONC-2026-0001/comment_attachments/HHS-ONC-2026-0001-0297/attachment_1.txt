Response to the Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial
Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care (RIN 0955–AA13)
Thank you for the opportunity to provide comments on the Request for Information: Accelerating
the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care. As one of the nation’s
leading academic health systems, we appreciate the opportunity to provide comments as the
administration considers opportunities to promote AI innovation and adoption in clinical care.
Duke Health integrates the Duke University Health System (all of our hospitals, clinics, and the
Duke Health Integrated Practice), Duke University School of Medicine, Duke-NUS Medical School,
Duke University School of Nursing, and incorporates the health and health research programs
within the Duke Global Health Institute as well as those in schools and centers across Duke
University, including the Duke Robert J. Margolis Institute for Health Policy.
Our organization is committed to excellence through the adoption of AI innovations to improve
patient outcomes, reduce administrative burden, and streamline care delivery. To achieve this, we
established the Algorithm-Based Clinical Decision Support (ABCDS) Oversight Committee in
2020. This committee governs and reviews AI solutions for patient care. At that time, AI quality and
ethical considerations were not widely understood or adopted by stakeholders, and FDA guidance
was still under development. Over the past five years, we have worked to balance innovation with
high-quality, ethical care, ensuring responsible deployment of AI solutions.
Based on our experience, Duke Health offers the following considerations for promoting AI
innovation and adoption in clinical care:
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption
and use in clinical care?
Currently, significant variation in legislation across states creates barriers to widespread
adoption. A shared federal legal and regulatory framework, developed in partnership with
states, would help reduce inconsistencies and minimize the compliance burden on companies
that would otherwise need to meet different requirements in each state.

HHS can play a critical role by establishing greater transparency requirements for AI solution
testing, validation, and implementation practices. Building on the transparency elements
recommended in HTI-1, additional guidance related to AI workflow integration and
decision‑making based on model outputs could serve as a practical playbook for healthcare
organizations. Such transparency standards would help increase trust, provide operational
clarity, and ultimately drive broader adoption of AI tools in healthcare settings.
Incentives are needed for both healthcare organizations and end users of AI solutions to adopt,
purchase, and maintain these technologies. Strengthening infrastructure readiness will also be
essential to support successful implementation. (See below)
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or
programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care? Please
provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
AI has significant potential to improve health outcomes for all Americans. However,
determining which AI solutions are truly effective remains a core challenge. Because AI models
are fundamentally data‑driven, their performance can vary substantially across health systems
due to differences in patient populations, care delivery patterns, and data quality. Ongoing
monitoring is therefore essential: only through real‑world evaluation can organizations
determine whether a given AI solution improves outcomes in their specific clinical environment.
Yet many healthcare organizations lack the necessary expertise to implement, validate, and
monitor AI tools effectively. They may also lack the technical infrastructure required to deploy
and maintain these algorithms at scale. Incentives that build organizational readiness for
responsible AI adoption are critical. These could include grants from HRSA, ASTP/ONC, and
the CMS Innovation Center to support AI readiness assessments, workforce development, and
infrastructure modernization. Additionally, a national AI technical assistance program—
modeled after ONC’s Health IT Regional Extension Centers—could provide hands‑on support to
organizations that currently lack in‑house expertise.
Today, each health system evaluates AI solutions largely in isolation, determining for itself
whether a tool is effective and whether its benefits outweigh its risks. A national approach would
reduce this duplication of effort. National registries could facilitate information sharing across
healthcare organizations by aggregating real‑world evidence on AI model performance, clinical
effectiveness, and safety risks. Such a system would accelerate learning, support continuous
model improvement, and promote the adoption of AI tools with demonstrated benefit.

HHS could help lead this effort by establishing a federated national AI registry, supported by
CMS and ASTP, to collect standardized, privacy‑preserving performance metrics.1 Participation
could be required for AI tools used in federally funded care settings to ensure broad coverage
and transparency. Duke Health, for example, is co‑developing an information‑sharing platform
with Avanade called SAIGE that will enable organizations to share data on which AI solutions
are deployed and which provide meaningful clinical value. This type of infrastructure could
serve as a foundation for a national registry model.
The health care delivery system currently lacks appropriate incentive structures to support the
adoption and sustained use of AI‑based tools. Today, the return on investment for many AI
solutions is driven primarily by staff time savings; return on health is not easily translated into
return on investment, which, in turn, can make it difficult for organizations to justify investment.
Payment models that recognize and reward the use of AI tools demonstrated to improve clinical
quality, patient outcomes, or system efficiency would create a more durable incentive for
adoption. Aligning reimbursement with outcomes would enable hospitals to invest in AI as a
mechanism for improving care delivery rather than as a cost‑cutting measure.
3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel legal and
implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures (e.g.,
relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
Looking ahead, the emergence of next‑generation AI systems—particularly agentic AI—raises
new legal and operational challenges. When healthcare professionals are partially or fully out
of the decision‑making loop, accountability for errors becomes ambiguous. Robust evaluation
methods rooted in both automation safety and human‑factors engineering will be essential to
assess the outputs, behaviors, and failure modes of agentic AI. These approaches can help
ensure that automated clinical actions remain predictable, effective, safe, and aligned with
clinical standards, even as systems become more autonomous.
4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and
postdeployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered
evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so, which
mechanisms would be most impactful (e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or
prize competitions)?
Because AI solutions can perform differently across healthcare organizations, both
pre‑deployment and post‑deployment evaluation as well as ongoing monitoring should be
supported and encouraged. To further promote the use of effective AI, HHS could consider

In addition, HHS could offer shared‑savings bonuses for organizations that implement AI tools

5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, industry
driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
Alignment around technical standards and risk frameworks is essential to promote consistent,
safe, and scalable adoption of AI in clinical care. Clear federal support for widely recognized
technical and governance standards—such as structured AI lifecycle management, transparency
requirements, and post‑deployment monitoring practices—would help reduce duplication of
effort across health systems and provide greater regulatory clarity for both developers and
providers.
In parallel, targeted investment in workforce education and re‑skilling is critical. Many
healthcare organizations currently lack the clinical, technical, and operational expertise needed
to implement, validate, and continuously monitor AI tools in real‑world settings. Federal
support for training programs, technical assistance, and AI‑focused workforce development
would increase trust among clinicians and administrators, strengthen organizational readiness,
and ensure that AI tools are used appropriately, safely, and in ways that deliver measurable
clinical value.
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the
greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help reduce
costs?
Ambient listening solutions have reduced the amount of time clinicians spend on documentation,
and patients report that these tools improve the quality of patient‑provider interactions. But for
many AI tools, it is too early to assign an “exceeded” or “fallen short” label on their
implementation as health systems are in early stages of evaluating new tools. Examples include
computer vision with AI models to aid in prevention of falls from a hospital bed and prevention
of pressure ulcers. The same computer vision technology is likely to support workflow
modification to improve efficiencies of acute care delivery, perioperative operations, Emergency
Department operations, and the patient and staff experience in all these services.
7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations have the
most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary administrative
hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?

A key driver of successful AI adoption is ensuring that each AI solution addresses a clearly
defined unmet clinical need. To achieve this, stakeholders across service lines and departments
should be engaged early in the selection process, including the build–buy–partner decision.
Governance bodies can play an essential coordinating role by guiding these decisions,
establishing clear accountability for AI deployment, and promoting transparency around which
AI solutions provide measurable benefit.
End‑user champions can further support adoption by coordinating pilot projects, training peers,
and educating potential users on the benefits of each AI tool as well as strategies for managing
associated risks. To cultivate a culture of innovation and support AI scaling, organizations can
designate AI ambassadors embedded across clinics who help facilitate adoption, provide
hands‑on support, and organize communication campaigns or events to raise awareness of the
value and appropriate use of AI solutions.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and
accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data types, data
standards, and benchmarking tools.
Currently, hospitals using electronic health record (EHR) systems have fairly good integration
of clinical data. However, there is a significant gap in integration of clinical data between
organizations not using the same EHR system. This has significant safety implications when
patients transfer between organizations. It also has a significant impact on the ability to screen
patients for clinical trial options if their health records are stored in a different EHR system. AI
is increasingly performative in finding the right patient for the right trial. However, without true
clinical data integration, that is very difficult to automate.
9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the
adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers have
related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
There is significant opportunity for AI to address patient education and monitoring in the home
setting. Patients with life threatening or chronic diseases can find it hard to absorb information
in the clinic setting. Providing direct to patient education tools that can be used and interactive
in the home setting gives patients the ability to digest information in a way that is meaningful to
them. Increased education and understanding of their disease can lead to better symptom
monitoring and treatment plan adherence.
10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the adoption of
AI as part of clinical care?
a. Are there published findings about the impact of adopted AI tools and their use clinical care?

b. How does the literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI as part of
clinical care?
Peer-reviewed publications are not consistent sources of organizations reporting outcomes and
risks of AI solutions, especially failures of AI solution implementation. See question 2 on AI
national registries.
Specific areas of research that can be considered for future funding are:
• Automated evaluation methods for AI solutions (agentic AI, LLM, predictive AI)
• Scalable frameworks for AI management - infrastructure recommendations through clinical
practice learnings
• New Robust AI methodologies
