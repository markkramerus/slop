Overview: AI and Healthcare
For decades, AI has been a valuable tool in healthcare, recognizing patterns that help guide clinical
research, care delivery, medical supply chains and more. As more sophisticated machine-learning
algorithms and generative AI have emerged, human elements of healthcare can be augmented even
further to serve patients and communities. AI-enabled capabilities have the potential to sharpen the
accuracy of diagnoses, improve clinical decision-making, detect public health threats, and enable
earlier disease intervention, with applications ranging from administrative tasks to operational
efficiencies and predictive analytics for disease outbreaks. Despite these promising advancements,

Barriers and Burdens to Remove
HLC and the Confidentiality Coalition strongly support the removal of unnecessarily burdensome
requirements that restrict the development and deployment of AI; we share your goal of enabling the
growth of private-sector AI innovation and advancements to improve health. The emerging
patchwork of regulations at the federal and state level is, perhaps, the most significant threat. Private
sector companies currently navigate complex and potentially conflicting regulatory and legal
frameworks pertaining to data science use in healthcare and beyond. Additionally, there is
uncertainty regarding best practices and the risk of a state-by-state patchwork emerging without
federal guidance. The myriad requirements create confusion and inconsistencies that stifle
innovation, increase compliance burdens, and hinder the seamless adoption of evidence-based AI-
driven advancements – underscoring the essential need for a clear, cohesive federal framework to
provide regulatory certainty.
To harness the full potential of AI, the U.S. healthcare system—public and private sectors, and
regulators—must act together to enable progress. A unified federal approach to regulation is needed
to harmonize expectations, reduce ambiguity, and give organizations the predictability to adopt AI
responsibly.
Developed by cross-sectoral AI subject matter experts representing nearly 30 companies across the
healthcare ecosystem, this report outlines barriers to uniform AI adoption in the U.S. and delivers
targeted policy recommendations to address them. It urgently calls for a clear, forward-looking
national standard that defines AI and establishes what responsible governance must entail.
The Report (detailed in Addendum 3) identifies three key barriers to adoption and nine policy
recommendations:
BARRIER: Governance and Regulatory Complexity
Inconsistent and intricate regulations impede AI integration at scale.
Policy Recommendations:
• Centralize Legislation: Establish a comprehensive federal policy baseline, ensuring risk
based oversight and human-in-the-loop, to harmonize and preempt conflicting state laws.
• Modernize Regulations: Update key regulatory frameworks such as the Health Insurance
Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) to reflect today’s digital reality.
• Clarify Accountability: Define accountability for AI outputs across all stakeholders.
• Ensure Liability Protection: Create clear legal frameworks and safe harbor provisions for
responsible use of AI for patient care.

BARRIER: Data and Infrastructure
Issues surrounding data access and privacy dissuade widespread adoption.
Policy Recommendations:
• Fortify Data Readiness, Accessibility, and Sharing: Strengthen national standards for data
quality, security, interoperability, and AI-ready data access.
• Establish Transparency Standards: Set federal standards for clear disclosure of AI
capabilities, limitations, and ongoing monitoring.
• Mitigate Bias: Create federal standards to reduce bias in healthcare AI through
representative data, ongoing output monitoring, and mandatory corrective action.
• Standardize Terminology: Develop universal terminology for what constitutes “AI use” that
bridges technical and healthcare-specific contexts across research, clinical, policy, and
operational domains.
BARRIER: Capabilities and End User Trust
Skill gaps and trust among users inhibit effective AI implementation.
Policy Recommendation:
• Enhance Workforce Training and Education: Embed AI literacy and ethics into K–12
education and upskill healthcare professionals through AI-focused education and training.

Addendum #1
Principles for the Responsible Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare
Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly evolving, and improving healthcare delivery, augmenting research
and development across drugs and medical devices, aiding diagnoses, assisting intricate
procedures, streamlining administrative tasks, and identifying individuals who could benefit from
additional health services. AI’s full potential in healthcare can only be achieved with collaboration
between private and public sector stakeholders to foster continued innovation. Our members have
collaboratively developed the principles below to guide a policy framework governing the use of AI in
the healthcare field.
Principles
Benefits and Innovation. The use of AI tools in healthcare lowers costs, reduces human error,
increases access to care, augments existing clinical practices, alleviates clerical and administrative
burden, and improves health outcomes. For example, AI enables review and translation of
mammograms thirty times faster than human review with 99% accuracy and reduces the need for
unnecessary biopsies.1 The benefits of existing tools represent only the first step in realizing AI’s
potential to revolutionize healthcare and patient outcomes. It is therefore essential that regulators,
stakeholders, and AI experts work together to ensure any regulation or policy takes a risk-based,
patient centered approach that supports and nurtures developing AI technology innovations, while
recognizing industry-specific differences and needs.
Risk-based Approach. Regulatory agencies and organizations using AI should adopt a risk-based
oversight approach to AI applications. Risk assessments should be tailored to use cases and should
be entity-dependent assuming there is a baseline, agreed-upon floor to align with consensus-based
risk management frameworks such as the AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) developed by
the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). Oversight processes adopted by
organizations using AI should routinely identify potential risks, possible mitigation strategies, and
recommended uses for the tool(s). Regulators should avoid imposing duplicative compliance
requirements, or those that conflict with existing requirements, that create an unworkable operating
environment and consider organizations that follow a framework such as the NIST AI RMF to be
adopting best practices. It is important to permit flexibility in assessing and allaying risk to enable
companies to tailor risk mitigation to the unique considerations of each AI use case - as opposed to
prescriptive requirements to guide AI oversight.
Federal Standards. Any regulatory framework(s) for AI applications should be developed and
applied at the federal level, while reducing regulatory compliance costs and creating a safer
environment for long-term investment. A single national standard that preempts state laws in this
area will avoid conflicting, patchwork requirements and facilitate compliance without unduly
restricting innovation.
Privacy and Security. Personal information used in AI should be subject to robust privacy and
security protections at the federal level. This involves adhering to existing health data privacy and
security protections in the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) and
applying equivalent protections for non-HIPAA health data, by establishing baseline privacy
protections for the processing of non-HIPAA personal information that often comprise the datasets
used to train, validate, and test AI models. Privacy considerations should be integrated into AI tools

from the start to reflect the importance of data minimization and use limitations. Individuals should
have the right to be informed about the collection and use of their personal information, and the right
to access, correct and, if feasible, delete their personal information.
Congress should establish a single national standard for the use of personal information not already
subject to HIPAA that encompasses use in AI applications by entitles also not regulated by HIPAA.
Security safeguards, which may be based on guidelines such as those provided in the NIST
Cybersecurity Framework and RMF, should protect against data breaches, data poisoning,
exfiltration of models or training data and other threats that could expose the data used or alter the
use, behavior, or performance of an AI application.
Harmonization. Federal agencies such as the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the U.S. Equal
Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) should
collaborate to align the federal government’s approach to the regulation of AI. This will allow
organizations subject to the authority of different federal agencies to harmonize their AI
implementation approach, to avoid confusion, support innovation, and achieve greater compliance.
While agencies may approach this technology from varying regulatory angles, whether in safety,
privacy, consumer protection or otherwise, they should take a patient-centered approach and reach
sufficient alignment so that compliance with one framework will not result in violation of, or inability to
comply with, another. Failure to coordinate regulatory frameworks will not only create interpretation
and compliance burdens but will slow AI development and deployment, and stifle innovation by
creating a regulatory patchwork incompatible with the way health is delivered.
Any additional requirements on HIPAA-covered entities must balance the need to protect patient
privacy while not adding unnecessary burden to the regulated entities. Alignment enables
consistency to ensure requirements are complimentary. Additionally, conflicts between federal
agencies’ regulation of AI will hamper U.S efforts to lead globally in the regulation of AI. Other
countries want to adopt a framework for the regulation of AI that harmonizes across business
sectors and regulatory areas, rather than having to deal with discordant or conflicting requirements.
Accountability. Health organizations that use AI should determine and establish a risk-based
structure of accountability that extends across its partnerships to ensure that their AI use cases are
deployed in a responsible, fair, and consistent manner. This includes developing, implementing, and
documenting principles, policies, procedures, and an internal collaborative governance structure and
controls to oversee the development and use of AI applications. These controls should include
quality control parameters for the data used as well as criteria against which the performance of the
AI applications is monitored, evaluated, and re-evaluated, as needed, at regular intervals throughout
the lifecycle to ensure that AI can be iteratively enhanced and adapt within the risk framework.
Accountability should extend to the highest levels of management and should include key elements
such as risk assessment, training, monitoring, and internal sanctions.
Transparency. Transparency is essential to build trust in AI technology. While organizations should
protect their intellectual property or proprietary technology, they should also disclose when they are
using AI tools in high-risk applications rather than instances where it is used for administrative or
clerical purposes. Organizations should not be required to reveal the inner workings of their AI
systems to the public or regulatory agencies, nor is there any benefit in doing so. The detailed
disclosure of either data inputs or algorithmic processes would not be meaningful to patients,
providers, or payers, would force AI developers to disclose their intellectual property or proprietary

technology, could create AI vulnerability risks, and may limit innovators’ willingness to work with the
already highly regulated healthcare industry on meaningful AI applications.
Explainability. Developers of AI applications for use in healthcare must be able to explain to users
how a decision is made by a high-risk AI application in a way that is sufficiently understandable to
those users. Users should be able to gauge the context in which an algorithm operates and
understand the implications of the outcomes. Users should in turn be able to explain the role of
algorithms to individuals affected by AI-assisted decisions. Explanations should be meaningful and
useful, tailored to the audience, and calibrated to the level of risk.
Addressing Adverse Bias and Discrimination. There are instances where AI applications have
been found to be biased, as the underlying, especially historical, data sets may lack representative
or accurate data. Organizations should take comprehensive steps to identify and mitigate potential
sources of harmful bias across the lifecycle of their model development, and where reasonable and
appropriate for specific models, align with industry-developed standards. Ongoing inclusion of
sensitive personal data or data of vulnerable groups in AI training data is critical where such data is
relevant for the intended use and/or population can help alleviate and remedy biases, so all patients
may benefit from the advances from AI in healthcare.

Executive Summary
Artificial intelligence (AI) has been revolutionizing the U.S. healthcare delivery system for decades.
As the technology has evolved and become more attainable, interest in its use to advance patient
care and relieve provider burdens has only increased. AI is already enhancing diagnostics and
clinical decision-making, streamlining operations, expanding access to care, and accelerating drug
discovery - and innovation continues at a rapid pace.
However, despite growing momentum and interest in its potential, adoption of AI across the
healthcare ecosystem is inconsistent and inhibited. The current landscape is marked by evolving,
disparate federal and state policy frameworks, regulatory uncertainty, infrastructure development
needs, challenges in data interoperability, and growing concerns around fairness and trust. These
barriers continue to stunt progress and impede the sector from achieving the full benefits of further
implementation.
To harness the full potential of AI, the U.S. healthcare system—public and private sectors, and
regulators—must act together to enable progress. A unified federal approach to regulation is needed
to harmonize expectations, reduce ambiguity, and give organizations the predictability to adopt AI
responsibly.

healthcare ecosystem, this report outlines barriers to uniform AI adoption in the U.S. and delivers
targeted policy recommendations to address them. It urgently calls for a clear, forward-looking
national standard that defines AI and establishes what responsible governance must entail.

pathways intended to demonstrate how the recommendations could be advanced. Examples are
included to illustrate approaches and initiatives currently observed across the healthcare landscape.
Their inclusion does not imply endorsement, opposition, or a unified position by HLC, its members,
or the authors of this report.
Purpose, Call to Action, and
Guiding Principles
This report offers recommendations to federal legislative and regulatory efforts focused on fostering
responsible, unbiased, and scalable AI adoption in healthcare. Drawing from nearly 30 members of
HLC, existing standards, and past precedence, our goal is to equip lawmakers and agency leaders
with actionable strategies to reduce barriers and accelerate AI integration.
By embracing AI technologies and unlocking the full potential of available data, the U.S. has the
opportunity to:
• Improve patient experiences and expand access through faster, scaled, more accurate,
and personalized care that improves health outcomes, well-being, and access to care.
• Reduce the cost burden on U.S. taxpayers through greater efficiency and smarter
resource allocation.
• Alleviate healthcare workforce shortages and burnout by augmenting—not replacing—
human expertise.
• Decrease healthcare-related fraud, waste, and abuse with advanced detection and
prevention tools.
• Reduce the frequency of mistakes and errors to enhance safety and quality of care.
The sector is already proving the power of this technology, as evidenced in many use cases, such
as:
• In 2025, a multicenter, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled Phase 2a trial of a first-
in-class, generative AI-discovered TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis was
completed. Beyond meeting its primary safety endpoint, the study reported improvement in
lung function at the highest dose, highlighting that an AI-generated compound can produce
patient-relevant signals in humans and merits larger trials.
• A prospective, multi-site study (over 590k encounters across five hospitals) of Johns
Hopkins’ TREWS AI-enabled early-warning system for sepsis found that when clinicians
confirmed an alert within 3 hours, patients had an 18.7% relative reduction in in-hospital
mortality, along with less organ failure and shorter stays.

• Ambient tools directly address one of clinicians’ biggest barriers to AI adoption,
administrative overload, and free up time for patient care while maintaining clinician oversight
of notes. A 2025 JAMA Network Open pre-post study across 17 specialties using Nuance

Barriers to AI Adoption at Scale
While AI holds immense promise to transform healthcare, real-world adoption faces significant,
industry-specific hurdles. In-depth interviews with experts across every sector of the healthcare
ecosystem point to three interdependent areas where progress can benefit from further clarification
and/or support:
Governance and Data and Capabilities and End
Regulatory Complexity Infrastructure User Trust
Governance and Regulation Complexity
Inconsistent and intricate regulations impede AI integration at scale.
• Regulatory Fragmentation:
Healthcare organizations must navigate inconsistent, and often conflicting, state, federal, and
international AI regulations. Without a unified federal framework, compliance becomes
costly, burdensome, and uncertain—especially for entities operating across multiple
jurisdictions.
• Pace of Innovation vs. Regulatory Lag:
AI advances faster than regulatory policy can adapt. Slow and complex federal policymaking
leaves innovators unsure whether emerging technologies and techniques will comply with
outdated or unclear rules, discouraging investment.
• Bias and Ethical Risks:
AI models trained on incomplete or non-representative datasets risk perpetuating bias and
discrimination. Standardized federal requirements for demonstrating fairness,
representativeness, and accuracy are needed to maintain patient wellbeing.
• Accountability and Legal Ambiguity:
Uncertainty regarding liability and legal precedent complicates the assignment of fault when
AI systems fail or cause harm. The lack of clarity—across developers, data providers,
clinicians, manufacturers, and users—complicates business agreements, malpractice
coverage, and legal risk management.
8

Data and Infrastructure
Issues surrounding data access and privacy dissuade widespread adoption.
• Data Privacy and Security:
The integration of AI in healthcare, particularly concerning patient outcomes, faces significant
challenges related to data privacy, patient consent, and the complex global regulatory
landscape. While HIPAA provides essential protections for certain information when used by
covered entities, the inconsistencies in state laws, the varied nature and volume of data
collected, and sometimes, international regulations like the EU’s General Data Protection
Regulation create hurdles for data access and usability. Additionally, threats to data security,
particularly in today’s digital landscape, heighten risks as data protected within healthcare
institutions can be compromised when accessed through less secure third-party applications.
Without national standards for consent and privacy, innovation in healthcare may stagnate.
• Data Access:
There are many factors that contribute data extraction for AI applications including but not
limited to: differences in technological maturity, inconsistent data codification, and purpose-
based standards. Much of this data remains siloed, locked behind paywalls, or technically
incompatible, often increasing the cost of extracting and cleaning data. For AI to be
developed for purposes such as diagnosing, surfacing incidental radiology findings, or
recommending treatment protocols, training sets must be diverse, representative, and
comprehensive. Enforceable data standards and accessible infrastructure are essential to
realizing AI’s full potential.
• Interoperability and Integration:
Despite progress, current efforts to support AI tools within healthcare continue to face
challenges with interoperability. The incomplete adoption of consistent standards for data
extraction and use are limiting the ability of AI applications to meaningfully interoperate with
electronic health records, imaging platforms, and other clinical systems. This results in
fragmented data exchange and inconsistent performance across and within health systems.
• Cost, Resource, and Infrastructure Limitations:
The high costs of implementing, safeguarding, and maintaining AI solutions create barriers,
especially for underserved organizations. Significant investments in technology
infrastructure, data procurement, and skilled personnel are often required, hindering broader
adoption. Additionally, many providers face challenges integrating AI due to outdated
systems and limited resource capacity.
Capabilities and End User Trust
Skill gaps and trust among users inhibit effective AI implementation.
• Domain Expertise Deficit:
A critical barrier to effective AI adoption in healthcare is the lack of medical expertise among
most AI developers. Without a strong understanding of clinical practice, developers are not
well equipped to validate or train AI systems for real-world medical use. This gap often forces
9

healthcare organizations to divert clinical staff, such as nurses, from direct patient care to
assist in AI training and implementation.
This not only strains resources amid nationwide workforce shortages but also increases the
risk that AI tools fail to meet clinical standards. Where none already exist, targeted initiatives
to embed medical expertise into AI development, coupled with structured training for
implementation teams, are essential to ensure AI solutions are safe, effective, and aligned
with patient care priorities.
• Workforce Resistance and Patient Trust:
There is some lack of trust and comfort with AI among healthcare workers and patients.
Many practitioners remain skeptical due to fears of job displacement and potential legal
liability in case of AI-errors. These concerns are compounded by the “black box” nature of
many AI systems, including concerns around the completeness of the training data and
limited visibility into how models are built and how outputs are generated.
Patients also share concerns, particularly around the accuracy of AI outputs and the
protection of their personal health data but tend to be less skeptical than providers. Past data
breaches, the commercialization of health apps, and uncertainty about how AI informs
clinical decisions reinforce the demand for greater transparency, accountability, and
oversight. An additional significant risk is the tendency for end users to over trust AI outputs,
assuming they are always correct. This overreliance can lead to serious errors when
decisions are made without human judgment or critical evaluation.

Policy Recommendations to Break
Down Barriers
By embracing AI technologies and unlocking the full potential of available data, the U.S. can improve
overall patient outcomes across communities, alleviate workforce shortages, reduce costs for
taxpayers, detect and curb healthcare-related fraud, and reduce the burden of mistakes and errors.
This section presents a series of nine policy recommendations designed to address key barriers to
AI adoption in healthcare. Each recommendation is grounded in the guiding principles outlined
above and mapped out to one of three previously articulated categories of barriers (i.e. governance
and regulatory complexity, data and infrastructure, and capabilities and end user trust).
Each recommendation includes actionable tactics (25 total) detailing practical potential next steps for
addressing these opportunities. These recommendations are supported by examples that
demonstrate precedent and are intended to be used as general learnings that regulators can take or
adopt relevant attributes where applicable. These examples are neither endorsed nor opposed by
HLC, its members, or the authors of this report.

relative level of effort and feasibility to implement the particular recommendation. The left, white

Recommendation: Centralize Legislation
Centralize Legislation
Establish a comprehensive federal policy that preempts conflicting state laws to ensure a consistent,
nationwide approach to mitigating AI-related risks in healthcare. This framework should define
currently unaddressed areas—such as permissible levels of data de-identification and required
safeguards for compliance—while remaining flexible enough to adapt to rapid AI innovation. A single
federal standard will reduce regulatory fragmentation and foster responsible adoption.
TACTICS
Review Current State Legislative Landscape:
Review existing state AI-related laws to identify effective practices, inconsistencies, and gaps, and
assess their impact on healthcare. The U.S. has several instances that can be leveraged as a
starting point, but we can also learn (wins and potential pitfalls) from international regulations that
are well ahead on this path to standardization.
• U.S. Example: California’s AI Risk Management Framework, linked to the California
Consumer Privacy Act and California Privacy Rights Act introduces transparency
requirements for automated decision-making. Other states, such as Colorado (SB21-169),
address algorithmic bias in specific sectors (e.g., insurance) but lack comprehensive
healthcare AI provisions—highlighting the need for federal alignment.
• International Example: The EU AI Act uses a risk-tiered model, classifying certain health-
related AI as “high-risk” with strict oversight and documentation requirements. While this
framework demonstrates the commitment to protections, its complexity and potential
overreach offer learnings for US policy makers to be aware of. In contrast, the UK’s MHRA
(Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency) “regulatory sandbox” allows
controlled real-world testing—offering a potential model for U.S. pilot programs.
Establish a State Law Moratorium:
Rather than a sweeping ban, a federally guided, narrowly scoped pause on state laws related to use
of AI in healthcare should be considered.
• Example: Although the proposed five-year moratorium on state-level AI enforcement in the
One Big Beautiful Bill Act was removed in a nearly unanimous 99‑1 Senate vote in early
July 2025 after clearing the House, its consideration highlights bipartisan concerns about an
inconsistent state patchwork as well as a broad and lengthy preemption of state law.
• Example: In December 2025, President Donald J. Trump signed an executive order to block
states from implementing excessive regulations and create a patchwork of varying
12

"regulatory regimes." Through the development of a framework serving as a "minimally
burdensome national standard," the executive order is intended to cultivate an environment
for U.S. AI companies that enables freedom in innovation.
Develop a Comprehensive Federal Policy:
Collaborate with private sector entities (across healthcare and technology) to shape a
comprehensive federal policy framework that incorporates all the aspects of successful AI
development, deployment, adoption, and scaling. This framework should include data privacy
standards, evaluation and safety standards, post-market monitoring protocols, and transparency
guidelines. HLC members widely agree that a unified set of regulations would simplify compliance
and ensure consistency across states.
• Example: While not enforceable regulation, nor tailored for healthcare, the National Institute
of Standards and Technology’s (NIST’s) AI Risk Management Framework (RMF 2.0)
outlines voluntary standards for AI governance, addressing key areas such as data integrity,
system safety, transparency, and accountability. It exemplifies how public and private sector
collaboration can produce practical, consensus-driven guidance that balances innovation
with risk management.

Recommendation: Modernize Regulations
Centralize Legislation
To keep pace with the rapidly evolving digital and AI landscape, federal regulations must be
modernized to integrate within outdated frameworks that limit responsible scaling and widespread
adoption. Updating core policies—such as HIPAA and others—will foster accountability, consistency,
and agility, enabling healthcare organizations to innovate without being constrained by legacy
policies and standards. Modernized regulations should provide clear, forward-looking guidance on
critical data privacy and security challenges, including consent management, uniform security
requirements, and protections aligned to today’s digital reality.
13

TACTICS
Evaluate Existing Federal Regulations:
Assess current regulatory standards to identify gaps in applicability, coverage, and enforcement—
particularly where definitions (e.g., protected health information, de-identification) no longer reflect
today’s technology and data practices.
Revise and Modernize Frameworks:
Update existing regulations, including HIPAA, to address accountability, data governance, security
and provenance, while retaining the flexibility provided by HIPAA’s permitted uses of data for
treatment, payment, and health care operations. While existing ONC HTI-1 standards promote
interoperability and foundational data transparency, the Health IT framework must go further to
address fairness, accountability, and real-world safety for widespread adoption. This framework
should clarify display requirements and coverage expectations to ensure all providers delivering
health AI solutions are accounted for—not just a subset. We also support utilization of regulatory
sandboxes to test approaches and adjust to enable the unified federal approach to regulation and
requirements.
• Example: While HIPAA provides strong federal standards for protected health information
(PHI), it has not received a major update since 2013. Its current security standards do not
fully address the realities of AI-driven healthcare, leaving gaps in areas such as consent, risk
classification, and accountability. Updating HIPAA to reflect evolving technologies would
strengthen privacy, security, and trust in an AI-rich environment.
• Example: FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) was originally designed to
provide a set of rules and specifications for the secure exchange of electronic healthcare
data, but initiatives like SMART (Substitutable Medical Applications, Reusable Technologies)
on FHIR, AI Metadata Extensions, and Clinical Decision Support (CDS) hooks are being
introduced and piloted across use cases to expand coverage to AI workflows. The pace of
establishment, adoption, and enforcement of these standards will support keeping up with
the rapid pace of demand, innovation, and utility of AI within the healthcare ecosystem to
formalize the process.

Recommendation: Clarify Accountability
Centralize Legislation
Clearly define and assign accountability for AI outputs across all parties, including solution
developers, data and technology providers, and healthcare providers, to eliminate ambiguity in
liability and build trust in AI-enabled care. Current healthcare regulations and standards do not
extend to most healthcare value chain partners or developers, as well as healthcare service
providers, leaving significant accountability gaps.
TACTICS
Documentation of Accountability:
Establish federal requirements that clearly define roles, responsibilities, and oversight for all parties
involved in developing, deploying, and monitoring AI systems, ensuring patients and caregivers
understand who is responsible at each stage.
• Example: The EU AI Act requires clear documentation of roles and responsibilities for “high-
risk” healthcare AI, with providers accountable for design and validation and deployers
responsible for data quality, oversight, and communication of system limitations. As U.S.
policymakers consider similar approaches, it will be important to evaluate how the EU AI Act
has impacted AI innovation in the EU, and whether this model, or an alternative one, would
best align with the U.S. healthcare landscape. We note that HIPAA applies only to covered
entities, leaving many AI developers, digital health platforms, and third-party aggregators
outside federal privacy and security obligations despite handling sensitive health data.
• Example: In early 2025, the FDA issued a Draft Guidance on “Considerations for the Use of
Artificial Intelligence to Support Regulatory Decision-Making for Drug and Biological
Products.” This guidance incorporates risk-based considerations and may serve as a starting
point for developing future regulatory frameworks and best practices for AI in healthcare.
Establish Standards for Non-covered Entities:
Develop comparable HIPAA-aligned standards to ensure the privacy and security of personal health
information and health-related data are protected when used by non-covered entities. Today, many
AI developers, digital health platforms, and third-party aggregators may be subject to different
federal or state privacy and security obligations despite their significant role in the handling of
sensitive health data.
Expand Malpractice Insurance Coverage:
Revise existing malpractice insurance statutes to account for AI-enabled clinical decision-making
and clearly distinguish when liability lies with clinicians (e.g. negligent use), healthcare institutions
(e.g. insufficient controls), or technology partners (e.g. poor modeling), to protect responsible users
while reinforcing oversight and safety.

Recommendation: Ensure Liability Protection
Centralize Legislation
To foster responsible AI innovation, federal policy must clearly address liability in the use of AI for
patient care. This includes defining permitted uses of AI in patient care, setting expectations for
controls, and outlining protections for organizations that comply with established standards and best
practices. Reducing liability uncertainty will encourage more developers and healthcare
organizations to design, deploy, and scale AI solutions safely.
TACTICS
Establish and Incentivize Industry Certifications and Audits:
Create auditable standards, certified by recognized industry bodies to promote adoption of safe
development practices.
• Example: The HITRUST AI Assurance Program offers AI Security and Risk Management
Assessments to help organizations secure AI-powered applications and demonstrate
trustworthiness. Establish mechanisms that promote universal adoption of this or an
equivalent assessment, making healthcare organizations responsible for ongoing certification
and monitoring.
Develop a Federal Legal Framework:
Provide clear guidance on interpretation, culpability, burden of liability, and permitted use across the
spectrum of healthcare AI applications. Several interviewees, especially data partners, suggest that
federal leadership should set national standards, convene industry partners, and address liability to
enable safe and effective AI use.

Establish a Regulatory Safe Harbor Provision:
Offer targeted liability protection for organizations that meet defined guardrails, transparency, and
monitoring requirements.
• Example: A modernized “Patient Engagement and Support Safe Harbor” could expand
protections to additional entities and permitted support areas, enabling broader care
coordination while maintaining safeguards.

Issues surrounding data access and privacy dissuade widespread adoption.
Recommendation:
Fortify Data Readiness, Accessibility & Sharing
Strengthen data quality, security, and interoperability standards to ensure healthcare information is
secure, accessible, portable, and ready for AI integration throughout its lifecycle. This includes
providing clear federal guidance on data access, sharing, usage, and security tailored to AI
applications across healthcare. Mandating standardized digital information sharing for payment and
operations use cases will help reduce delays, denials, and invalid claim edits that negatively impact
both patients and providers.
Anchored in FHIR and the U.S. Core Data for Interoperability (USCDI) standards, the National
Coordinator for Health Information Technology’s (ONC’s) Cures Act Final Rule establishes
nationwide requirements for the standardized exchange and accessible sharing of electronic health
record (EHR) data. These rules enable secure connections between third-party AI tools and patient
information while emphasizing the need for structured, high-quality data to ensure both regulatory
compliance and trust across the healthcare system.
17

TACTICS
Establish Guidelines for Data Cleansing and Standardize Intake:
Develop comprehensive protocols that outline the process for identifying, correcting, and removing
inaccurate or irrelevant data. Also establish standards for how data should be collected, formatted,
and input to ensure consistency and accuracy across all datasets to improve future use.
Promote Integration, Interoperability, and Standards-based Exchange:
Broadly incorporated by major EHR vendors and providers, ONC’s FHIR and USCDI standards
represent meaningful progress toward nationwide interoperability. Despite this progress, true data
portability and seamless exchange remain challenging and costly, particularly for small providers
and practices with limited resources. FHIR implementations vary as vendors often support multiple
FHIR versions in parallel, and data beyond the standard US Core profiles can include proprietary
extensions, which can lead to 'FHIR profiles' that complicate uniform integration. As the list of
clinical information required by USCDI continues to expand, these certification-required capabilities
should take into account what data elements are most important for AI. Beyond these technical
barriers, the cost and resources (i.e. people and expertise) required for implementation and ongoing
updates is high; many smaller providers and practices struggle to comply in a timely manner. As a
result, while EHR developers have advanced significantly within their domain, factors beyond their
direct control continue to limit widespread and uniform adoption. Federal leadership and targeted
incentives will be essential to close these gaps, reduce burdens on smaller organizations, and
deliver on the promise of true interoperability across the healthcare system.
Establish a Patient Data Registry:
Develop a national patient registry with a digital consent ledger, inclusive of an opt-out control, that
enables patients to track where their data resides, control who can access it, and manage
permissions to determine their data sharing preferences. As suggested in the interviews, by many
data brokers, manufacturers, and providers, a patient ledger puts patients at the center,
safeguarding privacy while enabling responsible data sharing to support innovation, including AI.
While precedents exist such as the oncology registries, the recently mandated Alzheimer’s therapy
registry, and the Health Information Exchanges from the Definitive Healthcare, these efforts are
fragmented and narrow in scope. They stop short of offering a comprehensive, patient-controlled
system that spans the full healthcare ecosystem.
The July 2025 announcement from the current Administration to develop a patient centric healthcare
ecosystem (in partnership with the private sector) marks progress in advancing aligned networks,
securing digital identity, and consistent data exchange standards. This initiative lays the groundwork,
but realizing true patient controlled centralized data repository will require significant funding, strong
regulatory governance, as well as strong commitment from public and private sectors.

Recommendation: Establish Transparency Standards
Establish clear federal standards for transparency in the design, deployment, and monitoring of AI
solutions in healthcare. These standards should define what information organizations must
disclose—such as AI capabilities, limitations, training data sources, performance metrics, and error
rates—how the disclosed information must be communicated to both clinicians and patients, and
requirements for ongoing monitoring. AI-specific laws, such as those that relate to transparency and
ethical use, should not conflict with existing privacy laws and should take them into account.
Regulatory frameworks should strike a balance—ensuring accountability and improving adoptions
without stifling innovation and limiting the potential benefits of improving patient outcomes. Greater
transparency will strengthen trust among providers, patients, and regulators, while promoting
responsible AI use.
TACTICS
Develop a Configurable AI “Nutrition Label”:
Create a nationally recognized disclosure framework to communicate bias safeguards, privacy
protections, and algorithm performance in a clear, consistent format. The label should be
configurable to allow comprehensive detail for high-risk applications and lighter specificity for lower
risk use cases, as well as adaptable to the specific parameters available for each AI tool.
• Example: NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) aims to foster the development
of innovative approaches to address characteristics of trustworthiness, including valid and
reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and
interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair, with harmful bias managed.
19

Define Transparency Policies:
Mandate disclosure of AI system performance, limitations, and risk mitigation measures in a
standardized format, coupled with appropriate monitoring of outputs.
• Example: FDA Guidance on AI-Enabled Medical Devices (in draft) recommends
documenting validation data, bias mitigation strategies, and limitations, and encourages the
use of model cards to communicate risks and capabilities.
• Example: ONC’s HTI-1 Final Rule (effective February 2024) requires algorithm transparency
and risk documentation for certified AI decision-support tools, including standardized
disclosure of development methods, validation results, and performance metrics.
• Example: The U.S. can learn from the EU AI Act, which sets risk-tiered transparency
obligations for AI applications, requiring clear instructions for use and disclosure of system
capabilities, limitations, and accuracy metrics.

Recommendation: Mitigate Bias
Establish comprehensive federal guidelines to reduce bias in healthcare AI applications. This
includes leveraging representative datasets for training, requiring appropriate monitoring of AI
outputs, and mandating corrective action when biases are detected. A consistent, nationwide
approach will reduce discrimination, promote fairness, and build trust in AI tools.
TACTICS
Establish Continuous Monitoring Guidelines:
Require organizations to implement periodic bias detection and transparency practices during model
development and post-deployment, with mandated corrective actions to maintain fairness and
accuracy.
20

Define AI Development Guidelines:
Define best practices for AI design, testing, and retraining that prioritize representative datasets and
multidisciplinary development teams to more comprehensively represent multi-faceted patient
populations.
Develop AI Validation Pathways:
Ensure clear validation pathways for accuracy testing, usability assessments, and simulated
deployments. In line with a risk-based approach, higher risk use cases could be validated and
certified by local evaluators or external experts.
• Example: A 2023 JAMA proposal recommends a nationwide network of Health AI Assurance
Laboratories as shared public–private resources to test and validate models against agreed
best practices established by Nation Academy of Medicine.
• Example: Several health systems have developed local validation and monitoring
platforms—emphasizing multidisciplinary, cross‑department collaboration inside the health
system—to ensure AI tools perform safely within their own clinical environments. For
instance, Vanderbilt University Medical Center’s Vanderbilt VAMOS (Algorithmovigilance
Monitoring and Operations System) system supports ongoing, system‑wide monitoring of AI
models throughout their lifecycle to promote safe and effective use. Additionally, University of
California, San Francisco Health’s IMPACC (Impact Monitoring Platform for AI in Clinical
Care) platform evaluates AI tools in real-world workflows and monitors performance and
bias.

Recommendation: Standardize Terminology
The definition of what constitutes ‘AI use’ in research and development remains fluid and
inconsistent across the industry. As more advanced forms of AI begin to demonstrate tremendous
potential for drug discovery tools, clinical decision support, care coordination, and patient
engagement, the need for consistent, universal terminology becomes even more urgent.
Establishing a standardized vocabulary that bridges both technical and healthcare-specific contexts
will promote clarity, improve communication across stakeholders, and ensure regulations are applied
consistently.
TACTICS
Define Healthcare-Specific AI Terminology:
Develop a federal glossary of AI terms tailored to the healthcare sector, like HIPAA’s definitions for
health data. This glossary should include concrete and relevant examples for each of the glossary AI
terms. For example, diagnostics tools in the context of medical imaging refer to algorithms that
interpret medical images.
Integrate Definitions into Federal Regulatory Frameworks:
Embed the standardized glossary into federal AI regulations and related guidance to ensure uniform
application across all policies and programs.
• Example: NIST Glossary for Trustworthy AI – A non-sector-specific resource for common AI
and machine learning (ML) terminology.
• Example: International Organization for Standardization/International Electrotechnical
Commission (ISO/IEC 22989:2022) – International consensus-based AI vocabulary widely
used in governance frameworks can serve as a seed to developing a US Healthcare centric
glossary.
• Example: FDA Digital Health and AI Glossary – Aligns with ISO standards and provides
practical definitions for AI systems and components.

Recommendation:
Enhance Workforce Training & Education
Develop comprehensive education and training programs to upskill healthcare professionals in both
foundational AI concepts and healthcare-specific applications. By embedding AI literacy into
professional development, the healthcare workforce can better understand, evaluate, and
responsibly adopt AI to mitigate job-loss fears among clinicians and strengthen patient trust.
TACTICS
Incentivize Employee Training:
Encourage healthcare organizations to provide workforce-wide AI training through federal financial
incentives, such as tax credits or subsidies. Training should cover AI terminology, data privacy and
security, transparency, bias mitigation, and healthcare use cases. This is supported by a 2022
Congressional Research Service report recommendation for targeted tax credits for employer
training, with an incremental model that rewards investment above a baseline and prioritizes low-
and middle-wage employees to promote fair and inclusive skill-building.
• Example: Potentially seek to leverage the One Big Beautiful Bill’s expansion of Pell Grant
eligibility for short-term training programs to include AI-focused healthcare courses, enabling
nurses and other mid-carrier providers to access affordable upskilling opportunities.
Revamp Curriculum to Drive Inherent AI-Readiness:
Adapt curricula from K–12 through postgraduate levels to include foundational AI concepts, ethical
considerations, and real-world healthcare applications. AI readiness amongst the incoming
workforce will ensure faster adoption and scaling across the industry.
• Example: The White House Executive Order on Artificial Intelligence Education published in
April 2025 directs federal agencies to integrate AI literacy into K–12 curricula,
train educators, and prioritize AI in discretionary grants and workforce
development programs.

Create a Secure Sandbox:
Establish controlled environments where healthcare professionals can safely test AI tools on de-
identified data to further build confidence, competence, and capabilities.
• Example: The UK’s NHS “AI Airlock” allows innovators to pilot AI tools in live settings under
strict governance, ethical review, and patient safety oversight.

Potential Benefits:
By addressing barriers, clarifying accountabilities, and investing in education and infrastructure,

Conclusion
While the barriers described within this report have inhibited progress thus far, removing them is
achievable through further public and private sector collaboration. HLC and its members are
committed to continue serving as a resource to inform needed AI regulatory and legislative actions.
The risk of inaction is compounding. A patchwork of rules will harden into incompatible and
competing systems. Incomplete or biased data will deepen access gaps and degrade model
performance. Public trust will erode further adoption. Waiting turns today’s friction into tomorrow’s
failure to scale when safely and effectively scaling AI can better support providers and serve
patients.
What is needed now is federal action with urgency and clarity. This report outlines practical, high-
impact actions that can be implemented immediately, alongside longer-term objectives that require
sustained effort and coordination. If the U.S. can align policy with technology and clinical realities, AI
will not only enhance human intelligence—it will extend the reach of better and safer care to all
communities.

Appendix
1. Summary of Interview Methodology
This report is grounded in a series of structured, 30-minute interviews with subject matter experts
from HLC member companies that span the healthcare ecosystem. Interviewees were nominated by
their organizations to ensure a diversity of perspectives, with representation from regulatory,
compliance, data, technology, clinical operations, and digital health innovation functions.
Each interview followed a consistent process focused on exploring barriers and accelerators to AI
adoption in healthcare, anchored around three core themes:
• People: Trust and adoption of AI, stakeholder awareness and education, and the incentives
or disincentives influencing AI uptake.
• Process & Policy: Existing standards and regulations, integration challenges, and the
operational burden of enabling AI solutions.
• Data & Technology: Data interoperability and privacy, data quality and completeness, and
the infrastructure required to support AI initiatives.
This approach ensured that the insights were grounded in real-world experience and reflect the
collective insights of leading organizations across the healthcare landscape.
