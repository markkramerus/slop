Dear Secretary Kennedy:
It is with great pleasure that Sanford Health submits our response to your Request For
Information “Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as part of Clinical
Care.” Sanford Health believes that the effective and responsible development,
implementation and utilization of artificial intelligence technologies will have seismic
impacts on the way healthcare is delivered and how patients live their best and fullest
lives. By taking a thoughtful and strategic approach to policy development, especially
around oversight and approval, reimbursement and privacy, we have the chance to chart a
course that will change the health not of just individual patients, but of communities and
countries. This is an exciting chapter filled with potential, but fraught with peril if done
incorrectly.
Introduction
Sanford Health is the largest rural health system in the United States, serving communities
across some of the most geographically expansive and sparsely populated regions in the
country. Headquartered in Sioux Falls, South Dakota, Sanford Health is a nonprofit,
integrated health system with 55,000 employees caring for more than 2 million patients
and nearly 415,000 health plan members across South Dakota, North Dakota, Minnesota,
Wyoming, Iowa, Wisconsin, and Michigan’s Upper Peninsula. Our care delivery footprint
includes 58 hospitals, 289 clinic locations, 145 senior care communities, and a physician
and advanced practice workforce of more than 4,500 clinicians.
Providing high-quality, timely care in rural America presents persistent and well-
documented challenges, including long travel distances, workforce shortages, limited
specialty access, and chronic underpayment by public payers. For more than a century,
Sanford Health has adapted its care models to meet these realities, prioritizing solutions

that preserve local access while connecting patients to the expertise and services they
need. Virtual care and digital health innovation have become essential clinical
infrastructure for sustaining access across our rural footprint.
Within this context, Sanford Health is thoughtfully integrating artificial intelligence and
digital tools to advance three core priorities: access, quality, and sustainability. AI is being
applied to address operational and clinical challenges inherent in rural care delivery,
including workforce efficiency, care coordination, and timely clinical decision support. Our
responses to the questions contained in the initial RFI are below, and are based on our
direct experience, reflecting the importance of aligning innovation with real-world care
environments, ensuring that emerging technologies are deployed safely, transparently, and
in ways that strengthen trust among patients and providers.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care
and its adoption and use in clinical care?
Regulatory Uncertainty
The rapid evolution of AI technology and capability make creating a comprehensive
regulatory framework difficult, if not impossible. When it comes to regulation, we need a
balanced approach that supports innovation, without stifling it, while also protecting
patient safety. Clarifying regulations around AI tools and FDA approval requirements on
medical devices will alleviate liability concerns for health systems. Also, creating a special
reimbursement category for AI-enabled programs is imperative to incentivize provider
participation. At Sanford, we have found the implementation of a strong, enterprise-wide AI
Governance structure helps to ensure that AI tools are built to internal and external
standards and requirements. HHS should prioritize identifying and publicizing best
practices in this area so that effective models can be replicated at health organizations of
all sizes.
Data Access
If done correctly, AI can turn fragmented data into a connected, community-wide view of
health. This would support everything from public health surveillance to personalized care
plans. However, data quality and completeness prove to be a barrier. The source and
curation of the data affect its completeness, structure, and consistency with coding,
leading to a ‘gold mine’ or a ‘dump’ of information. There can be multiple EHRs between
health systems (sometimes even within health systems) causing fragmented ecosystems.
Limited access to data, especially within standardized methodology, is also a barrier. In
rural health care systems, there may be limited resources, causing all of the above to be
compounded. HHS has a crucial role to play in identifying baseline standards of what type

of data sources are used to train models, how models are updated and refined, and how
data can be transmitted across and between systems in an efficient and safe way.
Workflow Integration and Change Management
With technology changing at such a fast pace, there are eager clinicians who are
continuous learners and are thriving in this new AI landscape, but for others, the pace of
change is challenging. A tremendous amount of change management for health care
teams is required not only in the use of AI tools, but also the acceptance of such tools. The
ever-changing landscape also requires more technology and IT roles to support our teams
and patients. This puts health systems in direct competition with tech-centered
companies. To keep up with pace of innovation, it is essential that health systems
continually invest in building trust, advancing new infrastructure, continued training and
certification and compete for talent in the technology space.
Cost
Creating and purchasing AI tools is very expensive for health systems, and many rural
locations simply do not have the funds to fully integrate AI programs and tools to the extent
that is needed, in addition to maintaining critical technological upgrades as the industry
rapidly changes. In many cases, AI tools are developed to predict chronic diseases. With
earlier diagnoses, it may prevent future illnesses and decrease costs long-term. Current
payment systems are simply not keeping up with these preventative interventions that best
benefit the patient. AI has the potential to fundamentally change how healthcare is
delivered. The entities that are able to access new tools and make them part of the normal
course of patient care will have an advantage. HHS must ensure that smaller entities that
may not have the upfront capital to invest in new systems are not permanently
disadvantaged or unable to leverage these tools.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What
HHS regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your
ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
At Sanford Health, we are focused on accelerating the pace of innovation and adoption of
new emerging health care technologies while establishing guardrails to mitigate any
potential risks and protect private health information. Any regulation should focus on
protecting patients and users of the technology, not becoming too prescriptive or hindering
innovation.

Reimbursement and Payment Models
The biggest barriers to the adoption and investment of AI in clinical care is the current
structure of reimbursement and payment models, or in reality, the lack of real and
meaningful reimbursement models. There is a crucial need to develop novel payment
programs to incentivize clinicians and patients to adopt AI tools. Current payment policies,
particularly fee-for-service programs, slow AI adoption and/or distort incentives. Creating a
special reimbursement category for AI-enabled programs may have large upfront costs but
could be very useful to give actors access to emerging technology.
Medicare reimbursement models need to support the use of AI models that not only
predict earlier needs for intervention of chronic diseases, but also to support remote
monitoring, assisted documentation, and ambient scribing. It is also a necessity that
payment models support the integration of AI into clinical workflows without increasing
administrative burden. The Medicare and Medicaid programs have the ability to be at the
forefront of this work, and providing the technical experience and examples that will also
induce greater adoption in the commercial reimbursement markets.
AI Medical Devices
There is much ambiguity around regulations when determining if an AI tool is a decision
support tool or may require FDA approval as a medical device. Providing clear guidance
that distinguishes FDA-regulated AI/ML medical devices from non-device, clinical-decision
support and operational tools will alleviate liability concerns for health systems. Further,
FDA should disseminate additional guidance on how AI products will be assessed and
regulated once approved, including post-approval surveillance and recertification, as well
as the degrees of human involvement are necessary for various technologies. We are not
at the place with the technology, from the perspective of both the patients and provider, to
fully automate care.
Data Security
It is a fundamental responsibility of health providers to use our utmost care to keep our
patients’ data safe and private. All providers must operate within secure, resilient, and well-
supported digital environments.
At Sanford Health, how we utilize data to improve overall care is something we take very
seriously. We have created a multidisciplinary AI/data governance/oversight committee
that provides us with a reliable and consistent process to evaluate and determine the
efficacy of AI tools (see question #4). The governance framework emphasizes when it is
important to keep a human in the loop and our commitment to responsibly develop and

deploy AI models safely and transparently. It is vital to continue to assess how any AI-
enabled technology we deploy will use our data and what protections are involved.
HHS already has a robust regulatory framework for data protection in HIPAA. Admittedly, it
could be updated to include the plethora of health data that is now sitting outside of
current HIPAA standards. For example, there continues to be ambiguity around the use of
PHI for developing AI models, requiring more clarification. There is an opportunity to
provide safe harbors for de-identification and limited datasets used for AI development.
Flexibility in Clinical Settings
By integrating AI tools across Sanford, we’re addressing challenges with targeted solutions,
especially in the rural communities we serve. These tools aren’t replacing clinicians –
they’re empowering our care teams to spend more time with patients and make more
informed decisions. They’re also expanding access, improving affordability, and driving
better outcomes for our patients.
By allowing more regulatory flexibility to deploy and evaluate AI tools, health systems
would see accelerated adoption and incentivized, effective use of AI tools in the clinical
setting. Health systems with the means to create clinical-based AI tools also need more
flexibility to expand use of these decision-support tools in order to connect with partners
as well as other non-integrated systems. This is especially the case for rural populations,
who usually face a more fragmented healthcare delivery experience, and higher rates of
chronic condition and acuity of their conditions. We must maintain regulatory flexibility
that allows us to build the AI ecosystem that best meets the needs of the patient and
communities we serve.
3. What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should
HHS play to help address them?
Data Ownership
There continues to be ambiguity around the ownership and use of health care data to train
AI models, creating an increased risk for health systems. Health systems need clarity and
flexibility on the rights of this data being used.
Limited Data Sets
Many models are trained in limited data sets which may lead to performance issues or bias
when applied to diverse populations. Maintaining HIPPA compliance may also limit model
effectiveness.

Cybersecurity Risks
Creating and implementing AI tools may increase cybersecurity risks. As stated previously,
it is a fundamental responsibility of health providers to use our utmost care to keep our
patients’ data safe and private. Reducing cybersecurity risks is extremely expensive and
cost prohibitive in many small rural facilities. HHS should create payment models to
support these increased costs.
4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods
(pre- and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow
and human-centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further
support these processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most impactful
(e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or prize competitions)?
Evaluation and Validation Methods
Evaluation and validation methods should include using diverse datasets, audits of AI tools
across demographic and clinical subgroups, prospective monitoring drift, bias, calibration,
and unintended consequences, and continued testing against EHR configuration changes
and/or missing data.
Governance Structure
At Sanford Health, we created a multidisciplinary AI/data governance/oversight committee
that provides us with a reliable and consistent process to evaluate and determine the
efficacy of AI tools. Their function is to evaluate requests from clinicians and other leaders
on our team who bring forward ideas as well as outside companies looking to partner with
us on new AI tools. We put the patient and the caregiver at the center of every single AI
decision.
This governance structure is most focused on ensuring these tools are deployed
responsibly, safely, and transparently. Our collaboration with industry partners on a
voluntary framework for health care AI commitments is critical to this work.
Creating an independent regulatory board comprised of non-governmental organizations
(such as health systems, payers, 3rd party developers, etc.) would provide frameworks and
a possible registry for development and use of AI tools. Further, as recommended
previously, HHS should prioritize identifying and publicizing best practices in this area so
that effective models can be replicated at health organizations of all sizes. The creation of
a third-party entity must not create an unnecessary or burdensome level of oversight but
instead be fashioned to serve as a catalyst for innovation, adoption and dissemination of
best practices in this rapidly expanding space.

Funding Opportunities
There is an opportunity for HHS to fund AI initiatives through grants or more robust AI-
supported payment models. Another funding opportunity could be through other
cooperative arrangements within HHS, such as supporting AI and innovation research labs
or supporting cooperation among health systems.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation,
certification, industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative
and effective AI use in clinical care?
Accreditation and Certification
Using the independent regulatory board as discussed in question #4 above, the function of
the board could expand to include accreditation and certification with active support from
HHS. In cooperation with other independent third-party organizations, accreditation
programs could be developed to include fairness, transparency, and monitoring standards.
Further success could be achieved if HHS encouraged these accreditation bodies to
incorporate AI governance into their quality standards. Offering additional support from
HHS such as technical guidance aligned with accreditation bodies would also assist with
the regulatory board’s success as well. It is important to keep in mind that external
validators or oversight boards should not create extraneous, redundant or superfluous
regulatory bodies that impede the creation and deployment of responsible and effective AI
technologies that will benefit patients, healthcare providers, and the well-being of the
nation.
Workforce Development
A different skill set is required in today’s clinical setting than just a few years ago. In fact, we
see that technology is different and/or new by the time someone graduates from a four-year
program. The rapid pace of evolving technology is an ongoing barrier within AI adoption.
Training an appropriate workforce will be crucial to maximize the impact and potential of
this technology.
Sanford Health uses advanced clinical training and simulation to strengthen the rural
health care workforce, improve readiness for high-risk clinical scenarios, and expand
access to specialized care in communities with limited local resources. By bringing
immersive, technology-enabled training directly to rural settings, Sanford equips clinicians
across disciplines with the skills, confidence, and support needed to deliver high-quality
care close to home. Sanford Health also addresses critical gaps in emergency care through
comprehensive EMS education and advanced practice provider (APP) training. Over the
past five years, Sanford has enrolled more than 1,500 students in EMT programs and

provides weekly live, interactive continuing education sessions for rural EMS providers.
Launched in 2026, the APP Emergency Department Foundational Knowledge and Skills
Program delivers more than 150 hours of structured training for nurse practitioners and
physician assistants staffing rural emergency departments, with plans to enroll more than
130 APP clinicians over three years. These efforts are further supported by the Virtual Care
Center Education Institute, which integrates telehealth and virtual reality into simulation-
based training.
Health systems are facing a global battle for talent, especially with roles that can be fully
remote. Health systems are competing for talent with every other company that has a
technology need across the country – not just Google and Amazon. This barrier can be
amplified in rural settings. We will need new roles like data scientists, predictive modelers,
AI developers to develop chatbots, product management and people that are focused on
the end-to-end patient journey and experience. However, health systems have a unique
differentiator in that many people are looking for a career that brings meaning and purpose,
and there’s no better place to make a difference every day than in health care.
HHS has an opportunity to support workforce development for the many areas and
expertise required in the AI setting while elevating the personal fulfillment of working in
health care. Overall, more training opportunities and funding for more formal education are
key for the future of workforce development.
Since the pipeline to develop talent can be long, HHS should focus on supporting this area
without delay. We also need to be cognizant that training should focus on what we need in
the future, not where we are at right now. Developing flexible training programs that can
adapt with the evolution of technology are imperative.
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and
cost expectations, and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI
tools would have the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give
new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
Sanford Health has made significant investments in advanced analytics and internally
developed AI tools that support both clinical decision-making and operational efficiency.
These tools are embedded within the electronic medical record and across care
management workflows to help identify patients at risk earlier, prioritize outreach, and
support timely intervention. Sanford Health has deployed AI models to identify patients at
risk for chronic kidney disease before symptoms appear, flag high-risk obstetric patients for
earlier monitoring, identify patients at elevated risk for colon and lung cancer, and predict

the likelihood of developing Type II diabetes prior to diagnosis. Additional AI-enabled tools
reduce missed appointments, identify drivers of avoidable emergency department use, and
support prevention of post-acute readmissions. Together, these capabilities allow care
teams to focus resources where they can have the greatest impact while reducing
administrative burden in workforce-constrained rural settings.
To be clear, these tools aren’t replacing clinicians – they’re empowering our care teams to
spend more time with patients and make more informed decisions. They’re also expanding
access, improving affordability, and driving better outcomes for our patients.
Although AI has not yet met many of the needs for health care, it does continue to advance
in many areas such as:
Predicting Chronic Disease States
AI has the potential to reduce gaps in care and identify early warning signs of chronic
conditions like diabetes, hypertension, or mental health risks, especially in patients who
might not visit frequently. For rural populations, this proactive approach can reduce costly
hospitalizations and improve outcomes.
Sanford Health’s enterprise data analytics team developed an AI-driven risk score that’s
now embedded in the electronic medical record. It alerts primary care providers to patients
who are at an elevated risk of developing chronic kidney disease. Primary care physicians
work closely with nephrologists to define care pathways tailored to each risk category. This
ensures patients get the right interventions at the right time. Since implementing the tool,
we’ve seen a significant increase in early diagnoses. Patients identified through the AI
model are more likely to follow through with labs, start appropriate medications, and
engage in care plans that can potentially delay progression to end-stage renal disease. The
tool also helps providers determine when a patient should be referred to a kidney
specialist, making follow-up more timely and appropriate. For patients who do progress to
dialysis, earlier preparation can mean learning how to manage it at home or making
decisions about care location before it's urgent—ultimately improving quality of life and
access to care.
With colorectal cancer rates rising and screening eligibility now starting at age 45, Sanford
Health built an AI model using 85 risk factors – from genetics to mental health status – to
identify patients at higher risk of colon cancer. Those at elevated risk are prioritized for
colonoscopy, while average-risk patients can use FDA-approved stool-based tests. The AI
model embedded in our EMR will begin rolling out across Sanford in early 2026. While
North Dakota has one of the highest colon cancer rates in the U.S., this AI model will help

guide clinicians in their decision-making for recommended screenings – especially critical
as we face a shortage of GI doctors in the rural Midwest.
Early intervention with high-risk OB patients can prevent complications like preterm birth
or preeclampsia, improving outcomes for both mothers and babies. Our team has
developed an AI tool that scans the EMR for clinical indicators to flag expectant mothers at
higher risk of complications. Providers are alerted and can initiate earlier, more frequent
check-ins.
Rates of lung cancer are rising among non-smokers, particularly women. Traditional
screening guidelines often miss these patients. AI can help identify risk earlier and improve
screening protocols. Sanford Health is piloting a research-based AI model to identify
patients at higher risk for lung cancer using broader data inputs in the electronic medical
record – not just smoking history.
Agentic AI supporting staff shortages
We’re piloting AI voice-based outreach to help patients schedule appointments and close
care gaps (e.g., overdue screenings or follow-ups). The AI-powered platform can tailor
communication based on a patient’s preferred channel (e.g., phone call, text, letter in the
mail, etc.). Personalized outreach helps make preventive care more accessible and
catches health issues before they become more serious, improving outcomes.
Ambient Documentation
Sanford Health has also piloted an AI‑powered ambient documentation solution to reduce
documentation burden and improve efficiency in clinical workflows. The pilot preserved or
increased clinician productivity while lowering time spent in the EHR outside working hours
and decreasing time in notes. Clinicians engaged in the pilot closed approximately 3,000
additional charts on the same day and 4,500 additional charts within seven days, directly
supporting timely follow‑up and care coordination. Documentation quality improved with
more comprehensive and accurate capture of Hierarchical Condition Categories,
enhancing clinical histories, continuity of care, and reimbursement accuracy aligned to
patient acuity.
Dictation volume and cost declined markedly: prior to implementation, the cohort
averaged 16,000 transcribed lines per month with 70 percent from clinic and ED dictation
at about $2,600 per month; after implementation, transcription fell to 5,000 lines per
month, clinic and ED dictation dropped to 6 percent, and costs declined to about $800 per

month. Enterprise analysis indicates an opportunity to eliminate up to 84 percent of
dictation, representing an estimated $2 million in annual savings.
Additional feedback from providers:
▪ 100% said they would be disappointed if they couldn’t use the tool
▪ 100% responded that it was easy to use
▪ 88% reported improvement in burnout or fatigue
▪ 90% reported higher job satisfaction
▪ 90% reported better work-life balance
▪ 95% of clinicians said it reduced cognitive burden
▪ 80% are more likely to continue practicing
Patient experience remained strong, with favorable provider Net Promoter Scores and
“provider spent enough time with me” responses, while clinician surveys indicated
reduced documentation burden and higher satisfaction consistent with observed
efficiency gains. Eventually, expanding ambient technology to additional clinicians,
including nurses and therapists, will increase efficiency across care teams, reduce
documentation burden, and expand capacity to scale services.
Operational efficiencies
AI has the power to offset rural workforce shortages by automating administrative tasks,
triaging patients and even supporting clinical decision-making.
Sanford Health has built an AI tool that uses patient data to predict patients more likely to
miss their appointment. Our teams proactively reach out to address potential barriers – like
transportation or work conflicts – and offer earlier appointments when available. Missed
appointments delay care and increase health disparities, especially in rural areas where
rescheduling can take weeks.
The above examples could also be considered where we are falling short today. Because of
HIPPA restrictions and ambiguity and data sharing regulations, this opens the door for tool
bias, inconsistencies, and possible errors. Additionally, benefits for utilizing clinical-based
AI tools fall to the payors vs. the providers.
Using broader and more standardized data sources, providing funding sources through
enhanced payment models, reducing restrictions on HIPPA related data, and having a
strong self-governance model in place, would allow us to create much stronger tools that
support providers at the bedside and alleviate labor intensive burdens.

Administrative Hurdles for adoption of AI in clinical care include:

8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel
research, and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please
consider specific data types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
Sanford operates a robust set of interoperability tools that support data exchange across
hospitals, retail partners, post-acute facilities, pharmacies, and health plans. This
infrastructure allows Sanford to deliver coordinated care across geographically dispersed
areas without increasing the cost of care.
Despite these capabilities, rural health systems continue to operate within fragmented and
state-based interoperability environments. States maintain or propose separate health
information exchange structures that create friction for multi-state systems.
Enhanced and expanded interoperability supports integration of wearable devices and
remote monitoring into clinical workflows, improving visibility into patient health and
enabling new rural care models, including pharmacist-led interventions that improve
chronic disease management, medication adherence, utilization, and access.

Enhanced interoperability would expand the market for AI tools to work reliably with
reduced bias across diverse populations and settings. Many health care systems that use
training models from internal data possibly lack a full longitudinal picture of a population.
The risk of a non-diverse population used to train models may create an ineffective and
biased clinical tool.
Standardizing clinical notes, imaging, labs, medications, device data, etc. Using FHIR and
USCDI, expansion would increase possibilities for research and development of clinical
care AI tools. However, this will also require regulatory changes to lessen the legal risks of
using expanded data sources.
As interoperability expands, cybersecurity must expand alongside it. Sanford views
cybersecurity as a core utility protecting all digital health functions, including AI,
telehealth, clinical software, and multistate data exchange.
9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see
addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns
do patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical
care?
What providers want:
• Time back in their day to focus on patients: Providers didn’t go to medical school to
stare at a computer screen. AI can automate routine tasks like documentation,
billing codes, prior-authorizations, and help with chart summarization and clinical
notes. AI serves as a tool to alleviate administrative burdens.
• Clinical decision support they can trust: Evidence-based recommendations are
necessary.
• Seamless integration with EHRs: If it’s not embedded in the workflow, it won’t be
used. Rural providers are stretched thin — tech must work with their reality, not add
complexity.
What patients want:
• Better access and faster care: AI-powered chat or triage that shortens wait times
and reduces travel. Reminders for screenings, med refills, or follow-ups.
• Clarity and transparency: Patients want to know how AI is being used in their care —
especially if it influences diagnoses or care decisions.
• Clear explanations: Patients are looking for tools to help explain complex medical
situations
Both patients and caregivers want better care coordination across multiple providers
and are looking for tools to predict chronic and adverse events.

Concerns by patients and/or providers using AI:
Ongoing concerns from both patients and providers are barriers that will take time to
overcome, but earning trust in AI tools and showcasing its’ potential may alleviate and
expedite some of the friction. Concerns from both parties include lack of human
connection, privacy and data security, risk of error and not fully understanding AI (fear of
the unknown). Providers are concerned about job security and bias/fairness with AI tools.
We need to be transparent so that providers and patients understand what AI is doing and
why. It’s also important we emphasize that AI supports – not replaces – the clinician’s
judgement. This is especially important in rural areas where the clinician-patient
relationship is deeply personal. And lastly, showing evidence and demonstrating how AI
improves efficiency, reduces burnout or helps detect health issues earlier. As we continue
to integrate AI in the clinical setting, it’s vital to gain trust by sharing reputable literature,
research and outcomes, choosing high-quality vendors and tools, and highlighting
successes in our own clinical settings.
10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate
the adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
a. Are there published findings about the impact of adopted AI tools and their
use clinical care?
b. How does the literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using
AI as part of clinical care?
By prioritizing AI research dedicated to the economic effects and impact of payment
models, health systems and providers may have more ‘buy in’ to accelerate adoption of AI
in the clinical setting. A stronger trust will be built knowing that the investment of AI is
worth the long-term outcomes, as well as knowing that providers will be compensated
fairly. Currently, providers are burdened with the up-front costs of developing and
purchasing AI tools with payors reaping the benefits. Also, with AI tools relatively new in the
clinical setting, many of the cost-benefits are harder to quantify in the short term, including
reduced provider burnout.
Research that also showcases the effectiveness and safety of AI tools across diverse
populations will build trust and transparence of AI for both providers and patients.
