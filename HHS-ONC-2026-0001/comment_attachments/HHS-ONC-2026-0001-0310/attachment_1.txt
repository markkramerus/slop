Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Request for Information (RFI) on the Use of
AI in Clinical Care.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
The American Nurses Association (ANA) believes the biggest barriers to private-sector innovation
and adoption of AI in clinical care include fragmented and low-trust data environments that hinder
interoperability, unclear and non-harmonized regulatory expectations, and misaligned payment
incentives that fail to reward proven improvements in quality or burden reduction. Additionally,
concerns about safety, bias, and accountability combined with limited workforce readiness and
inadequate nurse engagement in AI governance create cultural and operational resistance. Finally,
usability gaps and poor workflow integration often lead to alert fatigue and erode clinician trust,
slowing widespread adoption.
Some specific areas where there are barriers to innovation include:
1. Safety, Privacy, and Ethics: ANA has foundational concerns with AI innovation in clinical
care, including the lack of shared standards for safety, privacy, ethics, and transparency.
This leads to uncertainty about reliability, model drift, and potential for biased or
incomplete datasets to perpetuate inequities.
2. Clinical Integration and Validation: Some barriers to AI innovation in clinical care include
lack of clinical input during design, misalignment with clinical workflow, insufficient clinical
validation, and limited ability for health systems to test, support, and manage risks of AI
technology.
3. Transparency and Trust: Vendors often treat algorithms as proprietary, preventing
clinicians from understanding output generation, undermining trust, and slowing adoption.
4. Structural and Economic Barriers: Significant hurdles include development cost, limited
IT/Informatics staffing in smaller hospitals, reliance on electronic health record (EHR)
vendors for AI, data quality/interoperability issues, and a lack of clear regulatory
expectations and governance frameworks.
5. Adoption Risks: Providers using personal AI technology outside of approved software could
create patient privacy and cybersecurity risks.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies,
or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
Please provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
ANA has identified the following regulatory and policy changes that HHS can prioritize to further
incentivize effective use of AI in clinical care:

1. Safety and Efficacy Incentives: HHS should prioritize changes that reward safe,
transparent, and workflow-aligned use of AI. This change should be contingent on a
mature digital foundation, data quality, and governance structures capable of
mitigating AI risks, not just mere adoption. ANA also suggests a testing process
proving rigor prior to implementation.
2. Regulatory Updates: Regulations like the Office of the National Coordinator (ONC)
Health IT Certification Program (45 CFR Part 170) should be updated to include
criteria for AI-enabled decision support. This update should cover transparency, bias
testing, explainability, and post-deployment monitoring. ANA also suggests that the
Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy and Security Rule
(45 CFR Part 164) could be refined to address secondary data use for AI training and
vendor data retention.
3. Payment Policy Alignment: CMS should align the Quality Payment Program (42 CFR
Part 414) with AI that demonstrably improves outcomes, reduces documentation
burden, or enhances safety, explicitly recognizing reductions in cognitive load and
alert fatigue.
4. Programmatic Support and Oversight: ANA encourages HHS to support
standardized AI governance frameworks and clinical AI evaluation toolkits,
emphasizing nursing and frontline clinician involvement. Furthermore, Conditions of
Participation (42 CFR Part 482) should clarify expectations for human oversight,
incident reporting, and managing AI model drift and health safety events.
5. Compliance and Unapproved Tools: Including the use of any AI product not officially
approved as Certified EHR Technology into regulatory and HIPAA requirements could
help enforce compliance.
3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel
legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability
structures ( e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel
legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help
address them?
ANA recognizes that there can be legal and implementation issues regarding AI usage in clinical
care, which include the following: unclear liability and indemnification when AI augments clinical
decisions; privacy concerns around secondary data use for model training; and risks of bias and
discrimination that challenge civil rights compliance. These gaps create uncertainty for providers
and vendors. HHS should play a proactive role by issuing guidance on accountability frameworks,
privacy-by-design standards, and bias mitigation. Additionally, HHS can promote standardized
incident reporting and require transparency and post-deployment monitoring in procurement
contracts to ensure safe and equitable AI adoption.
Specific areas of concern include:
1. Reliable patient notification of when AI is “listening:” Organizations cannot depend on
individual practitioners to consistently and correctly disclose AI use to patients and families
in real time. Verbal notification varies by person, workflow pace, and setting, and it breaks
down most in high-acuity situations, which is the very environment where ambient
documentation or decision-support tools are the most useful. Patients also face a practical
transparency problem—they cannot easily tell when an AI tool is actively capturing,
transcribing, or analyzing their information versus when it is “off.” Blanket organizational

statements (“we use AI”) do not solve this issue as they do not provide meaningful,
situational awareness and can fail to meet expectations for informed decision-making.
Guardrails that standardize how and when AI use is communicated—especially for tools
that listen in the background—would promote consistency, reduce confusion, and
strengthen trust across care settings.
2. Consent and patient rights in AI-enabled care: A major gap is the lack of clear, consistent
expectations for whether, when, and how patients should be informed about and consent to
AI use in their care—particularly when AI influences triage, recommendations, or
documentation. Existing privacy frameworks were not designed for algorithmic decision
support, automated triage, or predictive analytics, leaving uncertainty about what
constitutes adequate notice and meaningful consent. Patients often have no right to know
when AI is used, how their data contributes to outputs, or whether their information is used
to refine models. This ambiguity undermines trust and creates uncertainty for health
systems. A clearer approach would define baseline patient rights: clear notice when AI is
involved, plain language explanations of what the AI is doing, and a consent pathway for
sensitive uses.
3. Secondary use of personal health information (PHI) for AI training, development, and
vendor retention: Secondary use of PHI for AI model development, training, refinement,
and vendor retention raises distinct governance concerns that current rules address
inconsistently. Even where de-identified data is used, modern re-identification risks
challenge the adequacy of older de-identification assumptions. Patients and clinicians may
not know if data is being stored, repurposed, or used to improve models—particularly when
vendors retain datasets or use them across clients. This creates both ethical and
operational risk: trust erodes when the data lifecycle is opaque, and organizations face
compliance uncertainty when vendor data practices are unclear. Stronger guardrails would
emphasize data minimization, defined retention limits, explicit disclosure when data
contributes to model training/refinement, and patient-facing clarity about what happens to
data after it is collected.
4. Accountability and liability for AI-influenced clinical decisions: When AI plays a role in
clinical care, accountability can become blurred, especially if the tool is not regulated as a
medical device. As malpractice claims emerge, it may be difficult to isolate the AI’s
contribution to decision-making, identify whether the clinician reasonably relied on the
output, and determine what obligations the organization and vendor had to ensure
performance and transparency. Clinicians can be held responsible for decisions influenced
by systems they cannot fully evaluate, while vendors may attempt to disclaim responsibility
via contract language. This mismatch creates legal and ethical tension and may chill
adoption or push unsafe, inconsistent workarounds. A shared-accountability model would
clarify roles: what clinicians must verify, what organizations must govern and monitor, and
what vendors must disclose and support when their tools influence care.
5. Transparency requirements for AI tools and vendors: Transparency is a prerequisite for
trust and safe use. Without clear expectations, patients may not understand when AI is part
of their care, and clinicians may not know the limits of a tool, what data sources drive
outputs, or what conditions degrade performance. Transparency also matters for auditing; if
outcomes are questioned, organizations need traceability (what tool version was used,
what data inputs were considered, and how outputs were presented). Vendor transparency
expectations—such as clear documentation, known limitations, update notices, and
auditability—help reduce variability across health systems and prevent “black box”
adoption that shifts risk onto frontline clinicians and patients.

6. Continuous monitoring, drift, bias, unintended consequences: Traditional governance
structures often treat clinical tools as relatively static, but AI systems can change in
performance over time as populations shift, workflows evolve, or models are updated.
Without a requirement or norm for ongoing monitoring, tools may drift, embed bias, or
introduce unintended consequences without being detected promptly. Continuous
monitoring is therefore a core patient safety issue, and not just an IT issue. Practical
guardrails would normalize lifecycle oversight: routine performance checks, bias
surveillance, post-deployment validation, incident reporting pathways, and clear triggers for
retraining, rollback, or removal—shared across vendors and health systems.
7. AI-specific privacy and security risks: Current security expectations do not consistently
address risks that are uniquely amplified by AI integration, including algorithmic
vulnerabilities, training-data poisoning, and risks introduced by large-scale model
connectivity and automation. As AI becomes embedded in clinical workflows, security
failures can compromise not only confidentiality but also clinical integrity, through
manipulated inputs, corrupted training signals, or altered outputs. Modernized guidance
would help organizations implement safeguards proportionate to AI risk: tighter access
controls, robust logging and audit trails, model/data integrity checks, and vendor
requirements that address AI-specific threat models. Standardizing these expectations
would reduce variability across organizations and strengthen baseline protections for
patients.
8. Explicit consent for feeding patient data into AI models: Consent becomes especially
important when patient data is used beyond direct care, such as training, fine-tuning, or
improving an AI model. Patients must have clear, understandable information about
whether their data will be used to develop or refine algorithms, how long it will be retained,
who will have access, and whether it can be used across clients or products. Even when de-
identification is claimed, patients may reasonably expect transparency and choice given re-
identification risks and the sensitivity of health data. A practical guardrail approach would
separate “AI used to support my care today” from “my data used to improve AI for future
use,” with clear consent/opt-out mechanisms for the latter whenever feasible.
4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and
post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered
evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so,
which mechanisms would be most impactful (e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative
agreements, and/or prize competitions)?
ANA recognizes that there are promising evaluation methods, metrics, and other considerations for
AI use in clinical care, including:
1. Evaluation Approaches: The most promising evaluation approaches treat AI as a clinical
intervention, not just a predictive model, and they should include frameworks assessing
capability, utility, and adoption. Post-deployment evaluations should be prospective and
measure changes in clinician behavior and patient outcomes, utilizing trials, A/B testing,
stepped-wedge designs, and continuous monitoring for issues like model drift and bias.
2. Pre-deployment Technical Validation: These should include metrics similar to clinical
trials, calibration, fairness metrics, and robustness testing (e.g., performance under data
shifts, missing data, and adversarial scenarios). External validation on data from multiple
sites and populations is essential.

3. Workflow and Human-Centered Evaluation: Methods like usability testing, simulation-
based assessments, time-motion studies, and cognitive load measures are important for
identifying how AI fits into clinical workflows, reduces burden, and supports clinical
judgment. Evaluating explainable AI is also key to ensuring clinicians can understand,
trust, and act on outputs.
4. Specific Metrics: ANA’s suggested metrics include time saved documenting and
increased time at the bedside, readmission rates to evaluate discharge planning and
education, surgical site infection rates, and timely clinical data in the chart.
5. HHS Support Mechanisms: The most impactful mechanisms for HHS to support these
processes are targeted grants and cooperative agreements, contract and certification
guidance, and support for learning collaboratives and shared evaluation infrastructure.
Education on AI use and documentation should be required at the school/education level
rather than per healthcare organization.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification,
industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in
clinical care?
As HHS seeks to strengthen private-sector efforts, ANA urges the agency to endorse standardized
accreditation and certification frameworks for healthcare AI that incorporate transparency, bias
mitigation, and safety criteria. Additionally, HHS should fund nurse-focused AI education and
micro-credentialing programs through ANA and the American Nurses Credentialing Center (ANCC)
to build workforce readiness. These steps will promote confidence, ensure ethical deployment, and
position nurses as key partners in advancing effective AI adoption across clinical care.
1. Support for Private Sector Efforts: HHS should recognize and endorse trustworthy private
frameworks (accreditation, certification, industry-driven testing) without mandating a single
model, and use funding/procurement levers to prioritize vendors and health systems using
accredited/certified AI tools.
2. Certification and Vendor Requirements: Certification of AI tools is desired to ensure a
certain level of rigor in testing, and HHS should work with EHR vendors should be required
to meet Certified Technology Requirements to assist smaller organizations with limited
resources.
3. Infrastructure and Training: HHS must support shared infrastructure and learning
collaboratives to lower the barrier for smaller organizations, and require clinicians to be
trained within their licensure for safe and accurate technology use.
4. Promote AI Use and Human-Centered Design: HHS can promote the use of AI in clinical
care, such as "smart" hospital rooms, and explicitly center human-centered design and
workflow fit in any supported accreditation or certification efforts.
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have
the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and
help reduce costs?
ANA acknowledges that some AI tools have exceeded expectations in areas like ambient
documentation and workflow assistants, which, according to those companies, significantly
reduce charting time and improve usability. This includes imaging, where validated models

enhance diagnostic accuracy. However, many tools have fallen short due to poor workflow
integration, alert fatigue, and lack of transparency, leading to low clinician trust. Importantly, we
lack strong evidence that these tools reduce workload or mitigate burnout, underscoring the urgent
need for standardized outcome measures that capture clinician burden and well-being. Looking
forward, the greatest potential lies in nurse-centric solutions such as explainable triage and risk
stratification, ambient nursing documentation with reasoning support, staffing optimization tools
that improve well-being, and AI-driven quality measurement systems designed to reduce burden
while improving patient outcomes.
Specific examples include:
1. Success of AI Tools: Examples include AI-assisted imaging for faster turnaround times,
capacity and throughput tools for bed management, and operational optimizations. Mobile
Point of Care Documentation devices for nursing have also greatly improved bedside care,
as well as ambient listening for provider documentation.
2. Shortcomings of AI Tools: AI tools fall short when they are generic, layered into already
fragile systems, increase alert fatigue, add clicks, provide unclear recommendations
without explainability, or do not translate into better outcomes due to a lack of underlying
workflows/resources. Some predictive models, models trained on biased data, or tools that
reinforce existing inequities have also fallen short. A lack of clinician input in development,
particularly for nursing workflows, can also lead to tools that don't improve workflows and
result in passed-on costs to patients without clear benefits.
3. Greatest Potential for Novel AI Tools: The greatest potential lies in tools that directly
support patient safety, nursing workflow, and proactive care, such as patient-movement
and safety monitoring tools (e.g., detecting unsafe movement, predicting fall risk). Other
high-potential tools include ambient and workflow-aware documentation support for
nurses, AI-enabled care coordination and handoff tools, and operational/staffing
optimization tools that align staffing with acuity and patient needs in real time. These tools
should be deeply embedded in workflows and evaluated as clinical interventions.
4. Need for Data: There is a current lack of sufficient data on the performance and cost-
effectiveness of AI tools.
7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations
have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary
administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
ANA believes that the adoption of AI in clinical care is most influenced by Chief Nursing Officers,
nursing informatics leaders, and quality or operations executives, especially when nurses have a
formal role in governance. However, there are administrative hurdles such as, unclear ownership of
AI initiatives, lengthy procurement processes, and limited time or resources for workforce training,
which slow implementation and reduce frontline engagement.
1. Influential Roles: Executive sponsorship is mentioned as having an impact on AI adoption.
2. Cost and Resources: The most significant barriers include the cost of AI add-on solutions
and the lack of implementation resources, with vendors shifting costs and risk to
organizations.
3. Governance and Accountability: Primary administrative hurdles stem from fragmented
governance, unclear accountability, and a lack of foundational digital maturity, leading to

prolonged contracting and legal review due to the absence of a consistent framework for
addressing data use, liability, and vendor accountability.
4. Government Role: HHS can best support adoption by establishing clear, consistent
expectations for safety, transparency, and accountability, and by defining minimum criteria
for responsible clinical AI, such as bias testing and monitoring, while vendors build
accreditation and testing programs.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and
accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data types,
data standards, and benchmarking tools.
ANA urges HHS to provide incentives to accelerate and support the development of AI for clinical
care in the following ways:
1. Data Interoperability: Enhanced interoperability would significantly widen market
opportunities and accelerate AI development by improving access to high-quality,
standardized clinical data, which is essential for reliable AI model training and safer
deployment.
2. High-Value Data Types: Prioritizing interoperability for data types such as vital signs,
laboratory results, medication administration records, device data, care plan, nursing
assessment, imaging metadata, and patient-generated health data is crucial.
3. Standards and APIs: Greater adoption of fast healthcare interoperability resources (FHIR)-
based application programming interfaces (APIs), United States Core Data for
Interoperability (USCDI) expansion, and consistent implementation of health level (HL7)
standards would reduce variability and allow vendors to build tools that work across various
systems.
4. Benchmark Datasets: Shared, de-identified benchmark datasets built on common data
models like observational medical outcomes partnership (OMOP) would accelerate
research by allowing models to be evaluated against consistent reference standards.
5. Specific Areas: Enhanced interoperability for device and IT data is important for AI tools
focused on patient safety, mobility monitoring, and early deterioration detection, and
secure databases of de-identified health information could also help.
9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by
the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and
caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
ANA knows that patients and caregivers want AI to address challenges like reducing delays in care,
improving communication, and freeing clinicians from excessive documentation so they can spend
more time delivering care. At the same time, they have concerns about privacy, data misuse, bias,
and the risk that over-automation could erode human interaction and accountability. Transparent
communication and strong nursing involvement are essential to building trust and ensuring AI
adoption aligns with patient-centered values.
Specific challenges include:
1. Desired AI-Addressed Challenges: Patients and caregivers want AI to address safety risks,
care coordination gaps, long wait times, navigating the complex system, and preventing

avoidable harm. They also desire improvements in communication between clinicians,
reduced delays in diagnosis, ensuring nothing is missed in care, better access to primary
care, and addressing clinician burnout.
2. Patient Education/Understanding: A major challenge is the general public's lack of
education and skepticism regarding AI, particularly in rural areas with poor health and
technology literacy, which hinders understanding of how data is used and how AI functions.
3. Concerns about AI: Concerns center around privacy, consent, the potential for AI to
replace human judgment, reduce relational aspects of care, introduce bias or inequity, and
create new errors that are difficult to detect. Patients also worry about the accountability of
clinicians for algorithm-influenced decisions.
4. Organizational/Systemic Challenges: Small hospitals with limited knowledge and
resources struggle to prioritize and afford updated AI technology, and organizations cannot
solely take on the responsibility of re-educating the public.
5. Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) and Resources: AI is desired to help link patients
with resources related to SDOH challenges to ensure they can attend appointments and
follow up on prescribed care.
10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the
adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
ANA urges HHS to prioritize research on standardized outcome measures to assess AI’s impact on
clinician workload, burnout, and equity, as current evidence is limited. Additional focus areas
include safety and bias mitigation, cost-benefit analyses, and real-world effectiveness studies
through nurse-led trials.
1. Prioritized AI Research: HHS should focus on AI research that evaluates real-world impact,
safety, equity, and burden reduction, including prospective studies of AI as a clinical
intervention, human factors, workflow impact, economic evaluation of
implementation/maintenance, and systematic bias assessment.
2. Need for Further Literature/Studies: The existing literature is considered lacking,
necessitating more long-term studies to understand the impact and return on investment,
as well as a need for more rigorous, standardized economic evaluations that capture
indirect costs like workflow redesign, training, governance, and monitoring.
3. Existing Literature Findings: A growing body of literature indicates that AI can improve
diagnostic accuracy, workflow efficiency, and patient outcomes, but many studies are
limited, specialty-specific, often optimistic, and inconsistent in reporting safety events,
equity impacts, or long-term effectiveness.
4. Implementation Considerations: It is important to promote mobile point-of-care
implementation and avoid a "one-size-fits-all" approach, as each discipline and setting will
have unique needs, requiring consideration of change management and the mental load on
clinicians.
