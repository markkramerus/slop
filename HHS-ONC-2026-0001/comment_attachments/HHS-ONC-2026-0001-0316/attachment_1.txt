PCMA is the national association representing the country’s pharmacy benefit managers
(PBMs), which administer prescription drug plans (PDPs) and operate specialty pharmacies for
more than 289 million Americans with health coverage through Fortune 500 companies, health
insurers, labor unions, Medicare, Medicaid, the Federal Employees Health Benefits Program,
and the Exchanges established by the Affordable Care Act. PBMs are hired by employers,
unions, government programs, and others, to drive down prescription drug costs and administer
prescription drug plans for more than 289 million Americans. Before getting into specifics on
RULE, there are four things to know about PBMs:
• PBMs are the only part of the drug supply chain whose primary role is to lower
prescription drug costs. On average, they save patients and families about $1,154 per
person each year.2
• PBMs are extremely effective at reducing prescription drug costs for employers and
patients, which is why some industries that profit from high drug prices oppose them.
• For the enormous savings and value that PBMs provide, they operate on thin profit
margins.

PBMs play a critical role at the intersection of clinical decision-making, benefit administration,
and cost management. Our members have long-standing experience in using advanced
analytics, automation, and decision-support tools to improve medication safety, ensure
appropriate utilization, and lower costs for patients, plan sponsors, and taxpayers. Several of
these tools are integrated directly into prescribers’ workflows at the point of care, providing real-
time information on coverage, cost-sharing, prior authorization requirements, and clinically
appropriate alternatives to support informed treatment decisions. PCMA supports the
responsible and ethical use of artificial intelligence (AI) to advance innovation and efficiency,
and improve patient support. As AI-enabled tools become more prevalent in health care delivery
and administration, it is essential that federal policy fosters responsible adoption while avoiding
regulatory approaches that could inadvertently impede innovation, increase costs, or shift
burdens onto patients and providers.
I. Regulatory Considerations
PCMA strongly supports a risk-based, use-case-specific approach to the regulation of AI in
health care. AI applications vary significantly in function and risk, and regulatory oversight
should reflect those differences. PBMs increasingly deploy AI-enabled tools to support coverage
determinations that may involve a clinical component, including prior authorization and step
therapy. These tools are designed to apply established clinical guidelines, benefit parameters,
and plan sponsor requirements consistently and efficiently, often with human oversight and
established appeal rights. When appropriately designed and governed, such tools can reduce
administrative burden, improve consistency, and support timely access to medically appropriate
care.
Federal policy should consider the level of risk involved in the deployment of AI solutions in
crafting guardrails and, where possible, allow for oversight through existing federal and state
frameworks, including Medicare Advantage and Part D requirements, ERISA fiduciary
obligations, state utilization review laws, and civil rights protections. Layering new, technology-
specific requirements on top of these frameworks risks creating duplication and uncertainty
without corresponding benefits for patients. To ensure a consistent and trusted approach,
PCMA supports the use of widely used and respected frameworks for responsible use of AI,
such as the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Principles on
Artificial Intelligence, and the U.S. Department of Commerce’s National Institute of Standards
and Technology (NIST) Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework. We encourage HHS
to work with NIST and other standards-development organizations to further develop and refine
voluntary, consensus-based industry standards, as well as risk-based frameworks and best
practices.
In order to build patient trust in the use of AI tools in clinical care, it is imperative that any
regulatory framework requires that AI tools that involve clinical decision-making facilitate, but
never substitute for, decision-making by clinicians, consistent with “human-in-the-loop” AI
models. In addition, transparency about the use of AI tools and robust national privacy
standards will help build the regulatory framework for AI to flourish.

Finally, as states have increasingly begun to regulate the use of AI, the multitude of varying
state laws and requirements is becoming a barrier to the development and implementation of AI
solutions. It is therefore essential that the appropriate regulation of AI in the health care sector
be established at the federal level. This will provide clarity and consistency to ensure that the
regulatory burden does not inhibit ongoing innovation to deliver better care and health
outcomes.
II. Reimbursement Considerations: Aligning Incentives to Encourage
Adoption
Although AI has demonstrated potential to reduce errors, improve efficiency, and lower costs,
current reimbursement and payment structures often fail to recognize or reward its use. This
misalignment presents a significant barrier to adoption, particularly in federal health programs
where payment incentives strongly shape care delivery and administrative processes.
AI-enabled decision support can enhance medication therapy management, improve
adherence, reduce inappropriate utilization, and streamline administrative workflows. These
improvements benefit patients through better access and lower out-of-pocket costs, while also
generating savings for plan sponsors and government programs. However, the entities that
invest in AI infrastructure and governance often do not directly capture the value created under
existing reimbursement models.
PCMA encourages HHS to consider how reimbursement policy can better support adoption of
AI tools that demonstrably improve quality, reduce errors, and reduce total cost of care,
particularly in areas where adoption has been slow or tentative because of investment,
regulatory, or other hurdles. Potential approaches include incorporating the effective use of AI-
enabled decision-support tools into value-based payment models, quality initiatives, and shared
savings arrangements, and aligning AI use with performance benchmarks where providers have
clear incentives to deploy tools that improve quality and efficiency. Additionally, HHS should
ensure that reimbursement rules do not treat AI investments as purely administrative costs
divorced from clinical value but should recognize that they also deliver meaningful value to
patients and the health care system. Private payers should be allowed to use reimbursement
models that incentivize the use of AI solutions that improve quality, efficiency, and more cost-
effective care.
In Medicare and Medicaid, targeted demonstration projects could help evaluate how AI adoption
by PBMs, plans, and providers affects outcomes, administrative efficiency, and program
spending. Without clearer pathways for aligning incentives, adoption of AI is likely to remain
uneven, limiting its ability to deliver system-wide benefits.
III. Research and Development: Supporting Innovation While Preserving
Flexibility
PCMA supports continued federal engagement in AI research and development, particularly
efforts that advance interoperability, data quality, and real-world validation. At the same time,
R&D policy should be structured to complement private-sector innovation and avoid
constraining model development or iteration.
PBMs and other stakeholders are actively investing in AI tools tailored to specific populations,

benefit designs, and clinical contexts. Federal efforts should prioritize access to high-quality
data, promote public-private collaboration, and support research into bias mitigation and
governance best practices, while allowing organizations flexibility to adapt tools as evidence and
standards evolve.
Regulatory clarity is also essential to sustaining R&D investment. Clear signals that low-risk,
non-autonomous AI applications will not be subject to disproportionate oversight will encourage
continued innovation and responsible deployment.
Conclusion
AI and machine learning have the potential to improve clinical care, enhance administrative
efficiency, and reduce costs across the health care system. PBMs are well-positioned to deploy
these tools in ways that benefit patients, plan sponsors, and federal health programs. To fully
realize this potential, federal policy should emphasize a risk-based regulatory framework and
reimbursement models that align incentives with value, while relying on existing safeguards
where appropriate.
PCMA appreciates the opportunity to provide input, and it looks forward to continued
engagement with the Department as it advances policies to responsibly accelerate the adoption
of AI in health care.
