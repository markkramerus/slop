Subject: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care
Submitted Electronically
To Whom It May Concern:
The American Psychological Association Services, Inc. (APA) respectfully submits the
following response to your Request for Information. APA Services is the companion
organization of the American Psychological Association, which is the nation’s largest scientific
and professional nonprofit organization representing the discipline and profession of psychology,
as well as over 190,000 members and affiliates who are clinicians, researchers, educators,
consultants, and students in psychological science. APASI emphasizes that while AI offers
transformative potential for diagnostic precision and administrative efficiency, its deployment
must be governed by the foundational principles of psychological science. To ensure these tools
are effective, ethical, and equitable, the federal government must prioritize a human-centric
regulatory framework that safeguards patient safety and restores public trust.
APASI recognizes AI’s immense potential to revolutionize critical sectors, especially health
care. AI can enhance diagnostic precision, expand access to behavioral health treatment, and
alleviate administrative burdens that cause provider burnout. However, a sizeable barrier to the
development, deployment, and adoption of beneficial AI is the profound and justified lack of
public trust. As the RFI notes, "foster[ing] public trust" is "critical" for adoption. This trust is
fragile because the current market is flooded with unregulated, direct-to-consumer AI products
that make deceptive claims and pose significant risks.
The RFI correctly identifies "lack of regulatory clarity" and "regulatory mismatches" as primary
barriers. This ambiguity has created a digital Wild West that hinders the adoption of safe and
effective AI tools. To promote innovation, we must address these gaps by establishing clear,
evidence-based guardrails. This requires embedding psychological science—the study of human
cognition, behavior, and emotion—into the entire AI lifecycle -- the end-to-end process of
developing and deploying an AI system.
Below, we identify specific barriers and recommendations corresponding to the RFI's questions.

I. Regulation
HHS must establish a regulatory framework that prioritizes patient safety and public trust.
• Prohibit Misrepresentation of AI as Licensed Professionals: A primary barrier to trust
is the "regulatory mismatch" allowing AI chatbots to misrepresent themselves as
"psychologists" or "therapists". For instance, a chatbots on a popular platform presenting
itself as a "psychologist," engaged in millions of chats and, in some cases, appeared to
validate users’ violent thoughts. Congress and HHS should make it illegal for AI to
generate fraudulent credentials or misrepresent itself as a licensed professional.
• Mandate Transparency and "Human-in-the-Loop": AI should be regulated as a tool
to augment, not replace, the clinical judgment and therapeutic relationships that are the
bedrock of quality care. Regulations must mandate persistent disclosure that a user is
interacting with an AI system and ensure a qualified human remains in the loop for high-
stakes clinical decisions.
• Strict Transparency and Informed Consent: It is imperative that more is known about
the data and methods used to train AI systems in the healthcare setting. Developers must
also be required to clearly, conspicuously, and persistently disclose to users that they are
interacting with an AI system, not a human. In clinical settings, authorities must obtain
informed consent, so patients understand how AI tools like predictive algorithms are
being used and specifically, what personally identifiable information is being retained
and accountability for information being released to third parties.
• Professional Competence and Liability: Policy should support continuing education for
the workforce, as professionals utilizing AI must maintain competence in the
technologies they employ. Over-reliance on technology without proper training,
oversight, and disclosure can lead to harm.
II. Reimbursement
Payment policies must incentivize evidence-based, high-value interventions rather than mere
engagement metrics.
• Incentivize Safety and Efficacy over Volume: Reimbursement should be tied to robust
clinical outcome data—such as remission rates and improvements in health-related
quality of life—rather than corporate metrics like user engagement or number of
downloads.
• Support Interdisciplinary Care: Payment models should incentivize the use of AI to
expand the reach of clinicians within a human-led care team, ensuring technology does
not displace the essential therapeutic relationships that are the bedrock of quality care.
This is essential in an environment where states are moving to limit the ability of
qualified providers to use evidence-based technologies to aid in the delivery of care.
III. Research & Development
The pace of AI development is far outpacing independent scientific research.

• Embed Psychological Science in the AI Lifecycle: Policy should mandate that AI
developers collaborate with psychological scientists during the design phase to mitigate
risks related to cognitive bias and emotional manipulation. This same integration should
take place among federal regulatory, investigatory, and research teams tasked with
developing AI policies.
• Mitigate Bias and Disparities: AI models often reflect biases from non-representative
training data, such as an algorithm that unfairly assigned lower risk scores to Black
patients because it used healthcare costs as a proxy for illness. HHS must require
rigorous, independent pre-deployment testing and ongoing monitoring for harms across
diverse populations to ensure AI reduces, rather than entrenches, health disparities.
• Investment in Independent Research: HHS should prioritize significant federal
investment in independent, longitudinal research to understand the long-term impacts of
human-AI interaction on mental health and social development. This research must be
free from corporate conflicts of interest and facilitate data collection and transparency
requirements for companies seeking to provide health-care related products reliant on AI
technologies.
IV. Data Privacy and Vulnerable Populations
Current privacy laws are "structurally incompatible" with AI’s capability to infer intimate details
from vast datasets.
• Ensure HIPAA Compliance and Data Privacy: Current privacy frameworks are
structurally incompatible with AI’s ability to infer intimate mental health details from
collected data. HHS must establish a right to “mental privacy,” safeguarding data that can
indicate a person's emotional state without explicit patient consent. Furthermore, many
current AI chatbots are not HIPAA compliant, leaving patient privacy and security of
their health data unprotected by federal law.
• "Safe-by-Default" Standards for Youth: Protections for children and adolescents must
be the default setting, not an opt-in. This includes limiting manipulative design features
that exploit the "neurodevelopmental mismatch" of adolescence, characterized by high
social reward seeking and low impulse control.
Conclusion
APASI believes AI holds the potential to create a more accessible, effective, and equitable
healthcare system. The path to realizing this potential is not through deregulation, but through
the creation of a clear, evidence-based framework that builds public trust. The current lack of
regulatory clarity is the primary key concern to AI adoption.
We must ensure AI is a tool to augment, not replace, the clinical judgment and therapeutic
relationships that are the bedrock of quality care. The APASI and its member scientists stand
ready to collaborate with OSTP to develop and enact policies that advance these principles.
