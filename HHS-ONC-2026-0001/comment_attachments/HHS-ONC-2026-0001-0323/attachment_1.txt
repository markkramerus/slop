Dear Deputy Secretary O'Neill:
As nursing stakeholders, the Alliance for Nursing Informatics (ANI) is pleased to offer comments
on “Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care.”
The Alliance for Nursing Informatics (ANI), cosponsored by the American Medical Informatics
Association (AMIA) and the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society
(HIMSS), advances nursing informatics leadership, practice, education, policy, and research
through a unified voice of nursing informatics organizations. We transform health and
healthcare through nursing informatics and innovation. ANI is a collaboration of organizations
representing more than 25,000 nurse informaticists and bringing together 29 distinct nursing
informatics groups globally. ANI crosses academia, practice, industry, and nursing specialty
boundaries and collaborates with more than 4 million nurses in practice today.
Below, we provide responses to each of the questions included in the RFI. In addition, we offer
the broad suggestion that HHS and policymakers consider conceptualizing AI through the lens
of care delivery; it should be layered on top of areas where evidence is fragmented or weak,
but not replace the honed clinical judgment of expert clinicians or the therapeutic relationship
they establish with patients and families. Through this lens, we highlight that nurses provide
continuous care for hospitalized patients and are the principal documenters of the clinical data
that AI models should be developed, trained, validated, and deployed using this data- a current
limitation of the tools in development. Nurses are also at the forefront of care delivery,
interfacing with patients and families more than any other health profession, and will thus
witness the effects of AI on patient care most clearly. Additionally, we highlight that this
frontline positioning exposes nurses to professional liability; nurses may be the first to detect

AI-related errors and may face documentation, accountability, and liability questions when AI
meaningfully informs decisions. As such, their voices must be centered in AI development,
testing, and deployment decisions. Without nursing engagement, AI tools risk being ineffective,
burdensome, or even harmful to patient care.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
A significant barrier includes the substantial return on investment requirements and the
level of effort needed for each individual health system to implement and validate AI
tools.1 The burden of implementation, testing, and outcome measurement is
considerable and often falls disproportionately on already resource-constrained clinical
teams. Additionally, there is insufficient cross-collaboration infrastructure to effectively
vet, test, and demonstrate AI tools across different care settings before widespread
adoption. Health systems are often operating in silos, each independently validating
tools that have already been tested elsewhere, creating inefficiency and slowing the
pace of innovation. The lack of standardized evaluation frameworks means that
promising AI tools may struggle to gain traction even when they have demonstrated
efficacy in specific contexts. We also note developer and implementer access to high-
quality EHR data as a barrier; they often have limited access to representative data, and
there is variability in documentation/data quality and constraints on using data for
development/validation (privacy, governance, contracting). This slows innovation and
increases the local validation burden. Finally, interoperability is a barrier. Inconsistent
data models/terminologies, variable EHR configurations, and limited standardized
interfaces make it difficult to deploy AI tools across sites and to compare outcomes,
contributing to duplicated implementation effort.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS
regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to
develop or use AI in clinical care?
We suggest that HHS regulations and policies be examined to ensure they facilitate safe
care transitions both within settings (such as from ICU to step-down to home) and
across settings (such as from acute care to post-acute or long-term care), for example,
through AI-generated handoff summaries and discharge planning. The focus on USCDI
data element sets provides important guidance, yet we need policies that enable this
minimum data set to flow seamlessly across the continuum of care. Current
fragmentation in data sharing creates barriers to AI tools that could support care
coordination and prevent adverse events during transitions. Further, we urge caution
around payment policies or programs that reward or incentivize the adoption of
evidence-based AI tools without commensurate investments in technical infrastructure
and interoperability capabilities across care settings nationally. Presently, many health
systems and care delivery organizations- particularly rural hospitals, critical access
hospitals, and long-term care, lack basic technical infrastructure to adopt AI, and such
programs might exacerbate differences in resources and patient outcomes.1

3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel
legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability
structures. What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any,
should HHS play to help address them?
A critical implementation issue that HHS must address is the lack of general education
and competency development about AI across the clinical workforce. Healthcare
professionals lack a foundational understanding of how to use AI tools safely, interpret
their outputs appropriately, and recognize their limitations. This knowledge gap creates
significant patient safety risks and liability concerns.2 HHS should play a leadership role
in establishing baseline AI competency standards for clinical professionals and
supporting educational initiatives to build AI literacy across disciplines. This includes
understanding when AI recommendations should be questioned, how bias can manifest
in AI outputs, and what data quality issues might affect model performance. The rapid
pace of AI deployment has far outstripped workforce education, and HHS has a unique
role in setting standards and supporting training infrastructure that ensures clinicians
can be safe, effective users of AI tools. Another critical issue is model transparency.
Clinicians and end users must be able to understand how AI tools generate their
recommendations and suggestions in order to appropriately evaluate and act on them.
Without sufficient transparency into model reasoning and decision-making processes,
clinicians cannot fulfill their professional obligation to exercise independent clinical
judgment, and patients cannot meaningfully participate in shared decision-making
about their care. HHS should encourage or require transparency standards that enable
clinicians to understand the basis for AI-generated outputs, including the data sources,
logic, and confidence levels underlying recommendations, so that AI serves as a tool
that supports informed clinical reasoning and the safest delivery of care. Specific aspects
of concerns include: (1) auditability (accessible logs of AI inputs/outputs, model
versioning, and user actions), (2) user verification requirements (expectations that
clinicians verify AI outputs before acting), (3) mechanisms to correct or override AI-
generated content with a documented rationale, and (4) clarity on standard-of-care
implications and liability allocation across vendor, health system, and clinician when AI
meaningfully informs decisions.
4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre-
and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-
centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these
processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most impactful?
The field of AI evaluation is in nascent stages and, while many have proposed
frameworks, benchmarking methods, and metrics, there are no agreed-upon standards.
HHS should support rigorous evaluation of AI tools through grant mechanisms that
enable both pre-deployment testing and post-deployment monitoring. Grants provide
the most flexible and impactful mechanism for supporting systematic evaluation
research because they allow partnerships between academia, practice, and industry to
develop comprehensive evaluation frameworks that include workflow analysis, human
factors assessment, and real-world effectiveness studies. We note that biomedical
informatics professionals have been conducting this research for decades and are

already well equipped with the skills, knowledge, and resources to conduct such work.
Furthermore, we feel that evaluation methods must include human-centered design
principles and incorporate feedback from the clinicians who will actually use these tools
in practice. Examples include robustness testing across workflow conditions and data
quality variation, post-deployment drift monitoring, and human factors evaluation (e.g.,
usability testing and cognitive walkthroughs). Too often, AI tools are developed without
meaningful clinician input and fail to integrate smoothly into existing workflows,
creating new burdens rather than alleviating them. Grant-funded evaluation studies
should be required to include multiple distinct clinical settings and populations to
ensure that AI tools perform equally well across different contexts and patient groups.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities to promote innovative and
effective AI use in clinical care?
Currently, many AI tools are developed by technology companies, computer scientists,
and engineers with limited clinical input, resulting in products that may be technically
sophisticated but clinically impractical or unsafe.3 HHS should consider establishing
requirements that clinicians, including nurses, who are the largest health profession,
must be meaningfully included in all stages of AI development and testing. HHS could
operationalize this by embedding clinician partnership expectations into
certification/accreditation criteria, as well as through HHS funding mechanisms (grants,
pilots, demonstrations), CMS program participation requirements and incentives, and
ONC/ASTP implementation guidance tied to interoperability/health IT policy. Naming
these levers will make the recommendation actionable and enforceable. This
requirement would not position these clinicians as advisors but rather as partners in
defining requirements, testing prototypes, and validating real-world performance. HHS
can support private sector innovation by creating certification or accreditation pathways
that recognize and reward developers who engage in robust clinician co-design and
testing processes. This would create market incentives for better development practices
while ensuring that AI tools are truly fit for purpose in clinical care. Also, we note that
many of the clinical health information technologies are developed and produced by
one very large health record vendor that uses a similar design and testing cycle for each
innovation. This requirement would discourage "smaller" innovators from embedding
design flaws in a large group of AI products.

expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would

quality, and help reduce costs?
There is significant promise in the generative, ambient, and agentic AI space to assist
with nursing care and documentation efficiencies. Ambient documentation has
demonstrated the ability to significantly improve clinician experience and patient
satisfaction, and has already been deployed widely for physicians.4 Yet nursing practice

7. Which roles, decision makers, or governing bodies within health care organizations
have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary
administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
The most influential decision-makers for AI adoption include practice councils,
security/risk/compliance committees, and AI governance bodies where they exist.
However, a primary administrative hurdle is inadequate staff education and limited
understanding among leadership of both the risks and the return on investment
associated with AI tools. Organizations often lack frameworks for systematically
evaluating AI tools or established processes for scaling successful pilots to enterprise-
wide deployment. Practice councils, which include frontline clinicians, are critical for
adoption success but often lack representation in early AI procurement and
development decisions. Nurses must be at the table during these discussions, not
merely informed after technology decisions have been made. The CMS Health
Technology Ecosystem initiative, which includes over 600 industry partners developing
AI conversational agents, highlights the urgent need for nursing representation in
technology governance. We encourage HHS to engage nurses meaningfully in oversight
groups for initiatives like the new CMS App Store for patient applications, ensuring that
the clinical perspective of continuous care providers shapes these important decisions.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research,
and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data
types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
Standardized data elements that flow seamlessly across care settings and EHR platforms
would enable AI tools to function effectively regardless of the underlying technology
infrastructure. One notable example is Epic's Cosmos platform, which is being leveraged
across Epic's customer base to enable large-scale data analysis and AI model
development. However, interoperability must extend beyond single-vendor ecosystems.
This is particularly critical for AI applications supporting care transitions and care
coordination, where patients frequently move between systems using different EHR
platforms. We note that HHS should consider the HTI-5 Proposed Rule carefully, on
which we also plan to offer comments, as it includes significant deregulatory actions
that may reduce interoperability. Finally, data standards that support nursing
documentation and capture the unique contributions of nursing care would be
particularly valuable for training AI models that support nursing workflows.

by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and

We encourage a review of the numerous studies that have explored patients’ and

identify insights or patterns that time-pressed medical providers may have missed
because they lack the capacity to dig deeply into literature or review comparable case
studies. This is underscored by recent research showing that 1 in 4 active users of
OpenAI’s ChatGPT prompt about medical questions weekly, and other research showing
that 60% of adults age 50 and older have used it for medical advice at least once.
10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the
adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
HHS should prioritize: (1) investments in biomedical informatics research, (2)
mechanisms for academic-industry partnerships, and (3) research and investments on
human factors consideration with AI implementations, especially ambient AI
applications and especially in nursing practice. First, HHS should fund research on AI
evaluation methodologies that can be systematically applied across different clinical
contexts to determine which tools genuinely improve care quality and reduce costs
versus those that simply add complexity. The literature currently lacks comprehensive
approaches to evaluating the costs, benefits, and tradeoffs of using AI in clinical care,
particularly from the nursing perspective. Research investments should also explore
how to scale successful AI implementations across different healthcare settings and how
to ensure AI tools perform equitably across diverse patient populations. Second, these
research priorities will likely require partnerships between companies developing
scalable AI tools and the healthcare delivery organizations containing the patient
populations and clinical expertise necessary to evaluate these tools in a rigorous,
generalizable manner. Therefore, partnerships between such organizations could
accelerate learning and dissemination of best practices, but require grant or contract
funding. Embedding nursing leadership in these collaborations is essential to ensure that
implementation strategies are clinically feasible, patient-centered, and aligned with
frontline care realities. Third, research is critically needed on human factors
considerations such as the cognitive burden and workflow impact of clinical AI, including
effects on clinician mental workload, attention, task switching, and documentation load,
and how interface design and escalation pathways can reduce cognitive overload and
automation bias. Moreover, we note that ambient documentation is rapidly being
adopted and integrated into care, but the evidence base lags behind. There is also
currently limited published evidence on how ambient AI tools specifically affect nursing
care delivery, despite the significant potential impact on the largest segment of the
clinical workforce. Research should examine current data about ambient AI and nursing,

In conclusion, we encourage HHS to adopt the framing that AI is a tool in service of care
delivery and transitions, not an end in itself. Through this patient-centered lens, it is clear that
nurses are not peripheral stakeholders in AI-enabled care but rather essential to its success. ANI
strongly advocates for HHS to recognize that successful AI adoption in clinical care requires
meaningful nursing engagement at every level, from development and testing through
implementation and evaluation. With thoughtful policy that centers patients’ and clinicians’
needs and ensures adequate education, oversight, and evaluation, AI has tremendous potential
to address the healthcare workforce crisis while improving outcomes for patients and
communities across America.
