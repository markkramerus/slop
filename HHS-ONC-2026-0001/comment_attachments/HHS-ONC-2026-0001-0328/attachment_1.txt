The United States overdose crisis represents one of the most significant preventable death
challenges in modern American history. In the past decade, more Americans have died from

that can inform Emergency Department protocols, harm reduction resource deployment, and

I. Regulation
A. Risk-Based Governance Clarity to Accelerate Responsible Adoption
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
Acoer’s direct experience deploying the Health Data Explorer and Forensic Intelligence Platform
in public health and clinical-adjacent environments identifies five foundational barriers:
• Regulatory ambiguity for non-medical device AI: Most AI deployed in public health and
prevention settings does not meet the definition of a medical device, yet governance
expectations are unclear. This creates risk aversion among CIOs, compliance officers, and
agency legal teams who cannot confidently deploy systems without defined accountability
frameworks.
• Unverifiable AI lineage: AI systems in clinical and public health settings currently cannot
demonstrate provenance of training data, consent status, model versioning history, or
post-deployment performance drift. This makes institutional trust impossible to engineer
and audit.
• Data sovereignty concerns in sensitive environments: Medical examiner offices,
behavioral health agencies, and county health departments are deeply cautious about any
AI architecture requiring centralized data pooling or external model training. The inability to
guarantee local data control is a primary adoption barrier in the public sector.
• Fragmented interoperability: Forensic, clinical, and public health systems operate in
separate data silos. AI-generated intelligence from mortality data cannot flow into
emergency department workflows or syndromic surveillance platforms without
standardized, trust-anchored data exchange.
• Misaligned procurement pathways: Innovation procurement pathways for public health
agencies and clinical-adjacent public sector organizations are far less developed than
those for hospital systems, creating structural disadvantages for responsible AI
deployment in the jurisdictions that need it most.
Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize?
HHS should prioritize the following specific regulatory changes:
1. Define a Minimum Verifiable AI Baseline Under 45 CFR Part 170
ASTP/ONC should amend 45 CFR Part 170 (Health IT Certification) to require AI systems used
in certified health IT to meet a minimum verifiable governance standard, including:
• Versioned model metadata with tamper-evident storage
• Dataset lineage and provenance disclosures tied to consent status
• Cryptographically signed audit logs for inference events

3. Support Privacy-Preserving AI Architecture Standards
HHS should formally recognize and incentivize AI architectures that:
• Operate within dedicated, jurisdiction-specific infrastructure without cross-jurisdiction data
pooling
• Maintain full local data sovereignty and explicit consent tracking
• Provide transparent, auditable extraction and inference logic
• Support third-party validation of model governance claims
Question 3: What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role should HHS play?
Three legal and accountability gaps require immediate HHS attention:
• Liability ambiguity for AI-assisted prevention decisions: When a clinical system or
public health agency acts on AI-generated pattern intelligence (e.g., an emerging drug
combination identified from mortality data) and that action prevents or fails to prevent
harm, existing frameworks under 42 CFR Part 482 do not clearly allocate responsibility
between the AI system, its operator, and the clinician or public health official who acted.
HHS should convene a multi-stakeholder working group to define accountability
frameworks for AI-assisted prevention decisions
• Cross-jurisdiction data sovereignty in multi-agency intelligence sharing: AI systems
such as Acoer’s Forensic Intelligence Platform that aggregate signals across medical
examiner jurisdictions, EDs, and public health departments require explicit frameworks for
who owns derived intelligence, who can act on it, and how consent flows across entities.
HHS should model these frameworks through TEFCA governance extensions.

B. Supporting Private Sector Innovation
Question 5: How can HHS best support private sector activities to promote innovative and effective
AI use in clinical care?
HHS should establish a tiered, risk-proportionate AI assurance framework that:
• Creates a voluntary certification pathway for health AI vendors demonstrating verifiable
governance practices
• Establishes public-private partnerships to develop open benchmarking datasets for
mortality intelligence, syndromic surveillance, and overdose pattern detection
• Funds cooperative agreements with health AI vendors operating in underserved public
health environments (county health departments, tribal health authorities, medical
examiner offices) to accelerate deployment of responsible private AI platforms in the
jurisdictions with the greatest preventable death burden
• Establishes safe harbor provisions for organizations that implement verifiable AI
governance standards, reducing liability uncertainty that currently deters adoption
Question 7: Which roles have the most influence on AI adoption and what are the primary
administrative hurdles?
In public health and clinical-adjacent settings, Acoer’s experience identifies the following as
primary adoption gatekeepers:
• Chief Medical Examiners and Forensic Directors: control access to the most valuable
outcome-validated health data in the system, but have minimal IT procurement support
• State and County Public Health Officers: primary decision-makers for prevention strategy,
but operate under constrained budgets and procurement processes designed for
traditional IT, not AI
• Healthcare System CIOs and CMIOs: strong influence but face compliance teams that
cannot approve systems without verifiable governance documentation
• State Medicaid Directors: increasingly relevant as prevention AI demonstrates
downstream cost avoidance
The primary administrative hurdles are: (1) absence of standard AI governance requirements
that give legal teams a basis for approval; (2) HIPAA ambiguity for AI inference on public health
data; (3) procurement vehicle constraints that favor established vendors over innovative small
companies; and (4) the requirement that AI tools demonstrate ROI in reimbursement terms that
do not yet capture prevention value.

II. Reimbursement
A. Aligning Payment Policy to Reward Prevention Outcomes
Question 2: What payment policy changes should HHS prioritize?
Under current fee-for-service frameworks governed by 42 CFR Parts 410 and 414, AI that
prevents overdose deaths, detects emerging drug threats, or reduces unnecessary Emergency
Department utilization generates no direct reimbursement signal. This is a fundamental
misalignment: the most valuable AI applications in public health (specifically those that prevent
costs and deaths before they occur) are precisely the ones that receive no payment reward.
Acoer recommends the following specific payment policy changes:
1. Fund Prevention AI Demonstration Pilots through CMMI
CMS should authorize CMMI to fund 3-5 year demonstration projects measuring the impact of
AI-enabled mortality intelligence and syndromic surveillance on downstream healthcare costs.
Eligible outcomes for reimbursement credits should include:
• Reductions in overdose-related Emergency Department visits
• Faster deployment of harm reduction resources (naloxone distribution, treatment referrals)
in response to AI-identified emerging substance threats
• Reduction in time-to-detection for emerging drug combinations (from months to days)
• Measurable reduction in overdose mortality in geographies where AI-enabled prevention
intelligence is deployed
The Economic Argument for Prevention AI
A single prevented opioid overdose death represents an estimated $1-2 million in avoided
lifetime productivity loss, healthcare costs, and downstream criminal justice expenditure. AI
systems that cost hundreds of thousands of dollars annually to operate can prevent
hundreds of deaths. The return on investment is not marginal. It is transformational. Existing
payment models simply do not capture this value.
2. Create AI Outcome Modifiers in Value-Based Care Arrangements
For Accountable Care Organizations, Medicare Advantage plans, and Medicaid managed care
entities, CMS should establish AI-specific outcome modifiers that provide financial credit for
documented AI-assisted prevention outcomes. Critically, all outcome claims must be auditable,
not self-attested. This is where Acoer’s cryptographic governance infrastructure provides unique
value: our systems generate tamper-evident proof of what AI actions were taken, what data was
used, and what outcomes were observed, generating the kind of independently verifiable
outcome attribution that outcome modifier programs require.
3. Include Forensic and Mortality Intelligence in Population Health Payment Models

Question 6: Where have AI tools met or exceeded expectations, and what novel tools have the
greatest potential?
Acoer’s direct deployment experience provides the following assessment:
Where AI Has Demonstrated Clear Value
• Natural language processing for unstructured forensic and clinical narratives: Acoer’s
Forensic Intelligence Platform demonstrates that private AI consistently outperforms
manual review in speed, consistency, and cross-case correlation, converting months of
work into hours without sacrificing accuracy.
• Cross-case pattern detection in overdose intelligence: AI systems that aggregate and
cross-reference individual case data identify emerging substance trends 4-8 weeks earlier
than traditional structured reporting systems.
• Administrative burden reduction: AI-driven pre-population of reporting fields, automated
coding assistance, and documentation support demonstrably reduce administrative time
for forensic and clinical staff.
Where AI Has Fallen Short or Faces Challenges
• Trust deficit in clinical adoption: AI tools deployed without verifiable governance
documentation face persistent resistance from clinical staff, compliance teams, and legal
counsel, regardless of technical performance.
• Integration complexity: AI tools that require deep EHR integration face months-to-years
implementation timelines that erode their value in rapidly evolving public health contexts.
• Lack of outcome feedback loops: Most deployed AI systems do not have mechanisms to
measure whether their outputs led to different decisions or better outcomes, making it
impossible to demonstrate or improve value over time.
Novel Tools with Greatest Potential to Reduce Costs and Save Lives
• AI-powered syndromic surveillance integration: Systems that aggregate signals from
mortality data, ED visits, EMS runs, and harm reduction encounters to provide a unified,
near-real-time early warning intelligence layer for emerging public health threats.
• Predictive overdose risk scoring at the individual patient level: AI models trained on
mortality intelligence ("what led to death in confirmed cases") that can identify high-risk
individuals in ED and primary care settings for targeted intervention.
• Substance combination early detection: AI systems that identify novel polysubstance
combinations in toxicology data before they are represented in ICD coding systems,
enabling faster public health response.

III. Research & Development
A. Recognizing Mortality Intelligence as Prevention Infrastructure
Question 10: What specific areas of AI research should HHS prioritize to accelerate AI adoption in
clinical care?
Mortality data is the most reliable, outcome-validated intelligence in the American health
system. Unlike clinical diagnostic data, mortality data reflects confirmed outcomes. Unlike
survey data, it captures what actually happened, not what was reported. Yet it is systematically
excluded from AI-enabled prevention strategy. HHS should treat structured mortality intelligence
as a foundational layer of national health AI infrastructure.
HHS R&D investment priorities should include:
• Natural language processing for forensic and clinical narratives: Investment in
private AI and NLP models specifically trained and validated on autopsy narratives,
toxicology reports, and death investigation documentation. These differ substantially from
clinical encounter notes and require domain-specific training, validation, and evaluation
frameworks. Acoer’s Forensic Intelligence Platform represents a deployable reference
implementation for this class of system.
• Cross-case pattern detection infrastructure: Development of federated AI architectures
that enable cross-jurisdiction pattern detection without requiring centralized data pooling,
thereby preserving data sovereignty while enabling population-level intelligence.
• Syndromic surveillance AI integration: Research and implementation science for
integrating AI-derived mortality intelligence into existing CDC BioSense Platform
syndromic surveillance workflows, enabling real-time early warning signals to inform public
health response.
• Predictive prevention AI for high-risk populations: Development and validation of AI
models that use mortality intelligence to identify and intervene with living individuals at
highest risk of overdose death, before the next acute event.
• Verifiable AI governance standards: Investment in standards development for
cryptographic AI audit trails, model versioning, and inference provenance documentation,
to enable interoperable, independently verifiable AI governance across health system
boundaries.
Regarding published evidence (Q10a): Acoer’s deployment experience is consistent with
emerging literature demonstrating that AI-assisted NLP of clinical and forensic narratives
achieves extraction accuracy comparable to expert human review at dramatically higher speed.
The gap between the evidence base and current deployment is not evidence quality. The real
constraint is governance framework maturity and interoperability infrastructure.
Regarding cost-benefit analysis (Q10b): Published literature consistently finds that AI systems in
high-volume, pattern-intensive health contexts (radiology, pathology, population surveillance)
generate positive ROI when total cost of care effects are captured over multi-year horizons. The
primary gap in current cost-benefit models is the failure to capture upstream prevention value:

C. Public-Private Partnerships and Demonstration Pilots
Question 4: What are the most promising AI evaluation methods and what mechanisms would be
most impactful?
Acoer recommends HHS fund demonstration pilots through cooperative agreements and
CRADAs with a dual evaluation framework:
Pre-Deployment Evaluation Standards
• Structured extraction accuracy testing validated against confirmed toxicological and
forensic findings (ground truth from completed death investigations)
• Expert domain validation by licensed forensic pathologists and toxicologists
• Reproducibility, consistency, and population-representativeness testing across
demographic and geographic subgroups
• Cryptographic baseline documentation of model version, training dataset, and validation
methodology before deployment
Post-Deployment Evaluation Framework
• Reduction in time-to-detection for emerging substance combinations (measured against
structured reporting lag)
• Accuracy of early warning signals validated against subsequent confirmed death data
• Clinical workflow integration effectiveness: did AI-derived intelligence actually change ED
protocols, harm reduction deployment, or prescribing patterns?
• Downstream outcome measurement: overdose ED visits, EMS naloxone deployments,
and mortality in jurisdictions with and without AI-enabled surveillance
The most impactful mechanisms for HHS investment would be: cooperative agreements with
state and county public health departments for deployment of AI-enabled mortality intelligence
platforms; CRADAs with technology vendors (like Acoer) to co-develop evaluation frameworks
and open benchmarking datasets; and prize competitions for demonstrated prevention
outcomes rather than technology features.

D. Leadership in Decentralized Science and Open Health Research
Acoer’s commitment to verifiable, transparent, community-grounded research extends beyond
commercial product deployment. Acoer CEO Jim Nasr serves in a leadership role on the
Coalition for Decentralized Healthcare and Life Sciences, an industry working group advancing
the principles of decentralized science (DeSci), an emerging movement that applies blockchain-
based governance, open data standards, and token-enabled coordination to scientific research,
peer review, and clinical validation.
DeSci is directly relevant to HHS’s AI strategy because it provides an operational model for the
open, verifiable, community-accountable research infrastructure that AI governance requires.
Key principles:
• Open provenance: Research data, methods, and findings are published with
cryptographically verifiable provenance, so any stakeholder (whether a regulator, patient,
or peer researcher) can independently verify what was done and what was found.
• Community-driven validation: Citizen science and open peer review platforms enable
distributed validation of research findings, broadening the evidence base beyond
traditional academic and industry gatekeepers.
• Data sovereignty: DeSci frameworks ensure research participants retain ownership of
their data and can verify how it is used in research protocols, a critical requirement for
sensitive health data.
• Transparent incentive alignment: Token-based coordination mechanisms can align
researcher, patient, and public health incentives around measurable health outcomes
rather than publication metrics or commercial returns.
DeSci in Action: Open Cancer Research on ResearchHub
Acoer is an active contributor to an open science project on ResearchHub
(researchhub.com/fund/4459) investigating the Wim Hof Method (WHM), a structured cold
exposure and breathwork practice, as a novel complementary approach for cancer patients.
This citizen-scientist pilot applies the principles of open, verifiable, community-funded
research to a clinical question that traditional research funding mechanisms have been slow
to address. It demonstrates that rigorous, outcome-driven health research can be conducted
transparently, with public accountability, at a fraction of the cost of conventional clinical
trials.
HHS Implications: DeSci as a Model for AI Research Governance
HHS should consider how DeSci principles can inform the governance of AI research in clinical
and public health settings:
• Open benchmarking datasets: HHS-funded AI benchmarking datasets for overdose
pattern detection, syndromic surveillance, and clinical decision support should be
published with DeSci-style open provenance, enabling independent validation and
continuous improvement by the research community.

Patient and Caregiver Perspectives
Question 9: What challenges do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by AI in clinical care,
and what concerns do they have?
Acoer’s work with families affected by overdose deaths provides a distinctive perspective on
patient and caregiver priorities:
What Patients and Caregivers Want AI to Deliver
• Prevention before death: Families who have lost loved ones to overdose consistently ask
why the warning signs were not detected earlier. They want AI systems that identify high-
risk individuals in clinical encounters and trigger earlier, more aggressive intervention.
• Transparency about what happened: Families navigating the aftermath of an overdose
death want clear, comprehensive information about what substance combinations were
involved, what medical interventions were attempted, and what systemic factors
contributed. AI that can answer these questions from existing data provides immense
value.
• Fewer administrative barriers to care: Patients with substance use disorders consistently
encounter administrative and insurance barriers to treatment access. AI that can automate
prior authorization, reduce treatment matching latency, and predict which interventions are
most likely to be effective for a given individual can meaningfully reduce these barriers.
• Privacy and control: Patients consistently express concern about AI systems that
aggregate their health data without their knowledge or consent, and about the use of
sensitive behavioral health information in employment, insurance, or law enforcement
contexts.
What Concerns Patients and Caregivers Have About AI
• Invisible automation replacing human judgment: Patients and families fear that AI-driven
clinical decisions will reduce the quality of human attention they receive from clinicians.

AI will not save lives until it is trusted, funded, and connected to the systems where lives are at
risk. The United States loses more than 70,000 people to drug overdoses every year. A
significant proportion of those deaths are preceded by signals, seen in clinical encounters, in
emergency runs, in the unstructured text of toxicology reports, that AI can detect, correlate, and
act on, if the governance, interoperability, and incentive infrastructure exists to support it.
Acoer stands at the intersection of this challenge and this opportunity. Our Health Data Explorer
and Forensic Intelligence Platform are deployed today in medical examiner offices and public
health agencies, converting unstructured mortality data into actionable prevention intelligence
using private AI that keeps sensitive data under local jurisdiction control. We use cryptographic
technologies to make every AI action verifiable and every data governance decision auditable.
We are aligned with FHIR and HTI-5 interoperability standards. We have built the privacy-
preserving architecture that sensitive public health environments require.
What we need from HHS is the governance clarity, the interoperability infrastructure, and the
reimbursement alignment to scale what works. The technology exists. The evidence is
accumulating. The political will to Make America Healthy Again is present. The moment to act is
now.
We recommend HHS prioritize three immediate actions:
• Establish a verifiable AI baseline: Amend 45 CFR Part 170 to require cryptographically
verifiable AI governance documentation as a condition of health IT certification. Make trust
in AI systems a requirement, not an aspiration.
• Fund prevention AI demonstration pilots: Use CMMI authority and cooperative
agreement mechanisms to fund 3-5 year demonstrations of private AI-enabled mortality
intelligence and syndromic surveillance, with clear outcome metrics and attribution
standards, prioritizing architectures that preserve data sovereignty.
• Extend interoperability to the full prevention data ecosystem: Bring ME/coroner
systems into TEFCA, fund FHIR extensions for mortality and AI metadata, and create APIs
that allow AI-derived prevention intelligence to flow into national syndromic surveillance
infrastructure.
Acoer welcomes the opportunity to demonstrate our deployed systems, share our evaluation
data, and partner with HHS in designing the governance frameworks and pilot programs that will
accelerate responsible AI adoption across clinical and public health settings. Every day that AI-
enabled prevention intelligence remains siloed, fragmented, or insufficiently trusted to act on is
a day when preventable deaths continue.
We appreciate the opportunity to contribute to this policy dialogue and stand ready to support
HHS in turning this RFI’s insights into action.
