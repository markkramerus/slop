● Barriers to Private Sector Innovation: Existing ambiguity in FDA guidance on AI in
healthcare presents an easily fixable barrier. Specifically, to remove that barrier to healthcare
AI innovation, the FDA should refine its Clinical Decision Support (CDS) guidance to
ensure that “independent review” requirements do not mandate citations for medically
self-evident diagnoses. By narrowing this construction, regulators can prevent administrative
bloat and allow AI tools to prioritize high-value insights without being reclassified as
regulated medical devices for performing basic data-to-diagnosis synthesis.
● Enhancing Accountability and Data Rights: To bridge the gap between AI
accountability and data rights, HHS should clarify that “derived data”—the system-generated
metadata and latent intelligence produced during model operation—remains the proprietary
property of the developer. This distinction ensures that while patient inputs remain strictly
protected, developers retain the essential diagnostic logs and performance metrics required
to fulfill statutory safety monitoring and anti-bias obligations under emerging frameworks
like the Colorado AI Act.

III. Use of AI to Improve Health Outcomes
Our AI platform is a non-medical device clinical decision support tool that analyzes a vast amount
of data from over 100 different sources—including claims, labs, pharmacy, and electronic health
records—to create a holistic view of each patient. Instead of a generic alert for a group of patients,
AI provides personalized, evidence-based recommendations tailored to the specific person in front
of the doctor. This approach enables our doctors to:
A. Identify and treat diseases earlier.
● Diabetes: Compared to those who did not have Clover Assistant visits with their
primary-care providers, enrollees who had Clover Assistant-assisted provider visits: (1)
primary-care practitioner use of Clover Assistant is correlated with earlier diagnosis (average
HbA1c 6.65% for members having a Clover Assistant visit with a PCP and 6.91% for
members not having such a visit); (2) were treated for diabetes an average of 36 months
earlier; (3) were much more likely to obtain earlier treatment with oral diabetes medications
and delay use of insulin at later stages of type 2 diabetes; and (4) had lower documented
rates of hypoglycemia, a severe complication of diabetes, at 1,000 days (1.5% versus 2.4%).2
● Chronic Kidney Disease: Primary-care physicians who used Clover Assistant were able to
diagnose–earlier–CKD stage 3 and higher. Earlier diagnosis of CKD resulting from
CA-assisted visits slowed the decline of kidney function significantly (GFR, or glomerular
filtration rate, of about 4.6 mL/min per year versus 1.3 mL/min per year), helping to delay
and prevent adverse and costly health outcomes from reduced kidney function, including
high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, dialysis dependence, and death.3

IV. Barriers to Private Sector Innovation
The FDA’s 2026 Clinical Decision Support (CDS) Software Guidance updates the 2022 guidance
and clarifies the types of CDS functions that are excluded from the definition of a device. The FDA
relies on four criteria for CDS to be excluded from the definition of a device:7
Criterion 1: The software is not intended to acquire, process, or analyze a medical image or a
signal from an in vitro diagnostic device or a pattern or signal from a signal acquisition
system.
Criterion 2: The software is intended for the purpose of displaying, analyzing, or printing
medical information about a patient or other medical information.
Criterion 3: The software is intended for the purpose of supporting or providing
recommendations to a provider about prevention, diagnosis, or treatment of a disease or
condition.
Criterion 4: The software is intended for the purpose of enabling the provider to
independently review the basis for the recommendations that such software presents so that
it is not the intent that the provider rely primarily on any of such recommendations to make
a clinical diagnosis or treatment decision regarding an individual patient.
The 2026 guidance expanded the 4th criterion for exclusion to state that the information should be
presented in a way that prioritizes decision-relevant details and avoids information overload. The
FDA also emphasized that recommendations must be grounded in “well-understood and accepted
sources,” such as clinical guidelines and peer-reviewed literature. This criterion should be narrowly
construed in order to preserve the clinical accuracy of the provider. For example, if an individual has
diabetes and their A1C indicates they have diabetes, Clover’s AI tool should not have to link to
clinical guidelines to confirm that the individual has diabetes. We urge the FDA to clarify that the
use of clinical guidelines to confirm simple diagnoses should not be construed as an FDA regulated
clinical decision support tool but an AI tool that is pulling an individual's data that the physician has
the ability to confirm or reject the diagnosis.

V. Enhancing Accountability and Data Rights
A critical legal issue surrounding the use of AI in healthcare is the assignment of ownership rights
over secondary data. Derived data is a new creation resulting from the developer’s proprietary logic,
weights, and processing power. HHS should clarify that derived data created by an AI tool remains
the property of the developer. This is distinct from the customer’s underlying patient data (inputs),
which remains subject to existing privacy and ownership protections.
Traditional healthcare contracts and Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) were designed for static
software. They typically mandate the return or destruction of data upon contract termination. But
continuous-learning AI requires access to secondary data—such as model confidence scores, latent
representations, and processing logs—to improve accuracy, reliability, and safety over time. If
developers are forced to “wipe” this intelligence per-deployment, the entire ecosystem suffers from
stagnating model performance and a failure to learn from past errors.
There is a fundamental principle at stake: ownership of derived data must follow the responsibility
for the model’s performance. Under emerging frameworks like the Colorado AI Act, developers face
heightened scrutiny and potential liability for “algorithmic discrimination” or harmful
“hallucinations.” It is a functional impossibility for a developer to fulfill these legal obligations if they
cannot retain the data necessary to:
● Conduct Post-Market Surveillance: Identify where a model may be drifting or
failing in real-world settings.
● Audit for Bias: Aggregate processing data across diverse deployments to detect and
mitigate disparate impacts.
● Validate Mitigations: Document that a known error has been successfully
corrected through model tuning.
HHS should provide clear guidance that while underlying PHI and customer-specific inputs remain
protected and owned by the covered entity, the derived analytical outputs and system-generated
metadata belong to the developer for the purposes of monitoring, auditing, and safety-enhancement.
Without this clarity, the “Duty to Monitor” imposed by regulators will remain in conflict with
traditional healthcare contracts that do not give developers the ability to access the underlying data
necessary to fulfill such duties.

VI. Conclusion
We support HHS’s vision of adoption and use of AI in clinical care in a way that improves health
outcomes, reduces barriers to innovation, and enhances accountability and clarifies data rights so
that AI can be deployed most effectively.
To accelerate the adoption of AI in clinical care, HHS should prioritize these three critical areas
based on Clover Health's experience:
● Use AI within clinical care settings to identify and treat disease earlier, personalize care for
patients, improve prevention and reduce acute care utilization.
● Reduce barriers to private sector innovation, such as narrowly tailoring the applicability of
the FDA’s clinical decision support guidance.
● Focus on enhancing accountability and data rights by defining derived data to address
liability and AI safety concerns.
Clover appreciates HHS’ engagement on greater adoption of AI in clinical care and shares the
agency’s commitment to use AI effectively and in a way that fosters public trust. We look forward to
continued collaboration and welcome further dialogue as HHS evaluates the use of AI in clinical
care.
