We have prepared the following comments in response to your recent RFI:
Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical
Care.
Question 1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health
care and its adoption and use in clinical care?
Response. Private‑sector innovation in clinical AI is shaped less by algorithmic
performance than by entrenched sociotechnical, infrastructural, and market‑structure
barriers. Across health systems, poor workflow fit, concentrated control of integration
pathways, variable technical capacity, and ambiguity in regulatory expectations
collectively slow development, prolong procurement, and reduce the viability of new
entrants in the clinical AI ecosystem.
Sociotechnical and Workflow Barriers. Sociotechnical constraints consistently
emerge as the most significant impediments to AI innovation and adoption.
Inadequate interprofessional co‑design, particularly insufficient integration of
nurses, who make up the largest segment of the healthcare workforce, combined
with weak alignment to real‑world workflows increases cognitive burden and
undermines trust, a pattern documented repeatedly across qualitative and
scoping reviews.1,2 Health‑system survey findings similarly show that
administrative AI tools, such as ambient clinical documentation, achieve faster
and more successful uptake than diagnostic decision‑support tools because they
align more naturally with existing documentation workflows and entail lower
perceived clinical risk.3
Market Concentration and Vendor Dependence. Market structure further
constrains innovation. Two electronic health record (EHR) vendors dominate the
acute‑care EHR market, jointly holding approximately two‑thirds of national

frameworks. In many organizations, these reviews extend contracting timelines
from several months to nearly a year, particularly for first‑in‑class or
enterprise‑scale AI deployments.1,3,9

Response. HHS should prioritize regulatory, payment, and programmatic reforms that
support a total product lifecycle (TPLC) approach to clinical AI, strengthen transparency
and governance, and align reimbursement with demonstrated clinical and operational
value. These efforts should address gaps in post‑deployment monitoring, value‑based
payment structures, organizational oversight, and supply‑chain transparency, all of
which are necessary to ensure that AI tools function safely, reliably, and equitably under
real‑world clinical conditions.
Regulatory Priorities. A TPLC posture is essential for both device and non‑device
AI tools. Consistent with emerging FDA direction for AI‑enabled technologies,
one‑time pre‑deployment accuracy assessments are insufficient; instead,
regulators should require ongoing monitoring to understand how models perform
and evolve in dynamic clinical environments.10,11 Minimum expectations should
include routine performance surveillance, documentation of model versioning and
provenance, including hardware and firmware dependencies, transparent change
logs, and formalized drift‑detection processes with documented mitigation
pathways.
Payment Policy Priorities and Rewarding Verified Value. HHS should modernize
payment policy to incentivize measurable improvements rather than mere
technology acquisition. In alignment with the RFI’s critique of fee‑for‑service
spending bubbles, CMS should pilot value‑based AI add‑ons within existing
payment frameworks to reimburse tools that demonstrably reduce clinician
burden, expand access, or improve outcomes.12 Such models would help shift
adoption incentives away from procurement volume and toward verifiable
improvements in efficiency, care quality, and patient experience.
Alignment with Federal Standards. To strengthen governance consistency across
the health‑care ecosystem, HHS should encourage practices analogous to the
Office of Management and Budget’s M‑24‑10 guidance, including AI inventories,
named governance leadership, and minimum risk‑management standards
tailored for clinical use cases involving protected health information.13 These
expectations would help health systems adopt more structured, transparent, and

Question 3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may
raise novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and
accountability structures ( e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security).
What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS
play to help address them?
Response. Non-device AI challenges legacy accountability structures because opaque,
adaptive models make it difficult to allocate liability among developers, hospitals, and
clinicians, and because ‘black-box’ architectures undermine explainability and
reproducibility of decision-making; in addition, when such tools influence care decisions
outside clearly defined regulatory indications, they raise practical concerns about
off-label use and exposure to unresolved medico-legal risk.15 Privacy questions also
emerge when models infer sensitive attributes or generate secondary data that are not
cleanly addressed by existing frameworks. HHS should build on emerging
responsible‑AI guidance by issuing model contract provisions for clinical AI, covering
performance and drift‑monitoring obligations, bias‑mitigation duties, and notification and
update service‑level commitments, clarifying expectations for human oversight of
non‑device AI in clinical workflows, and exploring safe‑harbor‑style protections for
organizations that implement specified governance and monitoring standards. These
steps would align with broader federal AI principles emphasizing transparency, human
oversight, and risk‑based governance, while directly addressing the contractual and
liability uncertainties that currently fuel institutional risk aversion. Clear expectations for
human‑in‑the‑loop oversight are needed to ensure that adaptive or generative AI
systems remain under meaningful clinical supervision, a safeguard emphasized across
recent federal AI strategy documents and FDA’s supervised agentic AI implementations.

Question 4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation
methods (pre- and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow
and human-centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support
these processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most impactful ( e.g., contracts,
grants, cooperative agreements, and/or prize competitions)?
Response. Robust evaluation must extend beyond accuracy to encompass workflow fit,
human‑AI teaming, and safety. Pre‑deployment, health systems should use
shadow‑mode comparisons, usability and workload testing, and bias assessment across
subgroups to detect risks before tools influence care. Evaluation frameworks should
also assess the integrity of human‑in‑the‑loop workflows, including how clinicians verify,
override, and contextualize AI‑generated recommendations, and how these oversight
points affect safety, workflow reliability, and cognitive load under real‑world conditions.
These human‑factors assessments should explicitly evaluate cognitive load impacts and
identify temporal clustering effects (e.g., whether shift‑change patterns or simultaneous
user activity degrade model responsiveness or reliability).2 Post‑deployment,
organizations need formal drift‑monitoring frameworks that continuously track input
data‑distribution shifts, model performance, and key human‑AI interaction signals, such
as clinician overrides, building on approaches already piloted for radiology and other
imaging AI tools.9,16 Federal advisory discussions similarly emphasize evaluating
generative AI for sociotechnical impact (workflow disruption, accountability, safety), not
accuracy alone.17 HHS can accelerate rigor by funding multi-site evaluation testbeds,
issuing human-factors guidance, and deploying cooperative agreements or prize
competitions to mature standardized benchmarking.

Question 7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care
organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are
the primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
Response. Artificial intelligence adoption in clinical care is shaped not only by
technological performance but also by governance structures, workforce readiness, and
implementation strategy. Importantly, responsible AI use does not end at initial
implementation and must be integrated into the daily, ongoing operational work of
healthcare organizations.
Key Point 1-AI Adoption Requires Structured Governance and Oversight
Adoption of AI tools within healthcare organizations depends on clearly defined
governance pathways that involve executive leadership, clinical leadership,
informatics teams, compliance officers, and frontline stakeholders. The National
Academy of Medicine emphasizes that safe and effective integration of AI
requires institutional oversight mechanisms, defined accountability structures,
and transparency in system design and deployment.19 Similarly, the World Health
Organization underscores the importance of regulatory clarity, lifecycle
monitoring, and institutional governance in its guidance on artificial intelligence
for health.20 Without structured oversight, organizations may hesitate to adopt AI
due to uncertainty regarding validation standards, risk management, and
responsibility. As a best practice, federal guidance could encourage
patient-inclusive governance structures, clear accountability for AI oversight

Key Point 2- Workforce Readiness and Competency Development are Critical
A significant administrative hurdle to AI adoption is the preparedness of
healthcare professionals to evaluate, interpret, and appropriately integrate
AI-supported outputs into clinical reasoning and documentation. The National
Academy of Medicine highlights workforce training and competency development
as essential components of responsible AI integration.20 Workforce readiness
directly shapes documentation transparency, communication with patients, and
decision-making confidence. Without structured education and clear expectations
for clinician oversight, organizations may experience hesitancy, inconsistent use,
or inappropriate reliance on AI-supported tools. Investment in competency
frameworks and practical training is therefore central to accelerating safe and
sustainable adoption. This need for workforce preparation is ongoing and
“evergreen,” particularly given routine staff turnover, the growth of contingent
workforce models, and the critical role of nonclinical staff who frequently serve as
primary interfaces with patients in both hospital and outpatient settings.
Key Point 3 - Implementation Challenges Extend Beyond Technical Performance
AI adoption barriers frequently arise not from algorithm performance alone but
from workflow disruption, ambiguity regarding liability, change-management
demands, and integration into existing documentation and quality oversight
processes. Implementation efforts must address how AI tools fit within clinical
workflows, how responsibility is allocated when AI informs decisions, and how AI
involvement is reflected in documentation without compromising clarity or
compliance. Regulatory developments such as the ONC’s Health Data,
Technology, and Interoperability (HTI) rulemaking, including HTI-1 provisions on
algorithm-transparency requirements for certified health IT, highlight the growing
importance of documentation disclosure and explainability in operational
settings.21 Addressing these nontechnical and operational factors is essential to
translating AI innovation into sustainable clinical practice.
Clear federal guidance that supports multidisciplinary governance, workforce
competency development, and transparent documentation standards may help reduce
institutional uncertainty and accelerate the responsible adoption of AI in clinical care.

Question 9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see
addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do
patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
Response. Patients and caregivers generally appreciate the potential of artificial
intelligence in clinical care to improve understanding, access, and coordination of
information, but meaningful adoption hinges on addressing concerns regarding trust,
transparency, explainability, and accountability.
Key Point 1- Improve Understanding and Navigation
Patients and caregivers frequently encounter challenges in interpreting clinical
documentation, coordinating information across care teams, and obtaining clear
explanations of their conditions and treatments. AI-enabled tools that summarize
clinical content, enhance clarity, and support navigation through health records
hold promise for addressing these challenges. Research indicates that a majority
of surveyed hospital patients, across multiple countries, expressed generally
positive overall attitudes toward AI use in health care, suggesting receptivity to
technologies that improve comprehension and accessibility when they are
integrated thoughtfully into care processes.22
Key Point 2- Concerns Related to Trust, Accountability, and Equity
Despite general openness to AI’s potential, patients express caution about
specific aspects of AI use. The study by Busch and colleagues (2025) found that
patients preferred AI systems that are explainable and that clinical
decision-making remains under physician oversight rather than autonomous AI
control. Attitudes varied by demographic, health status, and technological
literacy, with notable skepticism about AI’s accuracy in some diagnostic
information. These findings highlight that trust in AI is variable and is strongly
influenced by transparency, clinician involvement, and appropriate tailoring of AI
implementation.
Key Point 3- Transparency and Interpretability Must Guide Deployment
As patients increasingly interact with electronic health records and clinical
information, transparency and interpretability become essential for engagement
and trust. Evidence shows that patients are not uniformly confident in all aspects
of AI use, underscoring the need for clear communication about how AI is
applied, what it contributes, and how human clinicians remain responsible for

Question 10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to
accelerate the adoption of AI as part of clinical care? a. Are there published findings
about the impact of adopted AI tools and their use in clinical care? b. How does the
literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI as part of clinical care?
Response. HHS should prioritize research that strengthens the real‑world performance,
safety, and interoperability of clinical AI systems across diverse care environments.
Evidence from U.S. health‑system surveys shows that AI tools succeed when they
reduce documentation burden and integrate smoothly into workflows, whereas
higher‑risk diagnostic tools face more limited uptake, suggesting that research on
implementation science, human-AI teaming, and workflow fit remains foundational for
improving adoption. A second priority concerns rigorous evaluation frameworks that
capture long‑term clinical, organizational, and economic impact. The AI for IMPACTS
systematic review highlights the need for multidimensional evaluation, encompassing
integration, governance, safety, cost, and scalability, which expands beyond accuracy
metrics and aligns closely with a total‑product‑lifecycle approach increasingly
emphasized in federal policy.2,23 Third, as large language models and emerging
multi‑modal AI systems become embedded in documentation, summarization, and
decision‑support tasks, research should assess their reliability, error modes,
human‑oversight requirements, and performance across subpopulations and care
settings. Research is also needed to define optimal human‑in‑the‑loop models for large
language models, agentic systems, and multimodal AI, determining when human review
is required, how oversight affects safety and equity, and how to balance automation
