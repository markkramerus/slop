Clinical AI through Payment Reform

Response to HHS Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial
Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care (RIN 0955-AA13; 90 FR 60108)
Executive Summary
Patient-facing clinical AI represents a rare opportunity to introduce new technology that has
potential to reduce healthcare spending while improving access. The primary barrier to scaling
these gains system-wide is ambiguity in payment pathways (Q1, Q7). Today, no billing codes
exist for AI-enabled clinical services, asynchronous care remains largely unreimbursable, and
each payer relationship requires bespoke negotiation that cannot scale.
This response reflects the operational experience of leaders at five companies delivering AI-
enabled clinical care across pediatrics, HIV/STI care, primary care, and chronic disease
management: Summer Health, Healthvana, Counsel Health, August AI, and Doctronic.1 We
represent an emerging market category – patient-facing clinical AI – and draw on direct
operational, real-world experience to address the RFI's questions on barriers to adoption (Q1),
payment policy changes (Q2), where AI tools have met or exceeded expectations (Q6), and
administrative hurdles to adoption (Q7).
We recommend a phased approach (Q1, Q2, Q7):2
● Near-term: Modernize fee-for-service by recognizing asynchronous message-based
care as a billable modality nationwide (42 CFR § 410.78), creating an AI-augmented
modifier for existing E/M codes (42 CFR § 414.22), and expanding APCM codes to
cover AI-enabled care (G0556-G0558; 89 FR 97710)
● Medium-term: Establish billing codes for AI-autonomous clinical services (42 CFR §
410.32) and pilot PMPM models through CMMI
● Long-term: Layer outcomes-based payment as the claims data and clinical evidence
generated by earlier phases mature
These recommendations are intended to be net-deflationary, where each new payment pathway
reimburses AI-enabled care at rates below traditional equivalents, capturing cost savings while

Patient-Facing Clinical AI Delivers Clinical, Access, Operational, and
Cost Outcomes
AI is performing real clinical work: diagnosis, treatment guidance, chronic disease management,
and more. From our combined operations across pediatrics, HIV/STI care, primary care, and
chronic disease management, we’ve achieved the following outcomes (Q6):
Cost:
● $10/visit target at scale, down from current models (Summer Health).
● $29 visits, already below typical insurance video visit rates (Counsel Health).
● $39 cash visits with AI-generated documentation matching chief resident quality
(Doctronic).
Improved access:
● 13,000 previously unanswered patient messages are now resulting in clinical guidance
(Healthvana).

minutes (Counsel Health), with 4-5x the patient capacity of traditional video telemedicine
without sacrificing response quality (Counsel Health).

Current Payment Models Do Not Recognize AI-Enabled Care
The payment system that HHS aims to reform was built on a foundational assumption: a
clinician delivers a service to a patient in a discrete encounter. Payment was designed for brick-
and-mortar health systems that provided episodic care in an era when acute care was more
important and prevalent than chronic care management.
As care needs and patient preferences have evolved, payment mechanisms for patient-facing
clinical AI have not kept up. AI-enabled care breaks every element of that assumption: the
clinician may be supervising rather than personally delivering care, the encounter may be
asynchronous or continuous rather than discrete, and the value may show up months later in
avoided ER visits rather than in a single billable moment. While a handful of procedure-specific
CPT codes exist for narrowly scoped AI diagnostics (e.g., CPT 92229 for diabetic retinopathy
screening), there is no scalable national coding pathway for patient-facing AI that delivers triage,
treatment guidance, or ongoing care management.
Today, patient-facing clinical AI companies survive through a patchwork: cash-pay visits
(Summer Health, Counsel Health, Doctronic), employer contracts (Counsel Health), 340B drug
pricing savings (Healthvana), or direct-to-consumer subscription (Summer Health, Counsel
Health, Doctronic, August AI). Autonomous AI care (live in Utah through Doctronic) has no
payment pathway; only the human visit component is billable.
The specific barriers we face (Q1, Q7):
● Asynchronous care is not recognized as a billable modality. CMS and most state
Medicaid programs do not classify fully asynchronous text-based encounters as
reimbursable, despite widespread adoption of message-based care by providers
managing growing patient panels. Summer Health is the only company among us to
have secured payer reimbursement for asynchronous care, a process that took three
years of negotiation.
● No billing codes exist for AI-enabled clinical services. There is no CPT modifier or
service code for AI-assisted or AI-delivered care. When AI is deployed within health
systems today, it generates no direct reimbursement. Any value captured flows through
staffing efficiency or cross-subsidized programs, not through the payment system itself.
● Current contracting models require bespoke negotiation that cannot scale. PMPM
or case-rate arrangements must be negotiated payer by payer, plan by plan, which
creates uncertainty on both sides of the transaction: AI companies cannot reliably

forecast revenue, and health system buyers cannot justify procurement without a billing
code to point to.
As a result, innovation gets bottlenecked into direct-to-consumer approaches instead of scaling
through the healthcare system (Q1). Without clear reimbursement pathways, adoption will
remain confined to cash-pay patients and bespoke payer arrangements, limiting the market to
companies that can survive these conditions rather than those delivering the best care.
Clear payment pathways would transform AI-enabled care from a niche product into a
deflationary force for the whole system, lowering costs and broadening access beyond the
limited markets where this technology is already proving its value.
HHS Can Design Payment Policy That Improves Care and Lowers the
Total Cost of Care
HHS is right to be concerned that covering innovations rarely reduces net spending. The history
of healthcare payment is full of spending bubbles that followed new billing codes. To realize the
deflationary potential of AI-enabled care, payment systems must be designed to capture those
gains rather than repeat past patterns.
This requires learning from prior payment experience in both directions. Underpayment kills
adoption: one cost analysis of the Medicare Diabetes Prevention Program found that delivering
the program cost $553 per participant while Medicare only reimbursed $108, and provider
participation remained limited nationwide.5 A similar dynamic played out with IDx-DR, the first
autonomous AI diagnostic to receive a dedicated CPT code (92229) for diabetic retinopathy
screening. Despite FDA clearance and strong clinical evidence, adoption was slower than
expected in part because billing complexity and workflow integration costs outweighed available
reimbursement.6 Conversely, loosely defined codes invite overuse: the rapid proliferation of
remote patient monitoring billing, where broad code definitions led to widespread abusive billing
and subsequent enforcement action, illustrates the opposite failure mode.7
Our recommendation for episode-based bundling is designed to avoid both extremes. Across all
of these mechanisms, payment design should focus on per-episode reimbursement rather than
per-interaction billing, ensuring that cost savings accrue to the system rather than being eroded
by volume inflation. A unified national framework also would prevent the state-by-state

Though we recognize HHS's interest in outcomes-based payment, value-based care is the
wrong starting point for a nascent technology category, since it requires knowing how a
technology creates value, measuring it reliably, and attributing outcomes – none of which are
mature for clinical AI today. Starting with VBC would slow adoption and concentrate gains
among sophisticated players who can afford actuarial modeling and complex billing
infrastructure, rather than rewarding those who deliver the best care.
Fee-for-service provides the predictable revenue environment that health systems and AI
companies need to de-risk investment. It also creates the billing infrastructure and data capture
necessary to eventually measure outcomes at scale. Without a foundation of reimbursable AI
encounters, there is no data substrate for outcomes-based payment to build on. Given this, we
propose the following phased approach (Q2).
Near-term: Modernize fee-for-service for AI-enabled care (Q2)
Recognize asynchronous care as a billable modality.
We recommend that CMS amend 42 CFR § 410.78 to recognize asynchronous message-based
clinical encounters as reimbursable nationwide. Current regulations limit store-and-forward
technologies to Alaska and Hawaii demonstration programs (42 CFR § 410.78(d)), despite
widespread clinical adoption of text-based care. This limitation is particularly consequential for
rural communities, where text-based asynchronous care can be delivered over cellular networks
without requiring reliable broadband, specialist proximity, or scheduling around limited clinic
hours. The geographic-access rationale that justified the Alaska and Hawaii demonstrations
applies nationwide.
To prevent inflationary billing, we recommend episode-based bundling rather than per-message
reimbursement. All messages and AI interactions related to the same clinical issue would be
bundled into a single billable episode over a fixed time window (e.g., 7 days), consistent with
existing asynchronous care codes. This structure allows patients to return repeatedly over
several days (AI-enabled care is always on), while providers bill once per episode rather than
per interaction. This avoids the obvious abuse case where marginal AI touches generate
repeated charges.
Research on telehealth utilization supports this design: unbundled per-interaction billing has
been shown to increase spending without corresponding improvements in outcomes, while

episode-based structures contain costs by aligning reimbursement with clinical resolution rather

We further recommend removing the requirement that an initial visit be conducted via video or
in-person before asynchronous follow-up is billable. This requirement was designed to establish
a patient-provider relationship before asynchronous follow-up, but it blocks AI-native care
models that begin with text-based triage. We propose replacing it with direct safeguards that
address the same concern more effectively. Each billable async episode must include a defined
clinical question, a documented clinical assessment, and a treatment recommendation or an
escalation decision.
Create an AI-augmented care modifier for existing E/M codes.
We recommend CMS establish an AI-augmented modifier applicable to existing evaluation and
management codes, similar to the -93 modifier for audio-only services (42 CFR § 414.22). This
modifier would enable reimbursement at a reduced rate (e.g., 80% of the standard rate),
reflecting the lower cost of delivering AI-assisted care. Eligibility should require documentation
of AI-assisted components, clinical data capture, defined escalation protocols, and clinician
attestation of review.
Current E/M codes carry time requirements. The 99213 CPT code, for example, assumes 15-20
minutes of clinician involvement. When AI handles intake, history-taking, and documentation,
the physician's role shrinks to review and decision-making, often 2-3 minutes. Under current
rules, this shorter involvement means the visit doesn't meet time thresholds for billing. We
recommend that the AI-augmented modifier be tied to the service delivered rather than to the
clinician's minutes spent. This avoids both extremes: underpayment for genuinely efficient AI-
assisted care, and abusive billing for marginal AI involvement (two seconds of AI interaction
should not generate a $200 charge).
When rates are set correctly, payer incentives align naturally. If AI-augmented visits are
reimbursed at a fraction of standard E/M rates and AI-assisted workflows enable a physician to
see more patients, total cost per treated patient declines even as encounter volume increases.
Expanding Advanced Primary Care Management codes to cover AI
CMS's introduction of Advanced Primary Care Management (APCM) codes (G0556-G0558; 89
FR 97710) represents meaningful progress toward the kind of payment infrastructure AI-
enabled care requires. Several of its 13 service elements (i.e., 24/7 access to care, enhanced
communication opportunities, population data analysis) describe capabilities that AI-enabled
platforms already deliver as core functionality. CMS's decision to replace time-based thresholds
with risk-tiered reimbursement was the right design choice and should be preserved. We
propose two specific modifications that would make APCM viable for AI-enabled care models:

First, the initiating visit requirement allows telehealth but requires a synchronous encounter for
new patients or those not seen by the practice in three years. AI-native platforms onboard
patients through asynchronous text-based intake, triage, and clinical assessment, which do not
qualify under current rules. CMS should allow documented asynchronous onboarding for APCM
and predicates such as TPM, as we recommend for asynchronous care, to satisfy this
requirement. TCM's mandatory face-to-face visit within 7-14 days (CPT 99495, 99496) should
accommodate asynchronous follow-up.
Second, APCM's attribution model requires a single named clinician to serve as the patient's
comprehensive primary care provider. AI-enabled platforms may involve rotating clinical
oversight or team-based supervision rather than a single named practitioner, and often deliver
condition-specific management (HIV prevention, pediatric triage, chronic disease support) rather
than serving as the patient's full medical home. CMS should expand eligibility to include
condition-specific care management and clarify that platforms with defined clinical governance
and escalation protocols can qualify as billing entities.
Medium-term: Create pathways for AI-delivered care (Q2)
Establish billing codes for AI-autonomous clinical services.
For encounters in which AI delivers clinical services directly (diagnosis, triage, treatment
guidance) with defined escalation protocols to human clinicians, we recommend that CMS
establish a new service category (42 CFR § 410.32 or a new section). This would provide a
payment pathway for fully autonomous AI care, which currently has no billing mechanism. For
example, Doctronic operates autonomous AI care in Utah today; only the human visit
component is reimbursable.
We recognize that autonomous AI care warrants a risk-tiered approach. Initial billing codes
should target lower-acuity, protocol-driven scenarios where diagnostic and treatment pathways
are well established, such as routine UTI management, standard lab interpretation, and
medication refill assessments. Higher-complexity presentations would require mandatory
escalation protocols to human clinicians. As evidence of safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness
accumulates, the scope of reimbursable autonomous encounters can expand accordingly.
There should be room in the market for reimbursement across the full spectrum from AI-
augmented care with clinical oversight to AI-delivered care with human escalation pathways.
Both models exist today; neither has a clear payment pathway.
Support CMMI pilots for subscription and PMPM models.
The always-on nature of AI-enabled care lends itself to subscription or per-member-per-month
models rather than visit-based billing. A patient may message an AI platform daily for chronic
disease support, which does not fit discrete encounter logic. Forcing it into per-visit billing

creates perverse incentives. We recommend CMMI pilot a simple PMPM model for AI-enabled
primary care with straightforward attribution, creating an alternative pathway that matches how
these tools actually deliver value.
Long-term: Layer outcomes-based payment as evidence matures (Q2)
Attribution problems (i.e., isolating AI's contribution from human care interventions) are real, and
current measurement infrastructure cannot support them. Once near- and medium-term fee-for-
service codes generate sufficient claims data, outcomes-based models become viable. The
earlier phases build the data substrate that value-based care requires.
Accelerating adoption beyond payment reform (Q2, Q4, Q5)
State-level innovation is already underway (Q5). Utah's AI Learning Laboratory Program (in
which Doctronic is participating) has enabled autonomous AI care delivery with appropriate
oversight requirements.10 HHS can accelerate this by providing federal payment pathways that
reward successful state experimentation rather than requiring each state to build from scratch.
Additionally, the Rural Health Transformation Program (P.L. 119-21, § 71401) allocates $10
billion annually to states for rural health improvement, and the statute already contemplates
technology-enabled care delivery, including remote monitoring and AI. CMS should actively
encourage states to incorporate AI-enabled clinical care models into their transformation plans,
providing a funded pathway to test what this RFI envisions alongside the payment reforms
proposed above.
A note on evidence standards
Current evidence frameworks assume AI introduces risk that must be proven away; however,
AI-enabled care can be evaluated for 100% of encounters, compared to the small-sample chart
review that characterizes traditional quality assurance. This includes the ability to report
outcomes stratified by race, ethnicity, language, and disability status, which is critical for
ensuring that AI tools do not distort risk adjustment or produce disparate escalation patterns
across populations. HHS should develop evidence standards that account for this inversion in
which the AI-enabled model offers greater visibility into care quality, not less.
While payment ambiguity is the primary operational bottleneck, safety oversight and validation
standards are equally critical to sustainable adoption. Payment reform should proceed in
parallel with risk-tiered regulatory standards and evidence expectations.

Without Payment Reform, AI-Enabled Care Cannot Scale Through the
Healthcare System
The companies represented in this response will continue to operate regardless of HHS action.
Sufficient consumer demand exists to sustain direct-to-consumer business models, and
entrepreneurs will continue to build around the healthcare system if the system does not adapt.
Without HHS action, however, these gains will remain confined to cash-pay patients in metro
areas rather than reducing costs and expanding access system-wide, including to rural
communities where provider shortages make traditional models unsustainable.
The alternative is a fragmented, adversarial market that serves no stakeholder well. Health
systems will deploy AI under legacy fee‑for‑service rules and use it to dramatically increase visit
volume, capturing efficiency gains as revenue growth rather than lower total cost of care.
Payers, responding to rising utilization without a clear value or pricing framework, will refuse or
restrict reimbursement for AI‑enabled encounters. Fully autonomous AI care, which is already
operating in limited state sandboxes, will never progress beyond pilots because there is no
dedicated, nationally recognized payment pathway to support its adoption.
Meanwhile, patients are already turning to general-purpose consumer AI for health guidance.
Platforms like OpenAI’s ChatGPT and Anthropic’s Claude operate without HIPAA obligations or
clinical accountability, yet they are rapidly becoming the de facto source of health information for
millions of Americans. Consumers no longer need permission to ask about their healthcare. If
purpose-built clinical AI cannot scale through the healthcare system, general-purpose LLMs
without healthcare-specific obligations will fill the vacuum by default.
HHS has the tools to prevent this outcome. CMS's ACCESS model already points in the right
direction: its rates assume technology-enabled efficiency, creating a pathway for scaled digital
platforms rather than traditional fee-for-service operations.
Specifically, we recommend that CMS recognize asynchronous care as a billable modality
nationwide, create an AI-augmented modifier for existing E/M codes, expand APCM codes to
cover AI, establish billing codes for AI-autonomous clinical services, and pilot PMPM models
through CMMI. These payment pathways, designed to reimburse AI-enabled care at rates
below traditional equivalents, would reduce costs and expand access while generating the
claims data needed to support outcomes-based payment over time. We welcome the
opportunity to work with HHS to design and pilot these pathways.
