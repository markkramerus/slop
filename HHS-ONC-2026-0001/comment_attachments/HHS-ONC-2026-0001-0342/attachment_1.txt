The Asian & Pacific Islander American Health Forum (APIAHF) writes to highlight
critical considerations for Artificial Intelligence (AI) security and guardrails, workforce
impacts and transparency as the Department of Health and Human Services considers
AI adoption as part of clinical care.
The healthcare industry was amongst the first to explore AI applications and earlier
adopters to deploy AI to scale – from clinical workflows to insurance approvals and
other automated applications – utilizing AI at more than twice the rate of the broader
economy.12 AI has already transformed the healthcare landscape, and to disregard its
impacts, both positive and negative, can only harm patients.
With a national network of over 300 community-based organizational partners in 44
states and territories, APIAHF is the nation’s oldest national health advocacy
organization dedicated to improving the health and well-being of over 26.4 million
Asian Americans, Native Hawaiians and Pacific Islanders (AANHPIs) who would be
affected by these technologies.

AI Security, Guardrails, and Public Trust
The promise of AI in clinical care cannot be realized without robust security controls and governance
frameworks that protect sensitive health information and patient safety. AI systems handling protected
health data must integrate cybersecurity best practices, privacy-by-design principles, and continuous
validation protocols to guard against breaches, adversarial attacks, and systemic failure.
In addition to basic security measures, AI must consider health privacy protections including compliance
with HIPAA guidelines.3 This means, any and all training for AI models must be in closed loop systems
and have clear patient consent to drive improvement.
Without such protections, AI adoption risks eroding trust among patients, especially communities
historically subject to discrimination in healthcare systems. Americans are concerned about how AI
utilizes their data and how adoption of this data will affect their daily lives, including the quality of their
healthcare treatment and coverage.4,5 In order to build and sustain trust, there must be robust oversight
that includes quality standards and continuous monitoring and checkpoints – outside of industry, and in
conjunction from trusted leaders within community.6
Equally important is transparency in model performance, limitations and validation.7 AI developers and
healthcare organizations should publish performance metrics across diverse populations and explicitly
disclose known limitations, so clinicians and patients can interpret outputs appropriately. This level of
transparency enables informed decision-making, supports accountability, and guards against hidden
algorithmic failures that could disproportionately harm marginalized groups.
Real-World Challenges and Risks
Data is the most critical component of AI, and quality data is essential to make AI work for all
Americans.8 Accurate and representative data collection and data disaggregation is critical to eliminating
biases and further reducing clinical errors with AI in healthcare.

AI bias is not hypothetical. AI should improve, not amplify biases in clinical performance. Real clinical
AI tools have shown disparate effects across different demographic groups:
• AI models exhibiting underdiagnosis bias in chest radiographs for under-served patient
populations due to bias across multiple datasets, specifically for female patients, younger
patients, Black and Hispanic patients, and patients with lower socioeconomic status (via
insurance coverage). With patients who belonged in more than one of these categories, they were
even more likely to experience underdiagnosis.9
• An AI algorithm that assigned similar risk scores to Black and white patients even when Black
patients had worse health outcomes, including Black patients with greater chronic conditions.10
The result was that Black patients were less likely than equally sick white patients to be enrolled
for extra care or health programs. The algorithm didn’t use race as a predictor, but because Black
patients historically received less medical spending for the same level of illness, the real-world
bias toward Black patients resulted in under-identified care.
• AI models to detect skin cancers were trained predominantly using datasets of images of lighter
skin, developing an inherent bias toward the dominant skin tone and lesion colors from the
trainings.11 This can lead to underdiagnosis or misdiagnosis of patients with darker skin.
This is not to say that AI algorithms are inherently biased. These examples underscore how
insufficiently diverse training data, lack of representative validation, and opaque model design can
reinforce existing health inequities, particularly in communities that have already faced bias in clinical
settings, such as many AANHPI subgroups.12
According to the 2024 National Academies report which revisits the groundbreaking 2003 Unequal
Treatments report; racial and ethnic inequities in health and healthcare persist, costing our nation
millions in preventable deaths and hundreds of billions of dollars annually.13 One of the key factors is
the lack of uniform or comprehensive data on race and ethnicity which prevents data-driven decision
making in clinical settings.14
Race and ethnicity are not the only considerations for AI bias. A widely used AI model used to help
physicians diagnose and treat sepsis earlier identified just 7 percent of patients with sepsis who did not
receive timely treatment, but did not detect sepsis in 67 percent patients of those who did developed
sepsis.15 The model was tested in only a few geographical settings and was not correctly “calibrated” for

According to a 2020 study, out of 74 studies used to develop image-based diagnostic AI systems, there
were only 16 states represented, with the majority of these studies relying on data from only California,
New York, and Massachusetts.16 This means that rural states and rural communities were completely
excluded – and their quality of care as diagnosed by AI suffers. This is especially concerning, given that
AI is increasingly being deployed to supplement rural healthcare.17 Given the recent $50 billion Rural
Health Fund investment by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services to transform and support
rural health landscape, there must be intentional investment in rural health data to bridge gaps in care.18
To prevent AI from exacerbating disparities, developers and health systems must ensure diverse
representation in data, bias audits and fairness oversight. AI research through intentional design and
accountability must be core to HHS’s mission for implementation.
For the AANHPI community whose many subgroups span numerous languages, cultural practices and
health profiles, standardized models trained on limited populations risk insensitive or inaccurate results.
Disaggregated validation, robust bias mitigation methods and community-based monitoring must be
core features of AI deployment frameworks.
Future Opportunities of AI in Health and Healthcare
Despite risks, AI can expand care access and quality in ways that directly benefit underserved
communities, particularly when thoughtfully designed:
• Language access tools: AI-powered natural language processing and translation services can
support real-time interpretation and documentation in clinical settings for limited English
proficient (LEP) patients. This can improve communication, medication instruction
comprehension, and treatment adherence across communities, especially for patients with
complex medical needs.19 While many hospitals offer some sort of interpretation for LEP
patients, many report that there is a shortage of available, qualified and timely translators, despite
being mandated for large healthcare systems – leading to significant gaps in patient care.20
• Culturally sensitive patient engagement: Generative AI can produce culturally distinct responses

One example of AI models benefiting Asian Americans is the ability for early detection of stomach
cancer through non-invasive screening methods. Stomach cancer is one of the deadliest cancers in the
United States due to late diagnosis.24 In the U.S., only 30 percent of patients who are diagnosed reach
the five-year survival mark while in other countries with national screening programs, the survival rate
is much higher from 60-70 percent.25 Currently, medical societies do not recommend universal routine
screening for stomach cancer in the U.S., because it is not “common in the U.S.” despite high incidence
rates among certain groups.26 Because there are no standard or routine screening tests recommended for
stomach cancer, patients see higher insurance denials due to lack of “medical necessity.”27
AI can create more efficient screening protocols by using noncontrast CT imaging which is considered a
routine procedure and is more cost effective and less invasive than an endoscopy.28 Additionally, CTs
are more widely available and faster than endoscopies, making them a key tool, especially for
populations that may not be able to quickly access care or insurance approvals.29 This AI model, coined
GRAPE, was created as a large-scale screening method to identify gastric cancer; a huge benefit for
patients who have been denied from life-saving screenings due to cost or access.30

Conclusion
AI holds transformative potential for clinical care, but only if adopted with strong guardrails, transparent
evaluation, and equity-centered governance. Federal strategies should:
1. Mandate security, explainability and performance transparency across patient demographics.
2. Incentivize inclusive data practices, bias audits and community accountability boards to ensure
equitable outcomes.
3. Prioritize tools that expand access and language-sensitive care for underserved and diverse
populations.
By embedding these principles into AI adoption and oversight policies, HHS can safeguard patient
welfare, uphold public trust, and foster innovation that enhances clinical care for all Americans.
