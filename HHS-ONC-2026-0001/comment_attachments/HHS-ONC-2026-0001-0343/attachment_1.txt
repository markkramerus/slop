Dear ASTP/ONC Leadership,
Thank you for the opportunity to respond to the Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and
Use of Artificial Intelligence as part of Clinical Care. ADVION is a national organization representing
health information technology (health IT) companies that develop and distribute full clinical electronic
health records (EHRs), billing and point-of-care health IT systems and other software solutions serving
the majority of Long Term and Post Acute Care providers (i.e., assisted living facilities (ALF), Home
Health Agencies (HHAs), Inpatient Rehabilitation Facilities (IRFs), Long Term Care Hospitals (LTCHs) and
Skilled Nursing Facilities (SNFs)). ADVION also represents rehabilitation therapy companies; providers of
clinical laboratory and portable x-ray services; suppliers of complex medical equipment and other
specialized supplies. ADVION is a founding member of the Long Term & Post-Acute Care Health IT
Collaborative, which was formed in 2005 to advance health IT issues by encouraging coordination
among provider organizations, policymakers, vendors, payers and other stakeholders.
The following responses are tailored to long‑term post‑acute care (LTPAC) and rehabilitation settings
only, aligned to those questions and grounded in the RFI text.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption and
use in clinical care?
From a LTPAC and rehabilitation perspective, the biggest barriers cluster around infrastructure,
incentives, governance, and evidence.
 Fragmented data and limited interoperability

 LTPAC and rehab providers often lack certified EHRs with modern APIs, which limits
access to the longitudinal data needed to train and deploy AI safely (e.g., labs, imaging,
claims, SDOH, home health notes).
 Many facilities still rely on paper, siloed point solutions, or minimal EHR modules, so the
“fuel” for AI (structured, standardized data) is missing or of poor quality.
 Misaligned payment and thin margins
 Fee‑for‑service and per‑diem payment in SNFs, IRFs, LTCHs, and home health create
little direct financial reward for investing in AI tools that may reduce hospitalizations or
improve function but do not clearly change billable units.
 Tight operating margins in LTPAC make capital‑intensive AI investments difficult absent
explicit reimbursement, shared‑savings participation, or clear programmatic support.
 Governance, liability, and clinical accountability gaps
 Many LTPAC organizations lack mature AI governance structures (e.g., AI oversight
committees, model risk management, bias review), creating organizational risk aversion.
 Uncertainty about liability when AI contributes to clinical decision‑making (especially
without device‑like FDA oversight) discourages use in nursing homes and rehab facilities
that already face high litigation risk.
 Workforce, workflow, and usability challenges
 Nursing, therapy, and medical director staffing constraints limit capacity to learn and
safely integrate new AI tools into daily workflows (e.g., falls risk prediction, staffing
optimization, documentation support).
 Many tools are designed for acute care and not adapted to high‑acuity, long‑stay
populations, leading to poor fit and low adoption in LTPAC.
 Limited LTPAC‑specific evidence and benchmarks
 Most AI evidence, benchmarks, and reference implementations focus on hospitals and
ambulatory care, not skilled nursing, IRF, LTCH, or home health populations.
 This lack of LTPAC‑specific performance data makes it difficult for providers and payers
to invest, particularly for smaller and rural facilities.

implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures (e.g., relating to

what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them? In non‑device AI tools used as part of clinical
care (e.g., documentation assistants, risk stratification, workflow optimization), LTPAC and rehab
organizations face several distinctive legal and operational issues.
 Allocation of responsibility and liability

 When AI tools suggest care plans, staffing levels, or risk scores, it can be unclear how
responsibility is shared among vendors, facility leadership, and clinicians if harm occurs,
particularly where regulations emphasize interdisciplinary care planning and physician
oversight.
 HHS could develop frameworks and guidance, in partnership with other agencies where
appropriate, clarifying reasonable expectations for documentation of AI use, clinician
override, and institutional governance to support defensible clinical practice.
 Privacy, surveillance, and consent in congregate settings
 Non‑device AI tools that rely on video, audio, or sensor data in shared spaces (e.g., for
falls or elopement detection) raise unique privacy, consent, and dignity issues for
residents, family, and staff in nursing homes and rehab facilities.
 HHS could issue best‑practice guidance on consent, signage, data minimization, and
secondary use of such data in LTPAC, aligned with existing HIPAA and civil rights
protections, to balance safety and privacy.
 Vendor contracting and indemnification
 Smaller LTPAC organizations often lack bargaining power to obtain balanced terms
around performance guarantees, bias mitigation, incident response, and
indemnification when contracting for AI tools that influence care.
 HHS could publish model contract clauses or voluntary templates that address
transparency (e.g., access to performance metrics, data provenance), audit rights, and
responsibilities when AI tools materially affect resident care.
 Documentation and survey readiness
 AI‑generated notes and recommendations may not align with surveyor expectations for
clear attribution, clinician signatures, or demonstration of resident‑centered care
planning.
 HHS can clarify, through guidance and interpretive materials, how AI‑assisted
documentation can be used while maintaining compliance with existing conditions of
participation and survey processes.

LTPAC and rehab organizations need practical, low‑burden evaluation methods that recognize their data
and staffing constraints.
 Pre‑deployment and post‑deployment methods

 Prospective “silent mode” evaluations in which AI tools run in the background without
affecting care, comparing AI outputs with existing decisions and resident outcomes, can
be feasible in SNFs, IRFs, and home health, especially for readmission, falls, or pressure
injury risk prediction.
 Ongoing post‑deployment monitoring using simple dashboards (e.g., calibration drift,

 Human‑centered and workflow evaluations
 Usability testing with nursing, therapy, and aide staff in real LTPAC workflows (med
passes, therapy scheduling, restorative nursing) is critical, given high turnover and
varied digital literacy.
 Simulation‑based assessments (e.g., mock care conferences, documentation exercises)
can reveal where AI tools add burden or risk, particularly in interdisciplinary care
planning.
 HHS support mechanisms
 Grants and cooperative agreements could fund shared LTPAC “testbeds” where vendors
and providers evaluate AI tools using standardized measures and common data sets.
 Prize competitions could target AI tools that demonstrate superior performance and
usability in post‑acute outcomes (e.g., functional gains, community discharge,
rehospitalization), with clearly defined, transparent evaluation protocols.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, industry driven
testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
HHS can play an enabling role by recognizing and aligning with private sector initiatives that set quality
bars for AI in clinical care.
 Signal and alignment from HHS programs
 HHS could recognize independent AI accreditation or certification bodies that meet
transparent criteria (e.g., documented bias testing, post‑deployment monitoring,
user‑centered design) and allow participation or scoring advantages in relevant HHS
programs when accredited tools are used in LTPAC settings.
 Quality reporting and value‑based programs that include LTPAC and rehab providers
could incorporate optional measures or attestations related to use of accredited AI tools
that support quality and safety.
 Support for sector‑specific standards
 HHS could convene LTPAC‑focused stakeholders to define baseline expectations for AI
tools used in nursing homes, IRFs, LTCHs, and home health (e.g., data elements, minimal
performance metrics, explainability), which private accreditors could adopt.

6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost expectations
and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the greatest potential to
improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
In LTPAC and rehabilitation, many AI tools are emerging, but performance and cost experience is mixed.
 Areas where AI has met or exceeded expectations
 Ambient clinical documentation and coding support can reduce time spent on notes and
MDS/IRF‑PAI/HH documentation, decreasing burden for clinicians and enabling more
face‑to‑face care when well‑implemented.
 Predictive models for avoidable hospitalizations, when integrated into interdisciplinary
care management and nurse triage workflows, can help prioritize at‑risk residents and
support timely interventions.
 Areas where AI has fallen short
 Tools trained primarily on hospital or outpatient data often perform poorly on frail,
cognitively impaired, and multi‑morbid LTPAC populations, leading to low trust and
limited clinical uptake.
 Some solutions increase documentation or alert fatigue, adding complexity to already
stretched workflows and eroding any projected ROI.
 High‑potential, novel tools for LTPAC and rehab
 AI‑enabled functional status and mobility prediction to tailor therapy intensity,
anticipate discharge needs, and reduce rehospitalization risk in IRFs, SNFs, LTCHs, and
home health.
 AI tools that support behavioral health and dementia care in LTPAC (e.g., early detection
of behavioral escalation, personalized non‑pharmacologic interventions) while
respecting residents’ rights and privacy.
 Workforce optimization tools that align staffing, skill mix, and scheduling with dynamic
resident acuity and fall/pressure injury risk, supporting both quality and cost
containment.

7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations have the most
influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary administrative hurdles to the
adoption of AI in clinical care?
Adoption of AI in LTPAC and rehabilitation is shaped by a distinct set of roles and administrative
constraints.
 Influential decision‑makers in LTPAC and rehab
 Facility and system leadership (CEOs, administrators, CFOs) and clinical leaders
(directors of nursing, medical directors, therapy directors) are primary decision‑makers
for AI that affects care delivery and staffing.
 At multi‑facility organizations, corporate compliance, IT, and quality leaders often act as
gatekeepers, assessing legal risk, interoperability, and alignment with quality and survey
requirements.
 Primary administrative hurdles
 Lengthy contracting, security, and legal review processes-compounded by limited
internal expertise on AI risk-delay or prevent implementation, especially in smaller
organizations.
 Survey, compliance, and documentation concerns create additional hurdles, as leaders
worry that AI‑generated outputs could conflict with regulatory expectations or be
difficult to explain to surveyors and auditors.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and accelerate
the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data types, data standards, and
benchmarking tools.
Enhanced interoperability is foundational to effective AI in LTPAC and rehabilitation.
 Data types and standards
 Consistent, computable access to post‑acute assessment data (e.g., MDS items, IRF‑PAI,
LTCH CARE data set, OASIS), medications, vital signs, problem lists, functional scores,
and social needs is critical for accurate AI.
 Continued evolution and use of standard vocabularies and data models relevant to
LTPAC (e.g., alignment of assessment items with standardized codes and FHIR
resources) would support safer model development and deployment.
 Interfaces and benchmarking tools

9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the adoption
and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers have related to the
adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
Patients and caregivers in LTPAC and rehabilitation settings face distinctive challenges that AI could help
address, alongside notable concerns.
 Challenges AI could help address
 Need for more timely recognition of clinical deterioration, pain, depression, and
functional decline in residents who cannot easily advocate for themselves, including
those with cognitive impairment.
 Desire for better communication about care goals, progress in rehabilitation, and
discharge planning across multiple providers and family caregivers.
 Key concerns about AI use
 Fear that AI will replace human interaction and reduce the personal, relational aspects
of care that are central to long‑stay environments like nursing homes.
 Worries about surveillance, loss of privacy, and data misuse when sensors, cameras, or
conversational systems are introduced into resident rooms and common spaces.
 Concern that AI might introduce or amplify bias, resulting in lower quality care or fewer
rehabilitative opportunities for certain groups (e.g., by age, disability, race, language, or
payer).

of stay, staffing efficiency, and caregiver burden, as well as distributional effects across
payers, providers, and patients.
