I. Executive Summary: The Infrastructure for AI Readiness
While much of the healthcare industry's focus remains on the development of AI-
powered applications, Citrix identifies the primary bottleneck to adoption as the
deployment and delivery layer. In a healthcare environment characterized by shared
workstations, mobile clinicians, and strict HIPAA requirements, an AI tool is only as
effective as its availability at the point of care.
Citrix provides an industry leading platform that serves as a digital foundation for many
healthcare organizations in support of delivering secure, connected healthcare services.
With a footprint that includes a majority of the largest healthcare organizations in the
United States and over 4,900 healthcare customers worldwide, our role is to provide the
governance layer and platform that allows healthcare organizations to transition from AI
experimentation to large-scale clinical use without compromising security or
productivity. Our platform also helps remove friction at the bedside to ensure that
technology works invisibly in the background, allowing care teams to stay focused on
patients.
II. Response to Specific RFI Questions (1,3,5,6)
Q1: What are the primary barriers to the adoption of AI in clinical settings?
Citrix identifies the primary barrier to AI adoption as a deployment gap — the technical
and operational friction that prevents an AI insight from reaching a clinician at the point
of care.
To overcome this, HHS should address the following structural barriers:
● The "Last Mile" delivery problem: High-performing AI models have low adoption
in clinical settings because they are not integrated into the roaming workflows of a
hospital. In environments dominated by shared workstations, a clinician cannot
afford the "cognitive tax" of logging into a separate, disconnected AI application.

● Compute and hardware disparity: Modern clinical AI agents require significant
computational power. Most healthcare systems are burdened by "technical debt,"
utilizing legacy endpoints that cannot natively support resource-heavy AI
processing.
● Provider abrasion and "Login Fatigue": Clinicians are facing record levels of
burnout. If an AI tool adds even 30 seconds to a workflow or requires additional
authentication events, it will be bypassed.
● Governance-Induced latency: The traditional process for vetting and provisioning
new software in a hospital takes months, preventing clinical teams from iterating on
AI tools at the speed of innovation.
Q1 Recommendation:
HHS should prioritize frameworks that support unified workspace delivery. By
decoupling AI applications from physical hardware constraints; this allows healthcare
organizations to scale innovation instantly across legacy infrastructure while eliminating
clinician "login fatigue" through seamless, governed workflow integration at the point of
care.
Q3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may
raise novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance
and accountability structures (e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy,
and security). What novel legal and implementation issues exist, and what role, if
any, should HHS play to help address them?
The AI regulatory and legal framework is rapidly evolving, fragmented across US state
regulations, as well as global regulations that have extraterritorial applicability such as
the EU AI Act. Technology vendors provide the AI capabilities and are regulated as
such, but the extent to how those AI capabilities are deployed and used, together with
the associated legal and regulatory risks, at its core rests with the legal, security and
compliance leadership teams within the individual healthcare organizations.
Fundamentally, it is the type of tools and technologies that should be implemented that
HHS can best help influence organizations to meet their regulatory, legal and customer
obligations.
Given the pace of change, the guidance should be technology agnostic to
accommodate the unknown. Insofar as the implementation aspect of AI is concerned,
the primary challenge for non-medical device AI is the "Shadow AI" security gap and
subsequent data drift, where clinicians may input Protected Health Information (PHI)
into unauthorized public AI models. This not only leads to sensitive data residing on
unmanaged endpoints but also creates the risk that these public models could learn

from and retain PHI, potentially compromising patient privacy. To address this, robust
controls must be implemented to prevent the use of public AI models within the
healthcare workplace, ensuring that only approved, governed AI tools are utilized for
clinical purposes.
To address these risks, Citrix recommends that HHS prioritize a Zero Trust Access
framework that allows Risk & Resilience Leaders (CISOs) to quantify risk reduction in
an objective manner that will support their compliance and financial objectives, including
objective and verifiable data points that are required by the insurance market to
underwrite the associated risks.
• Granular, Role-Based Governance for AI Identities: HHS should encourage
adaptive, role based, and context aware access policies where AI availability and
scope of permitted use is tied strictly to a user’s clinical role and context aware
data loss prevention (DLP) rules. Since 71% of CISOs report that AI has access
to core systems, but only 16% govern that access effectively, Citrix recommends
a governed environment that enforces least-privilege for AI "identities" to prevent
unauthorized data exfiltration together with DLP rules that limit activities such as
printing, pasting or downloading of sensitive data.
• Improving MTTR and MTTC Metrics: Underwriters now demand proof of strict
controls to offer competitive coverage. Citrix suggests the use of virtualized,
governed sessions that enable IT to:
o Reduce Mean Time to Contain (MTTC): Quarantine unstable or
compromised devices within 30 minutes of threat detection.
o Improve Mean Time to Respond (MTTR): Slashing response times from
hours to minutes using automated session isolation and lockout for risky
behavior.
• Standardized Patching Cycles: To lower premiums and justify deductibles,
organizations must prove cybersecurity maturity through patching cycle time. By
centralizing AI application delivery, IT leaders can automate policy updates,
ensuring that the clinical ecosystem is hardened against attack precursors
without manual deskside toil.
• Quantifiable Risk Posture: Citrix recommends that HHS incentivize the use of
Agentic AI Posture Scores and Cyber Risk Quantification (CRQ). By
containing AI processing within hosted sessions, hospitals can demonstrate a
measurable reduction in "probable loss" (e.g., from $5M to $1M) by preventing
PHI from ever residing on local, unmanaged endpoints.

• Unified Forensic Auditability: Evaluating liability is a fact specific analysis and

negotiations.
Q3 Recommendation:
HHS should prioritize the adoption of a Zero Trust Access framework that enforces
context and role-based governance and unified telemetry for clinical AI. By delivering AI
tools through a governed, virtualized environment rather than on local endpoints,
healthcare organizations can effectively minimize the "Shadow AI" security gap, prevent
PHI data drift, and improve quantifiable risk metrics—such as Mean Time to Contain
(MTTC)—which are essential for maintaining cyber-insurance eligibility and
organizational resilience.
Q5. What type of workforce, skills, and resources (e.g., educational and training,
financial, etc.) are needed to enable and support the adoption and use of AI in
clinical care? How can HHS support these needs?
The successful adoption of clinical AI requires a workforce shift from traditional "break-
fix" IT to Clinical AI-Ops. Currently, healthcare IT teams are overburdened with manual
system maintenance, particularly on securing traditional PC endpoints, leaving little
capacity for the specialized oversight and optimization that AI requires. Citrix
recommends the following workforce and resource priorities:
• Transitioning IT to AI orchestrators: The workforce needs a new class of
professionals who understand both clinical workflow and the delivery of high-
compute AI models.
• Reclaiming workforce capacity through automation: IT staff cannot develop
new AI skills while buried under high ticket volumes and manual patching.
• Continuous enablement via telemetry: Training should not be a one-time
event, but a continuous process driven by real-world usage data.
Q5 Recommendation:
HHS should leverage existing high-standard technical training infrastructures to
establish a National Clinical AI-Ops Certification, while simultaneously incentivizing the
use of operational excellence automation to reclaim the IT workforce capacity
necessary to support and orchestrate AI innovation at scale.

Q6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance
and cost expectations, and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI
tools would have the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give
new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
While many AI tools exhibit high accuracy in controlled environments, they often fall
short in clinical practice due to high abrasion and the inability to maintain performance
across a hospital’s disparate hardware.
To ensure AI meets both performance and cost expectations, Citrix recommends that
HHS prioritize metrics centered on workflow integration rather than just algorithmic
accuracy.
● Addressing performance shortfalls through "Time-to-Chart": AI performance in
clinical care should be measured by its impact on the clinician’s entire session, not
just the tool's isolated response time.
● Novel AI potential: The greatest potential for cost reduction and outcome
improvement lies in AI that acts as an invisible background assistant rather than a
standalone application.
● Cost-effectiveness via infrastructure optimization: Many AI tools fail cost
expectations because they may require expensive local hardware upgrades.
Q6 Recommendation: HHS should define AI performance and cost-effectiveness
through the lens of Clinical Throughput; by prioritizing novel AI that is decoupled from
hardware and integrated into roaming workspaces, HHS can ensure that innovation
reduces provider burden and lowers healthcare costs without requiring costly
infrastructure overhauls.
III. Proposed strategic framework for HHS
To successfully transition clinical AI from isolated pilots to a national standard, Citrix
recommends that HHS prioritize a regulatory and reimbursement framework built on
three interdependent pillars: User Experience, Tailored Security, and Operational
Excellence. This framework is essential because even the most advanced AI model will
fail if it is not accessible within the high-pressure, mobile environment of a hospital.
User Experience must be the primary driver of adoption; AI insights must be
integrated into a roaming workspace that ensures sub-10-second access at the
bedside, preventing the "login fatigue" that contributes to clinician burnout.
Tailored Security must provide the necessary guardrails through role-based
access and governed sessions, effectively closing the "Shadow AI" gap and
preventing data drift without introducing workflow friction.
