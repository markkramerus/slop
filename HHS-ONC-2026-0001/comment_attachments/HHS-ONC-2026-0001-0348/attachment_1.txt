Three Pillars for AI Acceleration
Pillar 1: Regulatory Clarity Enables Innovation
Lilly supports risk-proportionate, adaptive regulatory frameworks that provide certainty while maintaining
rigorous safety and efficacy standards. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) should also leverage AI
internally to streamline review processes while maintaining transparent governance and protecting
confidential information.
Pillar 2: Reimbursement Modernization Is Critical
Legacy fee-for-service (FFS) payment systems represent the single largest barrier to AI adoption in clinical
care. The fundamental problem: FFS systems are structurally misaligned with AI’s value proposition. AI tools
deliver value through preventive interventions that avoid future costs, efficiency gains that reduce
unnecessary procedures, and quality improvements through earlier detection, none of which generate
billable services under FFS economics, which reimburses based on volume rather than outcomes. 2 Without
payment reform, AI innovations may remain throttled by reimbursement inertia regardless of clinical value
or regulatory approval.
Pillar 3: Strategic Research & Development (R&D) Investment Accelerates Translation
Public-private partnerships and targeted research funding can bridge the gap between AI development and
real-world clinical implementation.

Priority Recommendations
I. REGULATORY CLARITY
1. Expand Adaptive Clinical Trial Frameworks
Current Barrier: Traditional clinical trial regulations (e.g., 21 CFR Parts 312, 314) assume static protocols,
fixed endpoints, and predetermined sample sizes, creating friction for AI-enabled adaptive designs that
optimize recruitment in real time.
FDA should clarify acceptable approaches to AI-driven adaptive randomization, dynamic endpoint
modification, and continuous learning algorithms, including AI-driven dosing recommendations such as
precise timing of next dose administration. This clarity would accelerate precision prevention therapies
requiring large, long-duration studies.
2. Modernize HIPAA De-identification Standards for AI Era
Current Barrier: The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) de-identification
framework under 45 CFR §164.514 is structurally incompatible with modern data operations. The safe
harbor’s 18-identifier checklist neither accounts for AI-enabled re-identification risks nor provides flexibility
for privacy-preserving techniques that may offer stronger protections. More critically, the expert
determination pathway requires individualized expert certification for every dataset configuration, which is
an operational impossibility for organizations running continuous, enterprise-scale tokenization and
pseudonymization pipelines.
HHS should modernize §164.514 in three targeted ways: (1) establish a formal pseudonymization tier that
provides regulatory recognition for tokenized data held under controlled re-linkage architectures, rather
than treating it identically to unprotected personal health information (PHI); (2) replace per-instance expert
determinations with a certification framework developed by the National Institute of Standards and
Technology (NIST) for approved privacy-preserving methodologies (including differential privacy, federated
learning, and synthetic data generation) that satisfy the “very small risk” standard by design; and (3) issue
affirmative guidance on permissible use of health data for AI model training, closing the significant
interpretive gap that currently drives inconsistent compliance practices and impedes beneficial AI
development. These changes would reduce compliance friction while providing more consistent and
technically grounded privacy protections than the existing framework achieves.
3. Establish Platform Technology Designations for AI-Enabled Therapeutics
Current Barrier: AI platforms that generate multiple therapeutic candidates must undergo separate,
redundant regulatory review for each candidate, despite using the same validated underlying technology.
FDA should expand its Platform Technology Designation program to AI-enabled drug discovery platforms,
allowing one-time validation of core AI methodology with streamlined review for subsequent therapeutics
generated using the validated platform. This accelerates development of AI-discovered therapeutics,
particularly for rare diseases and areas of high unmet need.
4. Clarify Governance for Foundation Models and Third-Party AI
Current Barrier: Many pharmaceutical AI applications leverage foundation models (e.g., AlphaFold or large
language models) developed by third parties, but current guidance doesn’t address validation requirements
when sponsors lack full access to training data or architecture. 3

II. REIMBURSEMENT MODERNIZATION
6. Create Medicare Coverage Pathways for AI-Enabled Preventive Diagnostics
Current Barrier: Medicare’s statutory framework (42 USC §1395y(a)(1)(A)) has been interpreted to
exclude coverage for “screening tests,” creating a categorical barrier to AI-enabled diagnostics that identify
disease risk before symptoms manifest. 5
The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) should clarify its coverage authority to distinguish AI-
enabled diagnostic procedures (targeted testing based on validated biomarkers, genetic risk, or AI-predicted
risk scores) from traditional screening tests (population-wide testing without risk stratification). Example:
AI-powered digital cognitive assessments can detect early cognitive decline and enable care planning yet
lack clear Medicare coverage pathways because they may be classified as screening rather than diagnostic
tools. 6 This reform would enable earlier intervention rather than reactive late-stage treatment.
7. Expand Voluntary Value-Based Payment Models for AI Clinical Decision Support
Current Barrier: Healthcare systems have no direct financial incentive to adopt AI clinical decision support
tools under FFS payment; efficiency gains from AI may actually reduce revenue (fewer hospital days, fewer
procedures). While CMS’s WISeR model tests AI-enabled prior authorization to reduce waste, no parallel
incentives exist for AI tools that proactively improve clinical outcomes. 7

CMS should create complementary alternative payment models (via 42 CFR Part 425, §414.1380) that
reward healthcare systems for adopting validated AI tools that demonstrably improve outcomes or reduce
total cost of care. Medicare Advantage risk-adjustment bonuses, Merit-based Incentive Payment System
(MIPS) quality points, and shared savings programs should explicitly incentivize evidence-based AI adoption
that enhances care quality, not just reduces utilization. Balanced incentives would be created for AI adoption
focused on both waste reduction and outcome improvement.
8. Extend Medicare Eligibility for Conditions Requiring Early Intervention
Current Barrier: Medicare eligibility begins at age 65 (with exceptions for end-stage renal disease and
amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)), creating barriers to early intervention for chronic diseases with long
preclinical phases. The recently introduced BRIDGE Act addresses Alzheimer’s disease by eliminating
waiting periods for symptomatic young-onset patients, but broader payment system reforms are needed as
AI technologies increasingly enable identification of high-risk asymptomatic individuals requiring preventive
intervention decades before age 65. 8
Congress should pass the BRIDGE Act and use it as a model for condition-specific Medicare eligibility
expansions as scientific evidence supports early intervention. As AI-powered precision medicine advances,
HHS should establish frameworks ensuring payment systems can accommodate validated preventive
therapies for high-risk populations identified through genetic testing, biomarker analysis, and AI-enabled
risk prediction, enabling intervention during optimal treatment windows regardless of age-based eligibility
thresholds—an approach that improves patient outcomes and reduces long-term program costs.
9. Prevent Misuse of AI in Coverage Determination
Current Barrier: Growing concern that payers may deploy AI tools to automate coverage denials or
accelerate prior authorization rejections without adequate clinical oversight or transparency. 9
HHS should establish guardrails for payer use of AI in coverage decisions, including transparency
requirements (disclosure of AI system purpose, data sources, decision criteria), clinical oversight mandates
(meaningful physician review, with AI authorized to approve coverage but all denials requiring human
clinician determination), timely appeals processes, and bias auditing. AI should improve care delivery, not
automate barriers to access through coverage denial. 10 Public trust is built by preventing algorithmic
barriers to care while allowing AI to reduce administrative burden for routine approvals.

11. Invest in Bias Detection and Mitigation Research
Current Barrier: AI algorithms trained on historical data may perpetuate or amplify existing health
disparities; detecting and mitigating biases require specialized research expertise.
NIH should establish a dedicated research program focused on fairness metrics for clinical AI, algorithmic
auditing methods, bias mitigation techniques, and prospective studies measuring real-world impact of AI
tools on health disparities. AI algorithms used in drug development and clinical care must be free from bias
and avoid exacerbating health inequities. For example, in Alzheimer’s disease, which disproportionately
impacts women and certain minority populations, AI algorithms must account for sex-based and racial
differences in disease presentation, progression, and treatment response to ensure equitable outcomes. 13
Ensuring AI tools reduce rather than exacerbate health disparities is essential to building public trust.
12. Develop Real-World Performance Monitoring Infrastructure
Current Barrier: AI tools that perform well in controlled research settings may degrade when deployed in
diverse real-world environments; robust post-deployment monitoring remains methodologically
challenging.
HHS should fund research on automated performance monitoring systems, benchmarking frameworks, and
rapid validation protocols for algorithm updates. Cooperative agreements with health systems, academic
medical centers, and AI developers can create shared infrastructure for ongoing performance evaluation.
This enables continuous improvement while maintaining safety and building payer confidence in AI
reliability.
13. Foster International Harmonization
Current Barrier: Divergent international regulatory approaches to AI create duplicative requirements and
slow global development timelines.
HHS should prioritize engagement through the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH), International
Coalition of Medicines Regulatory Authorities (ICMRA), International Medical Device Regulators Forum
(IMDRF), and Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) to align on common AI
terminology, harmonized validation standards, and mutual recognition agreements. Recent FDA
collaborations with Health Canada and the United Kingdom’s Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory
Agency (UK MHRA) on machine learning-enabled medical devices demonstrate the value of this approach. 14
Regulatory burden is reduced for developers and accelerates global access to AI innovations.

14. Support Responsible FDA Adoption of AI for Review Efficiency
Current Barrier: As FDA scales AI adoption, lack of transparent governance creates uncertainty about
protecting confidential information, ensuring data quality, and maintaining human oversight.
FDA should continue deploying AI tools to streamline workflows and improve review consistency, while
establishing transparent governance frameworks addressing six critical considerations:
(1) Protect confidential commercial information, ensure AI tools are never trained on sponsor data without
permission, and ensure third-party vendors cannot access proprietary information; 15
(2) Maintain accountability, publish annual transparency reports on which AI tools are deployed, how they
contribute to decisions, and validation metrics; 16
(3) Ensure data quality, establish standards for training data representativeness and conduct bias audits; 17
(4) Require human oversight, mandate human review for novel therapeutics, expedited pathways, and safety
signals; 18
(5) Achieve cross-center consistency, create agency-wide AI governance body to harmonize deployment; 19
and
(6) Secure systems, protect against cyberattacks and adversarial manipulation. 20
Regular stakeholder engagement on FDA’s AI use is essential to building trust and sharing best practices. 21 It
enables FDA to realize AI efficiency gains while maintaining gold standard review and protecting sponsor
confidential information.
Responses to Key HHS Questions
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to AI adoption in clinical care?
Based on Lilly’s experience: (1) Reimbursement inertia (FFS payment systems fail to reward AI-enabled
prevention, efficiency gains, or quality improvements), (2) Regulatory uncertainty (unclear validation
standards), (3) Data access constraints (privacy regulations and fragmentation), (4) Liability ambiguity
(unclear responsibility allocation), and (5) Workforce readiness (insufficient AI literacy). Reimbursement is
the dominant barrier; many healthcare systems identify valuable AI tools but cannot justify adoption under
current payment models.
Question 3: What novel legal issues exist for non-medical device AI?
Two critical issues: (1) intellectual property (IP) protection during regulatory review, current trade secret
protections may be inadequate for AI algorithmic submissions to FDA; (2) Federated learning governance,
unclear rules on model ownership, institutional contribution recognition, and consent requirements when
data never leaves originating institutions. HHS should convene working groups to develop model liability

Question 5: How can HHS best support private sector AI innovation?
HHS can support innovation by demonstrating responsible AI adoption within its own operations. When FDA
successfully deploys AI tools, it sets standards, builds confidence, creates shared learning, and identifies
common barriers. However, this requires transparent communication through stakeholder engagement. 22
FDA should publish case studies, host workshops, maintain open dialogue, and work with NIST and
international regulators on best practices. See Recommendation #14.
Question 7: What influences AI adoption decisions within healthcare organizations?
Key decision-makers include C-suite leadership (strategic AI investment decisions based on return on
investment (ROI)), clinical department chairs (specialty-specific adoption), information technology (IT)
governance committees (technical integration), value analysis committees (cost-effectiveness), and front-
line clinicians (ultimate determinants through usage). Critical insight: Even when leadership approves AI
adoption, implementation fails without front-line clinician buy-in. Primary administrative hurdles include IT
integration complexity (legacy electronic health record (EHR) systems lack standardized application
programming interfaces (APIs)), credentialing processes (no clear approach for “credentialing” AI tools),
change management resources (limited capacity for training and workflow redesign), and contracting delays
(procurement processes poorly suited to AI tools requiring ongoing updates).
The Stakes Are High: If HHS acts decisively, the United States will maintain global leadership in healthcare
AI with enormous benefits for patients. If payment inertia and regulatory uncertainty persist, innovation will
migrate offshore and American patients will lose access to transformative technologies developed elsewhere.
Lilly stands ready to partner with HHS in building an AI-enabled healthcare system that is innovative,
equitable, and worthy of public trust.
