include companies and organizations from across the U.S. healthcare economy, agree

protect patient data and spur medical innovation.
We appreciate ASTP/ONC’s leadership in seeking input on how to accelerate the

adoption and responsible use of artificial intelligence (AI) in clinical care. AI tools
already support a wide range of clinical and operational functions across the healthcare
sector and have brought significant benefits in terms of improved quality, efficiency,
access, and outcomes. Their potential to further improve clinician care and the clinical
experience is enormous, but realizing this potential will require a policy approach that
encourages innovation, supports adoption at scale, and protects patient safety without
imposing unnecessary or duplicative regulatory burden.
A National Framework
ASTP/ONC asks about the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health
care and its adoption and use in clinical care. Currently, the most significant hurdle for
healthcare organizations is having to navigate and comply with a myriad of overlapping,
and potentially inconsistent, state laws regulating the use of AI and data, which is a key
building block for training AI models. Some of these laws are sector-specific, but many
are not. In 2025 alone, approximately 1,250 AI bills were introduced at the state level
seeking to regulate almost every aspect of the use of AI2, and the pace of new state
bills on AI is only increasing, with more and more being enacted each year. The number
and variations in state privacy laws has also increased sharply over recent years, with
many imposing significant new responsibilities and administrative burdens. Compliance
with this proliferation of state laws will be extremely challenging and onerous for the
vast majority of organizations that do not operate exclusively within one state.
Establishing a national standard or framework for the use of AI and health data in
healthcare is critical to ensure regulatory harmonization and avoid undue burden,
inefficiencies, and potential safety issues. For these reasons we support federal
preemption of state laws regulating AI and data privacy, especially for key provisions
where conflicting requirements would become impracticable or overly burdensome. This
would not apply to areas that fall under the authority of states to regulate, but only to AI
or privacy-specific laws. To the extent that states regulate or provide guidance in areas
that fall under the jurisdiction of states (like commercial insurance, data center
infrastructure, child safety protections, etc.), they should be encouraged to take a
coordinated approach using common models or frameworks to avoid inconsistencies
and variability.
Harmonize with Existing Frameworks to Protect Patients
Patient safety and trust are paramount to the acceptance of utilizing AI in clinical care,
and appropriate oversight is critical to ensure that the power of AI solutions benefit
patients and does not put their safety or wellbeing at risk. However, we believe that this
oversight and regulatory protection can largely be achieved through existing regulatory
frameworks, which already provide substantial protections for patients.
This is particularly important in the health sector, where various HHS agencies, such as
the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services
(CMS) and the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy and the Office of the National
Coordinator (ASTP/ONC) have already begun to issue regulations and guidance on the

use of AI within their areas of jurisdiction. A one-size-fits-all approach to AI regulation,
as is being proposed by some states, is destined to fail as it becomes clear that
overlaying a totally new framework over existing, often very different regulatory regimes
is impractical and, in many cases, infeasible. For example, the FDA has issued multiple
guidance documents on AI and machine learning in software as a medical device.
Similarly, HHS has issued regulations under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act
addressing the use of patient care decision support tools in clinical care, and
ASTP/ONC has issued transparency and risk management requirements for Predictive
Decision Support Interventions that are part of certified health information technology
(HIT), such as electronic health records. Any AI regulation should harmonize with these
existing requirements as well as with other regulatory requirements applicable to these
entities. It is also critical that the agencies within HHS and across other federal
departments that may have jurisdiction over activities of the health care sector
coordinate their approach to AI regulation so that there is consistency and
harmonization of requirements among agencies and across the sector.
For example, health care providers operate within established guardrails, including FDA
oversight where applicable, HIPAA and state privacy laws, professional standards of
care, accreditation requirements, and medical malpractice frameworks. These
mechanisms provide accountability and patient protections regardless of whether AI
tools are used. Similarly, health plans are using AI to support care management, quality
measurement, utilization management, and administrative automation such as prior
authorization and claims processing. These activities are already subject to program
integrity requirements, audit processes, consumer protection laws, and oversight
mechanisms that can be applied to AI-enabled functions without creating new,
duplicative rules.
Wherever possible, HHS should rely on and reinforce these existing guardrails rather
than layering on additional AI-specific requirements. Leveraging established oversight
promotes consistency, reduces compliance complexity, and allows health care
providers and plans to adopt AI responsibly while maintaining patient trust.
Avoiding Overregulation That Could Inhibit Innovation
Overly prescriptive or technology-specific regulation risks inhibiting innovation,
discouraging investment, and delaying the translation of promising tools into real-world
clinical use. For example, clinicians are increasingly deploying AI-enabled clinical
decision support tools to assist with imaging interpretation, pathology review, and early
detection of conditions such as sepsis or stroke. These tools augment clinician
judgment rather than replace it and are often refined continuously based on clinical
feedback and new evidence. Overly rigid requirements could slow iteration and limit the
ability of health care providers to tailor AI tools to their patient populations and care
settings.
Similarly, AI-powered documentation and workflow tools—such as ambient clinical
documentation, chart summarization, and coding assistance—are helping reduce
administrative burden and clinician burnout, allowing clinicians to spend more time on

direct patient care. These tools typically operate in the background of clinical workflows,
and prescriptive regulation could inadvertently discourage adoption even where patient
risk is low.
ASTP/ONC should prioritize flexible, principles-based approaches focused on
outcomes, transparency, and accountability, allowing innovation to evolve while
maintaining appropriate safeguards.
ASTP/ONC asks if any HHS regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to
augment the ability to develop or use AI in clinical care. There are several potentially
deregulatory actions that HHS could take to encourage the development of AI tools in
clinical care. These include addressing regulations that make it more difficult and costly
to manage data need to train AI models, such as significantly revising proposed updates
to the 2024 HIPAA Security Rule and better aligning the 42 C.F.R. pt. 2 (2025)
regulations with HIPAA consistent with amendments enacted in the Consolidated
Appropriations Act of 2021, Pub. L. No. 116-260. Working together with Congress, HHS
should also seek to strengthen HIPAA’s preemption framework and explore ways to
extend comparable privacy and security standards for personal health information held
by entities not currently subject to HIPAA. Greater nationwide consistency in privacy
and security protections would both enhance patient trust and provide clearer rules of
the road for responsible AI development and utilization.
Aligning Reimbursement to Incentivize Adoption
Reimbursement policy will be a critical driver of AI adoption in clinical care. Even when
AI tools demonstrate clear clinical or operational value, health care providers often lack
a sustainable financial pathway to adopt and integrate these technologies.
For example, health systems are using AI to support population health management
and risk stratification, identifying patients at high risk for hospitalization or care gaps and
enabling earlier, more targeted interventions. AI-enabled tools that improve diagnostic
accuracy, care coordination, or patient engagement—such as automated outreach,
triage, or follow-up—can improve access and outcomes, particularly in underserved
communities. Reimbursement policies that recognize the value of AI solutions will
facilitate this type of preventive and/or proactive care by making it financially viable for
health care providers. This not only benefits patients by improving care, but ultimately
reduces costs for the entire healthcare system.
We encourage HHS to explore reimbursement approaches that recognize and reward
appropriate use of AI tools, support integration into clinical workflows, and allow
flexibility across care settings and specialties.
ASTP/ONC notes that, for non-medical devices, use of AI in clinical care may raise
novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and
accountability structures (e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security),
and asks what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them. While ASTP/ONC
does not define "clinical care" in the RFI, it is important that regulation of AI in the health
Page 4 of 5

sector clearly distinguish between treatment or care provided by human beings,
including with the help of AI tools, versus consumer interactions with AI chatbots, such
as to ask about symptoms and treatments. The latter should without question be
regulated, but not as the provision of clinical care by health care professionals. Instead,
we recommend that the Federal Trade Commission oversee these types of generative
AI tools to ensure that consumers understand their risks and limitations.
The “supply chain” of AI models makes it challenging to determine responsibility/liability
in the event of an inaccurate recommendation. This underscores the importance of
transparency standards for vendors, and post-deployment standards for developers to
ensure ongoing integrity of tools.
Research and Development
ASTP/ONC asks if there are specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to
accelerate the adoption of AI as part of clinical care. We appreciate the approach of
regulatory sandboxes. Stakeholders from across the continuum including patients,
providers, developers, and payers should be included in these efforts.
Conclusion
Accelerating the adoption of AI in clinical care will require a balanced approach that
promotes innovation, aligns reimbursement with value, and relies on existing
safeguards to protect patients. By avoiding unnecessary regulation, incentivizing
responsible use through payment policy, and building on current oversight frameworks,
HHS can help ensure that AI advances clinical care in ways that are safe, effective, and
scalable across the health care system.
