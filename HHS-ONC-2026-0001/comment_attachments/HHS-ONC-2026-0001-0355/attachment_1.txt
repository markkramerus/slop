1 Executive Summary & Perspective
Mettle Solutions respectfully submits this response to the Department of Health and Human
Services (HHS). We respond not as an AI developer ("Builder"), but as a specialized Evaluator
and Builder of Assurance Tools that support the safe adoption of AI in clinical care. Our
perspective is informed by working across diverse care settings, from large academic health
systems to resource-constrained community practices, with AI tools that are often classified
as non-medical device technologies.
Regulatory Impact on AI Adoption: The Validation Gap
Current HHS regulatory frameworks appropriately emphasize patient safety, privacy, and public
trust. However, for many clinical AI tools, particularly those that fall outside traditional medical
device classifications—regulatory expectations for post-deployment validation and ongoing
performance monitoring remain unclear or implicit. This lack of clarity contributes to what we
describe as a “Validation Gap”: uncertainty among providers regarding how AI models perform
on their local patient populations over time.
In practice, validation is often retrospective, static, and disconnected from real-world clinical
workflows. Existing regulations do not consistently specify how health care organizations
should generate local evidence, monitor model drift, or demonstrate continued safety and
effectiveness once an AI tool is deployed. As a result, providers face two undesirable outcomes:
1) blind adoption, which introduces patient safety and liability risks, or
2) adoption paralysis, where otherwise valuable AI tools are delayed or rejected due to
regulatory ambiguity and unclear accountability.
This uncertainty disproportionately affects smaller and rural practices, which often lack the
resources to interpret evolving regulatory expectations or to independently design robust
validation processes.
An Independent Safety Layer as a Regulatory Enabler
To support a regulatory posture that is predictable, proportionate to risk, and enabling of
innovation, we advocate for an Independent Safety Layer approach that distinguishes
between building AI and assuring AI. This approach aligns with HHS’s goals of protecting
patients and maintaining public trust while allowing innovation to proceed more rapidly.
Our response outlines two implementation pathways to promote equitable adoption:
• Enterprise Track: “Shadow mode” validation prior to clinical use, combined with
continuous post-deployment surveillance (“sidecar” monitoring) to ensure models
perform safely across changing populations and conditions.
• Resource-Constrained Track: Mettle Solutions is currently developing a Local AI
Evaluator (under a contract with ASTP) which is a lightweight, no server backend
application that enables resource constrained healthcare providers to evaluate the
accuracy metrics of AI tools.

2 Responses to Specific Questions
2.1 Question 1: Barriers to Private Sector Innovation
What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption
and use in clinical care?
The single greatest impediment is uncertainty. Healthcare organizations operate in a zero-
failure environment. Providers cannot confidently adopt AI because they lack reliable tools to
answer the fundamental question: "Will this model work for my patients, in my workflow,
today?"
Key observed barriers include:
• The "Local Validity" Gap: A model validated in an urban academic center often
degrades when deployed in a rural clinic due to differences in demographics and
workflow.
• Undefined Liability: Providers fear that relying on AI advice creates new liability
exposure, particularly when models "hallucinate" or drift.
• The "Resource Gap": While large systems can hire data science teams to evaluate tools,
small practices cannot. This process risks creating a "two-tier" health system where AI
benefits are restricted to well-resourced centers.

2.3 Question 3: Novel Legal and Implementation Issues
For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel legal and
implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures (e.g.,
relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
1) Liability & Standard-of-Care Ambiguity
When "non-medical device" AI errors, it is unclear whether the fault lies with the clinician
(malpractice) or the vendor (product defect). This ambiguity stalls procurement and adoption.
Proposed Solution: "Gated Decision" Architecture
We recommend HHS endorse "Gated Decision" Architectures (or Safety Interlocks) as a
standard governance control. In this framework, an AI prediction does not reach the clinician
unless it passes a real-time safety check (e.g., Is the patient already on treatment? Is the
confidence score above threshold?).

• HHS Role: Recognize that providers utilizing "Gated" architectures are exercising due
diligence. This shifts reliance from "gut feeling" to auditable safety protocols.
2) Indemnification & Cryptographic Evidence
Indemnification is difficult because post-event forensic analysis is often impossible. Parties
cannot prove what the AI "saw" or "thought" at the moment of care.
• HHS Role: Encourage the adoption of Cryptographic Evidence Chains. By digitally
signing every validation event (input, prediction, safety check, user action), providers
and vendors create an immutable "Audit Package" that can factually resolve disputes
and support indemnification clauses.
2.4 Question 4: Evaluation Methods, Metrics, and Robustness
For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and post-
deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered evaluation
methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so, which mechanisms
would be most impactful (e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or prize
competitions)?
Validation is not a one-time event; it is a continuous lifecycle. We propose the following
standard methods:
A. Pre-Deployment: "Shadow Mode" Validation
The most effective evaluation method is Shadow Mode, where the AI runs silently alongside
clinical workflows. It captures real-time data and generates predictions without alerting the
clinician.
B. Post-Deployment: The "Auditor Sidecar"
To address "robustness testing”, An "Auditor Sidecar" sits parallel to the AI, continuously
monitoring for "Drift" (statistical changes in input data) and "Degradation" (drops in confidence
or accuracy).
HHS Support Mechanism:
HHS should use Contracts and Cooperative Agreements to fund "Clinical AI Innovation
Sandboxes." These programs would subsidize the cost of third-party validation (Shadow Mode
infrastructure) for participating health systems, allowing them to test AI tools risk-free before
purchase.
2.5 Question 5: Accreditation and Certification
How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, industry-
driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
Certification must be based on local, ongoing evidence, not just static vendor claims. The entity
that evaluates AI models in production cannot be the same as the developers of those
models.

Recommendation: HHS should support an "AI Assurance Evidence Package" standard.
Rather than a one-time seal of approval, certification should require a dynamic Evidence

2) Monitoring Plan: Defined thresholds for drift and safety signal detection.
3) Incident Response Protocol: Pre-agreed pathways for when the model fails.

3 Conclusion
The barriers to effective clinical AI adoption are not primarily technical; they are rooted in
uncertainty. Health care organizations lack clear, scalable mechanisms to verify how AI tools
perform in their specific clinical environments over time. This uncertainty affects regulatory
confidence, liability management, procurement decisions, and ultimately patient trust.
Throughout this response, we have described how an ecosystem of independent, post-
deployment validation and continuous assurance can address these challenges in a manner
that is predictable, proportionate to risk, and equitable across care settings. Approaches such
as enterprise-scale Shadow Mode validation, standardized evaluation evidence, and auditable
safety controls offer HHS practical levers to reduce adoption friction while maintaining strong
patient protections.
By clarifying expectations for ongoing performance verification, supporting independent
assurance mechanisms, and enabling interoperable evaluation evidence, HHS can help shift the
clinical AI market from reliance on static claims to reliance on real-world performance data.
This shift would strengthen public trust, support responsible innovation, and ensure that the
benefits of AI in clinical care are accessible to providers and patients across the U.S. health
system.
