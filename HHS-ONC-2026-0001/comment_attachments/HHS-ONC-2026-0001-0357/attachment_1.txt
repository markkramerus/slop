Question 1: Barriers to Innovation
Summary
• Workforce and capacity: AI expertise is concentrated in large academic medical centers.
Community hospitals, rural facilities, and safety-net providers lack the staff and processes to
evaluate, implement, or monitor AI tools, leaving most of the healthcare market unable to
participate in AI innovation. Public-private technical assistance programs, modeled on ONC's
Regional Extension Centers, could close this gap without expanding government.
• Data infrastructure: Fragmented health data and proprietary EHR platforms prevent AI tools
validated at one institution from working at another. Stronger interoperability standards and cost-
effective pathways for shared computing infrastructure would unlock AI deployment for smaller
providers currently priced out of the market.
• Payment misalignment: Fee-for-service payment cannot capture AI-driven improvements in
outcomes and efficiency, and no reimbursement mechanism covers the cost of adopting AI
responsibly, creating a race to the bottom where organizations that skip rigorous evaluation
deploy faster and cheaper. Value-based care models offer a more natural alignment but remain far
from universal.
• Regulatory fragmentation: Overlapping guidance from FDA, ONC/ASTP, OCR, and CMS
creates compliance costs that large incumbents can absorb but smaller innovators cannot.
Harmonizing requirements through a "OneHHS" approach and making guidance proportionate to
risk would level the playing field for competition.

Barrier 1: Workforce Capacity Gap
Most healthcare delivery organizations (HDOs) lack the people, skills, and organizational processes
needed to adopt AI responsibly. AI expertise remains concentrated in well-resourced academic medical
centers, while community hospitals, federally qualified health centers (FQHCs), rural facilities, and
safety-net providers often have no dedicated staff to evaluate, implement, or monitor AI tools. Without
trained personnel and standardized processes, these organizations cannot determine whether an AI tool is
safe, effective, and fair for their patient populations.
A nationwide analysis of 3,560 US hospitals found that nearly half reported no evaluation of AI model
accuracy (47%) and over half reported no evaluation for bias (51%), with hospitals in medically
underserved areas and health professional shortage areas significantly less likely to have adopted AI at
all.1 Among hospitals within HAIP's community of practice, our survey also found that only 61% conduct
local evaluations for accuracy, and just 44% assess for fairness.2 Interviews across 10 healthcare
organizations revealed no consensus on how to evaluate whether AI performs fairly for all patients.3 The
HEAAL framework, a structured fairness assessment protocol, found that rigorous evaluation requires
investment "beyond reach for most organizations" without external support.4
HAIP has developed freely available best-practice guides covering the full AI lifecycle, organized around
8 key decision points.5 Through its Practice Network, HAIP operates a hub-and-spoke model in which
experienced healthcare delivery organizations in the Corps Network serve as hubs, providing mentorship
on governance, implementation, and workflow design, while organizations new to AI serve as spokes,
receiving structured support to build local capacity. In its inaugural cohort, HAIP paired 5 safety-net
providers with mentors from 15+ organizations, building foundational governance capacity, including AI
use policies, governance committees, and staff training at each site.
There is an opportunity for HHS to support AI technical assistance programs built on public-private
partnerships. Our experience shows that the hub-and-spoke model is effective for disseminating
knowledge, governance best practices, and organizational readiness, but implementation, monitoring, and
ongoing management require technical infrastructure and expertise that go beyond what hub organizations
alone can provide. A sustainable model would pair hub-and-spoke knowledge networks with cloud and
AI technology partners that provide compute infrastructure, technical tooling, and deployment expertise,
while adopting institutions contribute their local clinical context, workflow requirements, and
community-specific needs. Programs such as HRSA's Telehealth Resource Centers and ONC's Regional
Extension Centers demonstrate that this approach works when each partner contributes its strengths.

Barrier 2: Data Infrastructure and Interoperability
Even when organizations have the workforce to adopt AI, many lack the underlying data infrastructure.
AI tools require access to structured, high-quality health data, but healthcare data remains fragmented
across systems, locked behind proprietary platforms, and difficult to share. Smaller providers are
particularly affected: over 20% of office-based practices still lack certified electronic health record (EHR)
technology, the most basic foundation for AI.6
ONC data shows persistent gaps in health IT adoption among smaller practices and safety-net providers.7
The lack of data interoperability means that AI tools validated at one institution often cannot be reliably
deployed at another without significant adaptation, a barrier that disproportionately burdens organizations
with fewer technical resources. Through its Practice Network, HAIP has seen these infrastructure gaps up
close, particularly at safety-net sites where data readiness is often the first barrier to AI adoption, well
before questions of governance or clinical validation arise.
Continued advancement of data interoperability standards through ONC/ASTP, with specific attention to
ensuring AI tools can access and operate on health data across different systems, would meaningfully
reduce this barrier. There is also an opportunity to explore pathways for healthcare organizations to use
protected health information on shared computing infrastructure in a cost-effective, HIPAA-compliant
manner, recognizing that computational costs may be prohibitive for smaller providers operating on thin
margins.
Barrier 3: Misaligned Payment Incentives
There is no clear mechanism to reimburse healthcare organizations for the work required to adopt AI
responsibly. Fee-for-service payment does not reward AI efficiency gains, and there is no financial
support for the substantial effort required to validate AI tools locally and assess their safety and fairness.
This creates a perverse incentive: organizations that skip rigorous evaluation can deploy faster and at
lower cost than those that do it properly.
Organizations that conduct thorough AI evaluation, including local validation, safety review, and fairness
analysis, bear higher costs with no financial return under current payment models.8 Some experts have
proposed value-based and outcome-linked reimbursement approaches, but these models remain
immature.9 Meanwhile, mixed evidence on AI return on investment suggests that current payment

Barrier 4: Regulatory Fragmentation
Healthcare organizations face fragmented AI guidance from multiple HHS agencies, each issuing separate
requirements. External frameworks from NIST, WHO, and state attorneys general add further complexity.
The resulting confusion is especially burdensome for smaller organizations that lack dedicated regulatory
or legal staff to navigate overlapping, sometimes conflicting requirements.
HAIP's research mapped 31 best practices against 8 regulatory frameworks, distilling 58 raw principles
down to 13 synthesized principles and translating them into step-by-step procedures with identified
stakeholders and data sources, revealing substantial redundancy and inconsistency across frameworks.11
State-level actions, including the California Attorney General's inquiry into AI bias and the Colorado AI
Act, add yet another layer of complexity.12 HAIP has maintained ONC as an ecosystem partner since its
founding and has engaged legal and regulatory experts from DLA Piper throughout its framework
development.
Advancing the "OneHHS" approach described in the HHS AI Strategy by harmonizing guidance across
FDA, ONC/ASTP, OCR, and CMS into coordinated, actionable guidance would significantly reduce this
burden. Translating high-level principles into practical, step-by-step implementation guidance would give
organizations a clear path forward. Making compliance requirements proportionate to risk and pairing
them with capacity-building support would help ensure that under-resourced providers are not penalized
for lacking the resources to navigate regulatory complexity.

Question 2: Policy Changes
Summary
• Regulatory harmonization: A "OneHHS" approach that harmonizes requirements across FDA,
ONC/ASTP, OCR, and CMS, preserves market transparency as certification is streamlined,
enforces information-blocking rules to enable independent AI developers to compete on merit,
and modernizes HIPAA to clarify permissible uses of data for AI development would level the
playing field for innovators of all sizes.
• Payment alignment: Value-based programs offer a natural vehicle for incentivizing responsible
AI adoption by tying payment to demonstrated outcomes rather than creating standalone
reimbursement codes. The ACCESS Model's outcome-aligned payments point in this direction,
but broader recognition of AI-enabled interventions across existing CMS programs is needed.
• Capacity-building infrastructure: A national hub-and-spoke network for AI technical
assistance, embedded within existing federal programs such as Rural Health Transformation,
would build capacity within under-resourced organizations without expanding government.
Capacity-building needs to precede compliance mandates to avoid penalizing the organizations
least equipped to comply.
• Agentic AI and autonomous care: AI in healthcare is moving from decision support toward
autonomous clinical action, as illustrated by Utah's AI prescription renewal program and ARPA-
H's ADVOCATE program. Current regulatory, payment, and governance frameworks are not
designed for AI that acts independently. HHS will need to address how to classify and oversee
autonomous clinical AI, how to structure liability, when human oversight is required and when it
is not, and how to reimburse care delivered by AI agents rather than practitioners.

Regulatory Changes
As discussed in Question 1, healthcare organizations face fragmented AI guidance from multiple HHS
agencies, each issuing separate requirements alongside external frameworks from NIST, WHO, and state
attorneys general. Here we focus on specific regulatory changes that would reduce this burden while
preserving the market conditions needed for competition and innovation.
The HTI-1 Final Rule (45 CFR § 170.315(b)(11)) took a meaningful step by requiring certified health IT
developers to disclose source attributes for predictive decision support interventions, enabling users to
assess whether tools are Fair, Appropriate, Valid, Effective, and Safe (the FAVES framework). However,
these transparency requirements apply only to certified health IT, leaving a large and growing category of
clinical AI tools outside their scope, including generative AI applications like ambient scribes that are
deployed across hundreds of health systems without FDA approval and with minimal transparency
requirements. The subsequent withdrawal of remaining HTI-2 proposals and publication of the HTI-5
Proposed Rule in December 2025 signal a welcome shift toward streamlining certification requirements
and reducing compliance burden on health IT developers.13 However, HTI-5's proposal to eliminate
source-attribute disclosure requirements for predictive decision-support interventions risks eroding the
market transparency that enables competition.
HAIP's experience piloting the AI Vendor Disclosure Framework with safety-net hospitals and FQHCs
illustrates why.14 Without structured disclosure, organizations with limited technical staff cannot
independently evaluate vendor claims about model performance, training data, or fairness, and default to
the AI bundled with their EHR, not because it is superior, but because evaluating alternatives requires
expertise they lack. Standardized transparency gives these organizations and independent AI developers a
common basis for comparing products on merit. Removing it does not reduce the burden on healthcare
purchasers; it shifts the advantage toward incumbents. As discussed in our response to the capacity-
building question below, building local capacity to evaluate AI tools is an essential complement to
streamlined certification; without both, competitive markets will not be achieved.
The transparency gap is compounded by a lack of data access. Enforcement of information blocking rules
(45 CFR Part 171) has not kept pace with the extent to which dominant EHR vendors control access to
clinical data and workflow integration points. As we have previously documented, this creates a market
failure: well-validated AI built outside EHR platforms struggles with adoption, while less rigorously
tested AI bundled into EHR systems achieves rapid uptake simply because it is cheaper to deploy.15 A

HIPAA's privacy framework, written well before AI, adds another layer of uncertainty. Healthcare

adoption. Smaller developers and community providers, which cannot afford specialized legal counsel to
navigate these ambiguities, are disproportionately affected. Modernizing HIPAA to clarify permissible
uses of health data for AI development and validation while maintaining patient privacy protections
would reduce regulatory uncertainty and enable broader participation in AI innovation.
A "OneHHS" approach to regulatory harmonization, as articulated in the December 2025 HHS AI
Strategy, would address the most immediate barriers. Key priorities include: (1) as the HTI-5 rulemaking
streamlines certification, preserving and extending source attribute transparency requirements to cover all
clinical AI tools that substantively influence care decisions, building on the HTI-1 FAVES framework so
that a modernized program retains the market transparency needed for fair competition; (2) strengthening
enforcement of information blocking rules (45 CFR Part 171) to ensure that EHR vendors cannot use data
control to disadvantage independent AI developers, leveling the playing field for market competition and
innovation; and (3) translating high-level principles into practical, step-by-step guidance proportionate to
risk, so that compliance is achievable for organizations of all sizes rather than only those with the
resources to hire dedicated regulatory teams. Relevant regulations include 45 CFR Parts 164, 170, and
171, and 21 CFR Parts 800–898.
Payment Policy Changes
Clinical AI spans direct patient care (e.g., sepsis prediction, diagnostic imaging), operations (ambient
scribes, care coordination), and administration (coding, prior authorization), and its value shows up
differently in each: cost savings in some cases, provider well-being in others, improved quality in still
others. Fee-for-service reimbursement was not designed for any of these, and there is no straightforward
billing mechanism for an ambient scribe that reduces burnout or a care coordination tool that avoids
downstream utilization. Many of AI's strongest applications, including prevention, risk stratification, care
coordination, and early intervention, are systematically under-supported in fee-for-service payment,
which rewards volume rather than the proactive, longitudinal care that AI enables.
Value-based and capitated payment models create stronger alignment with AI's strengths, but current
programs do not yet explicitly recognize AI-enabled interventions as qualifying activities. Meanwhile,
with shrinking margins, health systems rationally minimize adoption costs by skipping validation and
monitoring altogether, or defaulting to whatever AI their EHR vendor bundles in. The result is
widespread adoption of poorly governed AI, reinforcing the vendor lock-in and market concentration
problems discussed in the regulatory section above.

Programmatic Design Changes
The most immediate barrier to AI adoption is capacity. Most healthcare organizations, particularly those
in rural and underserved communities, lack the technical personnel, data infrastructure, and organizational
processes to evaluate, implement, and govern AI tools. Community demand for AI is already emerging:
patients and families are asking for AI-powered tools like multilingual virtual assistants that provide
support and connect them to community resources, but most organizations lack the capacity to deploy
such tools responsibly. Because AI implementation and health policy operate primarily at the state level,
federal infrastructure for AI capacity-building, embedded within existing programs like Rural Health
Transformation, would address this gap most effectively.
The Rural Health Transformation Program offers a natural venue for this work. Its digital modernization
investments are already creating state-level structures, including technical assistance centers, regional
hubs, and innovation funds, that require AI governance expertise to function effectively.17 HAIP and
DIHI have direct experience building this kind of capacity. As described in Question 1, HAIP's Practice
Network hub-and-spoke model has built foundational governance at safety-net sites, and its inaugural
cohort launched AI tool pilots at 3 of 5 sites within 12 months.
Key elements of this infrastructure include: (1) a national hub-and-spoke network for AI technical
assistance, with centers of excellence providing governance frameworks, evaluation standards, and
mentorship to regional and state-level partners; (2) embedding AI readiness criteria within existing
federal programs (Rural Health Transformation, Telehealth Resource Centers, AHRQ collaboratives) so
that capacity-building is integrated into digital modernization investments rather than siloed; (3) funding
research on AI applications that address community health needs, including patient-facing tools designed
for the populations that community health organizations serve; and (4) building foundational AI capacity,

Looking Ahead: Policy Frameworks for Autonomous and Agentic AI
The regulatory, payment, and capacity challenges discussed above assume AI systems that assist or
augment human clinicians. But AI in healthcare is already moving toward autonomous clinical action.
Utah's AI regulatory sandbox recently approved the first program allowing an AI system to independently
renew prescription medications for chronic conditions, with no physician in the decision loop for routine
renewals.18 ARPA-H's ADVOCATE program is developing agentic AI for cardiovascular care with a 39-
month timeline to FDA authorization.19 Combined with the ACCESS Model's outcome-aligned payments
and the FDA's TEMPO enforcement discretion pilot, these developments point toward a near-term future
in which AI systems act as independent clinical agents, not just decision support tools. The policy
framework is not keeping pace, and HHS will need to address this across all three dimensions: regulatory,
payment, and programmatic.
On the regulatory side, a threshold question is how to classify autonomous clinical AI. The current FDA
framework regulates AI as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), which assumes a human clinician
interpreting or acting on the AI's output. When the AI itself makes the clinical decision, that framework
breaks down. The Healthy Technology Act (H.R. 238) takes the opposite approach, proposing that AI
systems qualify as practitioners authorized to prescribe medications.20 These are fundamentally different
regulatory models with different implications for oversight, accountability, and market structure. Closely
related is the question of human oversight. Not all clinical decisions carry the same risk, and a one-size-
fits-all requirement for physician-in-the-loop would prevent AI from reaching the settings where it is most
needed, particularly underserved communities with severe clinician shortages.
Liability is equally unresolved. The medical malpractice framework assumes a physician-patient
relationship. When an autonomous AI system makes a clinical error, it is unclear whether liability falls on
the developer, the deploying institution, the supervising physician, or some combination. Utah's approach
places liability on the AI company (Doctronic carries malpractice insurance covering the AI system), but
this is an ad hoc state-level solution. Without a federal framework, liability rules will develop
inconsistently across states, creating the same kind of regulatory fragmentation that already burdens the
healthcare AI market.
On payment, the existing reimbursement system is built around paying practitioners for services. CPT
codes assume a human clinician performing or supervising work. The sole precedent for autonomous AI
reimbursement is CPT 92229, created for an autonomous diabetic retinopathy detection system that

Question 3: Legal and Implementation Issues (Non-Medical Devices)
Summary
• Liability framework misaligned with AI reality: Standard vendor contracts shift risk entirely to
providers through limitation-of-liability and hold-harmless provisions, creating an uneven playing
field that disadvantages smaller innovators and under-resourced health systems. AI compounds
the problem by making it harder to identify when and how harm occurs. Model contract language
tied to CMS participation requirements, combined with AI incident reporting, would level the
playing field without requiring new statutory authority.
• Non-device AI lacks meaningful oversight: The CDS exclusion framework was designed
before generative AI and assumes clinicians actively verify AI outputs. The 2026 FDA CDS
guidance made important advances, but ambiguity remains for AI that avoids the CDS label
entirely or whose outputs cannot be meaningfully reviewed. Automation bias already undermines
the premise that human-in-the-loop oversight is sufficient, and that premise breaks down entirely
for agentic AI that acts without human intermediation. Classification based on clinical function,
and actual clinical workflow impact rather than manufacturer labeling would give developers the
predictability they need to invest and compete.
• Governance structures not designed for AI: Non-device AI falls between existing institutional
governance structures, and every organization currently builds AI governance from scratch. This
costly duplication slows adoption, burdens health systems that want to deploy AI responsibly, and
creates unpredictable procurement environments for vendors. As autonomous AI enters clinical
care, governance needs will only intensify. There is an opportunity for HHS to support the
development of standardized governance models that reduce this burden on individual
organizations while giving vendors more predictable markets.

Liability Framework Misaligned with AI Reality
The current liability landscape for clinical AI is uncertain and unevenly distributed. Vendor contracts
routinely include limitation-of-liability and hold-harmless provisions that shift risk entirely to providers.
Large incumbent vendors have the legal resources to insulate themselves, while smaller innovators and
under-resourced health systems absorb liability by default. AI adds a further complication: when AI
systems contribute to adverse outcomes, the complexity of these tools can make it difficult to determine
that harm has occurred, who is responsible, and what went wrong.23 This uncertainty discourages both
developers and health systems from pursuing responsible AI adoption.
Because liability is primarily a matter of state law, HHS's direct role is limited. However, there are
practical steps within existing authorities that would reduce uncertainty and level the playing field.
Developing model contract language for AI procurement, tied to CMS conditions of participation or
government procurement requirements, would help ensure fair risk allocation and particularly benefit
smaller AI developers and community health systems that lack resources to negotiate bespoke
agreements. Establishing AI incident reporting requirements would help illuminate when and how AI-
related harms occur. Recognizing organizations that follow established AI governance frameworks
through existing quality program incentives, such as the CMS Quality Payment Program, would
encourage responsible adoption without requiring new statutory authority.
Looking further ahead, as AI takes on more autonomous clinical roles, a federal compensation program
modeled on the National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program (VICP) could provide a mechanism for
compensating patients harmed by AI while reducing liability uncertainty. This would require legislation,
but it is within HHS's policy domain and worth advancing as AI's clinical role expands.
Non-Device AI Lacks Meaningful Oversight
The CDS exclusion framework was a sound deregulatory step, and FDA's updated CDS guidance
(January 2026) made further progress by introducing a "meaningful independent review" standard and
clarifying that generative AI outputs may fall outside the non-device exclusion.24 However, regulatory
ambiguity remains and creates real costs for innovation. Developers, particularly startups, cannot easily
predict whether their products will be classified as devices, making it difficult to plan development
timelines, raise capital, or bring products to market efficiently.
Some areas remain unclear. The enforcement discretion policy does not cover time-critical scenarios or
AI systems whose basis cannot be meaningfully reviewed, precisely where generative AI raises the
greatest concern. The framework still relies on manufacturers' stated "intended use" to determine
classification, allowing companies to position clinically influential products outside the CDS framework
entirely. AI systems that avoid the CDS label altogether while still influencing clinical decisions through
workflow integration are not addressed. This ambiguity disadvantages responsible developers who seek
clarity while allowing less scrupulous competitors to exploit gray areas.

More fundamentally, the entire oversight architecture for non-device clinical AI rests on the premise that
a clinician independently reviews AI outputs before they affect patient care. Automation bias already

meaningful independent review may be impossible even when clinicians attempt it. As AI moves toward
agentic and autonomous clinical roles, as discussed in Question 2, there is no human review step to rely
on at all. Human-in-the-loop oversight remains important, but it cannot serve as the sole regulatory
strategy. Additional safeguards, including outcome-based monitoring, automated safety checks, and
override capabilities, are needed to complement human oversight where it exists and substitute for it
where it does not.
Governance Structures Not Designed for AI
Non-device AI falls between existing HDO governance structures in ways that create real barriers to
responsible adoption. Medical device committees oversee FDA-regulated products but have no
jurisdiction over non-device AI. IT governance manages technology procurement and security but
typically lacks the clinical expertise to evaluate AI's impact on care decisions. Clinical practice
committees set treatment protocols, but AI represents a novel technology category without established
clinical standards. Compliance and risk management offices handle regulatory obligations, but there is no
clear regulatory framework for non-device clinical AI to comply with. The result is that AI governance
responsibility is fragmented across multiple internal committees and external stakeholders, including
vendors, integrators, and clinicians, each holding partial jurisdiction with none having complete oversight
or clear accountability.
This fragmentation means every HDO must create new governance structures from scratch, a costly
duplication of effort with no standardized models to follow. HAIP's research across 10 HDOs found no
consensus on how to govern AI, with organizations independently "figuring it out" through trial and
error.25 The development of HEAAL demonstrated the substantial investment in personnel, data
infrastructure, and organizational capacity that rigorous AI governance demands, an investment that
stretches the budgets of even well-resourced organizations.26 Separate research on algorithm deployment
found that regulatory affairs involvement is often ad hoc rather than systematic, brought in too late to
shape implementation decisions.27 The burden of this piecemeal approach falls hardest on under-
resourced health systems, including rural hospitals, safety-net providers, and community health centers,
which face the same governance challenges as large academic medical centers but with a fraction of the

role in care decisions, would reduce liability exposure and improve post-deployment monitoring. These
are the kinds of enabling conditions that encourage responsible AI adoption without expanding
government mandates.

roles, as discussed in Question 2, governance will need to address fundamentally new questions: how to
establish accountability when there is no human in the decision loop, how to monitor and override
autonomous systems, how to document AI-initiated clinical actions, and how to ensure that governance
structures keep pace with rapidly advancing capabilities. The governance capacity that HAIP's Key
Decision Points framework and Practice Network represent becomes more critical, not less, as AI
autonomy increases. HHS can accelerate this work by supporting the development of next-generation
governance frameworks through the same mechanisms described throughout this response: technical
assistance networks, model governance structures, and capacity-building at under-resourced
organizations. As emphasized throughout, capacity-building needs to come before compliance mandates.

Pre-Deployment Local Validation
Vendor-reported performance metrics may not reflect the value and risks an AI solution presents within a
given organization's own patient population and clinical context. Each organization needs to assess these
locally, yet most lack the capacity to do so rigorously. National survey data shows fewer than a third of
hospitals evaluate AI accuracy across all deployed models, and nearly half have no accuracy evaluation
process at all.28 Rigorous evaluation requires significant investment in personnel, capabilities, and data
infrastructure that is beyond reach for most organizations, even well-resourced ones.2930 No standardized
procedures exist for what local validation entails. Some vendors have begun providing open-source
validation tools, such as Epic's Seismometer, which HAIP sites have tested.31 However, effective use of
these tools still requires underlying technical expertise and dedicated teams to implement, customize, and
interpret results accurately. The lack of standardization and capacity underscores the need for shared
networks where organizations serving similar patient populations can pool knowledge and resources for
validation.
HAIP's HEAAL framework offers step-by-step guidance for evaluating AI tools across five domains:
Reliability and Validity, Fairness, Fitness for Purpose, Transparency, and Accountability. With 37
procedures for existing AI and 34 for new AI development, the framework provides the kind of
structured, practical evaluation protocol that individual organizations otherwise have to create from
scratch, and it has been adopted by organizations across the health system.32 HAIP's best practice guides
provide structured evaluation protocols with identified stakeholders and data sources for each stage of the
AI lifecycle. These tools can serve as the basis for standardized local validation protocols, and
aggregating best practices and use cases across organizations can help inform these standards over time.
Grants to build technical and data infrastructure, train the workforce, and support dedicated evaluation
staff would directly address the capacity barrier. Cooperative agreements that enable organizations to
share lessons learned and validation approaches would strengthen system-wide capacity, allowing under-
resourced organizations to benefit from the experience of those further along. This is the kind of
investment that accelerates responsible adoption by making evaluation feasible rather than mandating it
before organizations have the means to comply.

production-quality deployment. Organizations that cannot systematically track how AI tools perform over
time cannot identify when performance degrades, when patient populations shift, or when a tool is no

Vendor Transparency and Disclosure Standards
Information asymmetry between AI vendors and HDOs remains a significant barrier to responsible
adoption and healthy market competition. National data reveals that most hospitals lack the capacity to
evaluate vendor AI claims, with fewer than a quarter assessing AI fairness across patient populations.45
Vendors, meanwhile, face unpredictable, ad hoc disclosure requests from each prospective customer.
Standardized disclosure frameworks address both problems: they level the playing field so that under-
resourced organizations can apply the same evaluation criteria as academic medical centers, and they give
vendors, particularly smaller innovators who may lack the brand recognition of larger incumbents, clear
and predictable expectations that reward product quality over marketing resources.
HAIP developed the AI Vendor Disclosure Framework, a community-informed procurement tool that
structures vendor evaluation across key dimensions of system performance, data governance, and
integration requirements. DIHI researchers created the Model Facts Label in 2020, the first AI product
label in healthcare, which was cited in the HTI-1 Final Rule. HTI-1 requires disclosure of source
attributes for predictive decision support interventions, a requirement that HAIP helped shape through its
ongoing engagement with ASTP/ONC. The proposed HTI-5 rule would further refine these transparency
requirements, and continued alignment between federal rules and industry-developed standards will be
important as the regulatory landscape evolves.
Aligning federal requirements with industry-developed standards rather than creating parallel systems
would reduce regulatory duplication and extend the reach of frameworks that have already demonstrated
effectiveness. For example, referencing private sector disclosure frameworks in ASTP/ONC guidance or
CMS conditions of participation would encourage adoption through existing federal programs without
imposing additional regulatory burden. There is also a need for technical assistance that helps
organizations, particularly smaller ones, understand and apply these disclosure requirements, since a
standard is only useful if organizations have the capacity to use it. More broadly, ensuring that third-party
AI vendors have fair access to health IT integration interfaces, without overly restrictive contractual
obligations from incumbent EHR firms, would support the kind of competitive market that disclosure
standards are designed to enable.

Question 6: AI Performance and Promising Tools
Summary
• Evidence has not kept pace with adoption: Over 1,250 FDA-authorized AI devices are on the
market and the ambient AI scribe category alone generated $600 million in 2025, yet rigorous
evidence on clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness remains sparse. Most research evaluates
algorithm accuracy rather than clinical impact, making it difficult for organizations to make
informed purchasing decisions and for genuinely superior products to differentiate themselves in
a competitive market.
• Operational AI is showing the clearest early returns: AI tools for revenue cycle management,
prior authorization, and patient access are demonstrating consistent efficiency gains. FQHCs have
found AI-powered call centers particularly valuable given staffing shortages. Ambient AI scribes
show strong evidence of burnout reduction, though business models increasingly center on
revenue capture, and direct clinical-facing tools continue to emerge with uncertain return on
investment.
• Fairness, generalizability, and workforce concerns: Nearly half of hospitals report no AI
accuracy evaluation, and tools validated at academic medical centers may not perform
equivalently elsewhere. Separately, significant efficiency gains from administrative AI have led

The Evidence Gap
The private sector has moved fast on clinical AI. Over 1,250 FDA-authorized AI/ML-enabled medical
devices are on the market as of mid-2025,51 and the ambient AI scribe category alone generated $600
million in 2025 with 2.4x year-over-year growth.52 Yet rigorous evidence on whether these tools actually
improve patient outcomes or reduce costs has not kept pace. Most published AI research evaluates
algorithm accuracy rather than measuring what matters to health systems, clinicians, and patients: clinical
outcomes, workflow impact, and return on investment.53 Randomized controlled trials remain limited, and
most evidence is retrospective, drawn from single academic medical center sites.54 A national analysis of
3,560 U.S. hospitals found that nearly half reported no evaluation of AI model accuracy and more than
half reported no assessment for bias, pointing to widespread gaps in the evidence organizations need to
make informed decisions.55
This evidence gap creates a market problem. Without reliable performance data, healthcare delivery
organizations (HDOs) cannot make informed purchasing decisions, and vendors developing genuinely
superior products struggle to differentiate themselves from competitors making unsubstantiated claims.
The organizations most affected are those with the fewest resources to conduct their own evaluations,
which means the market's information asymmetry falls hardest on safety-net providers and community
hospitals.
Where Clinical AI Is Delivering Value
AI tools deployed in clinical care extend well beyond direct patient-facing applications. Administrative
and operational AI, particularly tools for revenue cycle management, prior authorization, claims
processing, and patient access, is showing some of the most consistent early returns. These use cases
carry lower clinical risk, lend themselves to clearer performance metrics, and address pressing workforce
constraints. FQHCs in HAIP's Practice Network have reported that AI-powered call center tools are
particularly valuable given chronic staffing shortages, allowing smaller organizations to maintain patient
access at levels that would otherwise require additional hires they cannot afford. The efficiency gains in
these operational areas are real, though they raise a separate set of questions about workforce impact
discussed below.
Among clinical applications, ambient AI scribes represent healthcare AI's first breakout success, and the
evidence base is now substantial. A three-arm randomized controlled trial of 238 physicians across 14
specialties found approximately 7% improvement in burnout scores, reduced cognitive workload, and a

The novel AI tools with the greatest potential to improve outcomes and reduce costs are not necessarily

just governance and workflow design. HAIP's Practice Network provides a demonstrated approach, and
federal funding through grants and cooperative agreements could enable national scale.

AI products on a common basis through consistent disclosure of training data, performance metrics,
fairness evaluations, and integration requirements, the market rewards quality and innovation rather than
marketing spend. This particularly benefits smaller AI developers who build better products but lack the

Who Influences AI Adoption
AI adoption in healthcare delivery organizations (HDOs) requires coordination across executive
leadership, but no single role owns the decision, and that absence of clear authority is itself one of the
primary administrative hurdles slowing private sector innovation. The CEO or COO sets strategic
priorities and allocates resources. The CMO or CMIO evaluates clinical appropriateness and physician
adoption. The CIO or CTO manages technical integration and infrastructure. The CNO determines
nursing workflow impact. The CFO evaluates return on investment. Each brings legitimate concerns, yet
in most organizations there is no established process for reconciling them into a coherent AI adoption
decision.73 HAIP's research, based on 89 interviews across its member organizations, found no clear
ownership of AI lifecycle management activities, including decisions about whether to implement or
decommission AI tools. Some organizations give that authority to budget holders; others favor centralized
governance committees. Organizations with clear, centralized decision processes and an executive
sponsor tend to move faster and more consistently, while those where authority is diffuse often stall or
produce contradictory decisions across departments.
The emergence of dedicated AI leadership roles signals that some health systems recognize this gap.
Cleveland Clinic appointed its first Chief AI Officer in 2024, and other organizations including HCA
Healthcare and NewYork-Presbyterian have created dedicated AI leadership positions to consolidate
strategy and governance. But these appointments remain the exception. Most organizations distribute AI
responsibilities across existing roles, creating coordination overhead that slows adoption and increases the
risk of inconsistent decisions.
However, executive leadership alone does not determine whether AI is adopted in practice. Frontline
clinicians and their immediate supervisors are, in many respects, the most important influence on whether
AI tools are actually used in clinical care. Leadership can set strategy, allocate resources, and build
governance structures, but adoption succeeds or fails at the point of care. Clinician resistance driven by
workflow disruption, alert fatigue, lack of trust, or insufficient training can render even well-governed AI
tools ineffective.74 Constructing incentive structures that drive frontline buy-in, whether through
leadership engagement, peer influence, performance and career development, financial incentives, or
awareness of patient impact, is an underappreciated challenge that organizational governance structures
alone cannot solve. HAIP's Practice Network experience reinforces this: even with governance support
and leadership commitment, the organizations seeing the strongest adoption are those that invest in
clinician engagement and workflow co-design from the outset.

distributed across three levels: health center leadership and boards, who are responsible for clinical
quality and fiduciary oversight but often lack the technical expertise to evaluate AI solutions; Health
Center Controlled Networks (HCCNs), designated by HRSA to provide shared services and
infrastructure, which function as de facto system-level decision makers for technology adoption; and

Primary Administrative Hurdles
The most pervasive administrative hurdle is that organizations are deploying AI while simultaneously
trying to build governance structures for it. HAIP's research found that across multiple HDOs, clinical
committees, IT departments, and compliance offices each held partial jurisdiction over AI, but none had
complete oversight, and there was no consensus on how governance processes should work.75 A
systematic review of 35 AI governance frameworks confirmed that most existing frameworks were not
designed for real-world application in complex healthcare environments.76 The problem is compounded
by the pace of change: AI capabilities are evolving so rapidly that static governance frameworks risk
becoming outdated before they are fully implemented, requiring governance approaches that are adaptive
by design rather than fixed.
The barriers fall into three reinforcing categories. First, process barriers: organizations lack standardized
guidance on what to request from AI vendors, vendor responses are inconsistent, and there are no
common procurement criteria. Without standardized procurement criteria, many HDOs default to the AI
embedded in their existing EHR platform, not because it outperforms alternatives, but because the effort
of evaluating independent products exceeds their available resources. This dynamic favors incumbent
vendors over innovative startups developing better tools. Unclear regulation adds friction: liability
allocation between vendors and HDOs remains unresolved, data use agreements are complex and
variable, intellectual property disputes are common, and the absence of standard contract language for AI
procurement means every deal is negotiated from scratch.
Second, people barriers: most HDOs lack dedicated AI governance personnel. IT and clinical staff are
stretched thin across existing responsibilities, and the specialized expertise needed to evaluate AI tools,
including algorithm performance, fairness assessment, and workflow integration, is in short supply. This
expertise gap affects every stage of the AI lifecycle, from procurement through monitoring and
decommissioning.
Third, resource barriers: budgets at most organizations cannot absorb the overhead of building
governance infrastructure from scratch, particularly when the return on investment for governance itself is
difficult to quantify. These costs are real but invisible: staff time diverted from clinical work, consultant
fees for legal and technical review, and the opportunity cost of delayed adoption while governance
structures mature.

judgment. They take a lifecycle approach, governing AI from pre-deployment evaluation through ongoing
monitoring and potential decommissioning. And they calibrate oversight to the level of risk, giving
higher-risk clinical AI more intensive scrutiny while allowing lower-risk administrative tools to move
through streamlined pathways.

staff time and produce inconsistent results. Organizations without lifecycle governance deploy tools and
then discover performance problems only when harm has occurred. And organizations without tiered risk
frameworks apply the same heavy process to low-risk scheduling tools and high-risk clinical decision
support, creating unnecessary burden that slows adoption of both. Governance that is proportionate to
organizational capacity and AI risk, with lighter-touch approaches for smaller or lower-risk settings, is
more likely to be adopted and sustained.

Data Standards That Enable Market Competition
The most impactful step toward widening market opportunities for clinical AI is to ensure that health data
flows freely and in standardized formats across systems. Today, dominant EHR vendors control access to
clinical data and workflow integration points, creating a structural advantage for AI tools bundled into
their platforms, regardless of whether those tools are the best available. As discussed in our response to
Question 2, enforcement of information blocking rules (45 CFR Part 171) has not kept pace with how this
data control restricts competition. Independent AI developers, including startups building genuinely
superior products, struggle to access the data they need to validate and deploy their tools in clinical
settings. Enhanced interoperability would break this bottleneck, enabling a competitive market where AI
tools win on performance rather than distribution.
FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources), HL7's modern API-based standard, provides the
technical foundation for this vision and is the appropriate primary standard for AI-related data exchange.
The United States Core Data for Interoperability (USCDI) establishes which data elements flow through
FHIR-based APIs. ONC/ASTP has continued advancing USCDI adoption, and the HTI-5 Proposed Rule
calls for USCDI version 3.1, which would expand the data classes and elements available through
standardized APIs.83 Future USCDI versions present an opportunity to prioritize the data elements most
critical for AI development, including domains not yet well represented. The Medication Administration
Record (MAR), for example, is particularly relevant for inpatient AI models but is not currently required
as a standardized data element, limiting the ability of AI developers to build and validate tools across
institutions.84 Standardized data also enables what is currently difficult to achieve: fair comparative
analysis of AI solutions across vendors, giving payers, providers, and patients the information they need
to evaluate products on merit and driving quality-based competition across the market.
Beyond data access, there are contractual barriers that limit market competition for AI. Some EHR
vendors impose requirements that third-party AI vendors seek approval before hiring employees who
have had access to the EHR firm's proprietary information within the past 12 months. These restrictions
limit independent AI developers' ability to recruit the technical expertise needed to build effective
integrations, further entrenching the incumbents' advantage. Strengthening information blocking
protections to address these contractual practices would help ensure that market competition reflects
product quality rather than vendor lock-in.

data flows needed to develop, validate, and monitor clinical AI across diverse patient populations and

safety-net providers, rural facilities, and FQHCs whose patients are underrepresented in existing datasets.

organizations to participate in benchmarking without the infrastructure burden of large-scale data sharing,
while generating evidence on how AI performs in the settings where it is needed most.

Question 9: Patient and Caregiver Perspectives
Summary
• Access and administrative burden: Patients consistently identify access to care as the challenge
they most want AI to address, with 30% of US adults citing improved access as AI's top expected
benefit. Patients also feel the downstream effects of clinician burnout and administrative burden.
In an AMA survey, 57% of physicians identified administrative burden automation as AI's
biggest opportunity. AI tools that free clinician time for patient interaction, reduce wait times, and
extend care to underserved areas align with patient priorities.
• Trust and human oversight: Patient acceptance depends on maintaining the human relationship.
Only about 20% of US adults expect AI to improve their relationship with their doctor. Patients
want transparency about how AI is used in their care, how their data is handled, and whether AI

What Patients Want AI to Fix
Patients and caregivers consistently identify access to care as the challenge they most want AI to address.
Approximately 30% of US adults expect AI to improve their access to healthcare, the highest-rated
expected benefit in recent surveys, with patients in underserved communities particularly prioritizing
solutions that reduce wait times, enable remote monitoring, and extend specialty care to areas where it is
currently unavailable.87 AI also holds promise for better management of chronic disease through
personalized health management systems. Approximately half of chronic disease patients view AI-
enabled monitoring technologies as beneficial for their ongoing care.88
Patients also feel the downstream effects of the administrative burden that consumes clinician time. In the
AMA's 2024 physician survey, 57% of physicians identified administrative burden automation as AI's
biggest opportunity, 75% said AI helps work efficiency, and 54% said it helps reduce burnout.89 These are
not just provider concerns: when physicians spend more time on documentation than on patient
interaction, patients experience the consequences through shorter visits, delays in test results and follow-
up communications, and fragmented coordination between providers. AI applications like ambient
documentation tools directly target this problem by freeing clinician time for the patient relationship.
There is also emerging potential for large language models to provide patients with basic information
about test results directly, improving timely access to their own health information.
Beyond access and administrative burden, patients value AI's potential to improve diagnostic accuracy
and timeliness, detecting conditions earlier through imaging analysis, reducing diagnostic errors, and
providing second opinions on complex cases. At the same time, AI detection of subclinical disease that
clinicians cannot yet correlate or mitigate raises questions about how to communicate such findings
responsibly. Cost and affordability also matter: roughly 19% of US adults expect AI to make healthcare
more affordable, though evidence on whether current AI tools actually reduce costs for patients remains
limited.90

Patients as Partners, Not Just Recipients
A consistent finding across the research is that patient perspectives are underrepresented in AI
development and governance.99100 Patients prioritize being engaged from the beginning, at problem
identification and prioritization stages, not consulted after tools have already been built. This gap matters
because patient and provider priorities differ: patients focus on barriers to accessing care, while providers
tend to focus on efficiency and workflow. Research on what patients actually want from AI remains
scarce despite rapid adoption, and without systematic patient input, the market for clinical AI risks
optimizing for provider and vendor priorities rather than the patient outcomes that should ultimately drive
value.
HAIP has built patient engagement into its governance structure from the outset. A community
representative serves on HAIP's founding leadership team, bringing connections to grassroots coalitions
including the Comunidad de Durham network that engage communities on AI and health advocacy. The
HEAAL framework explicitly identifies patient and community stakeholders for each of its 37 assessment
procedures, making patient involvement a structural feature of AI evaluation rather than an
afterthought.101 The Practice Network extends this commitment by working with FQHCs and community
hospitals that serve populations whose voices are least represented in AI development.
There are several opportunities for HHS to strengthen patient engagement in AI through existing
authorities. AHRQ could fund research on patient priorities for AI across diverse communities, building
the evidence base that the field currently lacks. CMS could incorporate patient experience measures into
AI-related quality programs, ensuring that patient outcomes, not just technical performance, drive
adoption incentives. And ONC/ASTP could include patient-facing transparency requirements in future
rulemaking, giving patients the information they need to make informed decisions about AI in their care.

Question 10: Research Priorities
Summary
• Implementation science, not algorithm development: Despite thousands of AI models
validated in research settings, translation into clinical practice has been remarkably slow. The
evidence base is strongest for diagnostic accuracy and workflow efficiency, but evidence on
whether AI actually improves patient health outcomes, reduces costs, or narrows disparities
remains sparse. Federal research funding continues to favor algorithm development over the
implementation science that would close this gap.
• Costs, benefits, and value: Even for ambient AI, the most mature generative AI application, the
Peterson Health Technology Institute found that evidence on financial performance is uncertain.
Implementation costs are consistently understudied. Who captures the value from AI efficiency
gains is poorly understood, and the return on investment extends beyond financial metrics to
include clinician well-being and workforce retention.
• Priority areas: HHS is uniquely positioned to fund implementation science research, close the
capacity gap between well-resourced and safety-net providers, support AI tools that expand the
healthcare workforce, invest in shared evidence infrastructure, and develop research methods for
emerging applications like agentic AI systems.

Published Evidence: What Exists and What Is Missing
Published evidence on AI's clinical impact has grown substantially, though critical gaps remain. The
strongest evidence exists for diagnostic accuracy, where controlled studies show AI often rivals clinicians
in tasks like medical image interpretation, and for workflow efficiency, where ambient AI documentation
tools have demonstrated clinician time savings.102 The Peterson Health Technology Institute's evaluation
of major health systems found that ambient scribes likely reduce clinician burnout, though financial
impact remains unclear.103 Approximately half of chronic disease patients view AI-enabled monitoring
technologies as beneficial for their ongoing care, and there is growing evidence that AI can improve
chronic disease management through personalized health management systems.104
However, this evidence comes disproportionately from academic medical centers and does not generalize
well to safety-net settings where data quality, patient populations, and technical infrastructure differ
significantly. Evidence on clinical outcomes, whether AI actually improves patient health, remains sparse,
with few randomized controlled trials measuring patient outcomes rather than algorithm performance
metrics. A 2024 study of AI randomized controlled trials found that only about 15% explicitly cited the
CONSORT-AI reporting guidelines, and much of the available data has been acquired retrospectively,
making it difficult to draw causal conclusions about AI's impact on care delivery.105106 Evidence on
downstream effects of workflow tools, whether time saved translates into more patient interaction,
reduced burnout, or improved throughput, also remains limited. Most published research evaluates
algorithm accuracy metrics rather than the outcomes that matter to patients, providers, and the healthcare
system: improved health, reduced cost, better access, and enhanced clinician well-being.
Costs, Benefits, and Who Captures Value
The literature on AI costs and benefits remains immature. Even for ambient AI, the PHTI evaluation
found that evidence on productivity and financial performance is uncertain, with health system leaders
reporting gaps in evidence regarding impact on the number of patients providers can see or the accuracy
of billing codes.107 Implementation costs are consistently understudied: the substantial resources required
for local validation, governance, workforce training, and ongoing monitoring are rarely quantified, yet
these costs often determine whether an AI tool is feasible for a given organization. Initial implementation
costs remain particularly prohibitive for safety-net settings, where the return on investment must be
demonstrated before budgets can absorb the upfront burden.108

A key question the literature has not adequately addressed is how the value from AI efficiency gains is
distributed across stakeholders, including patients, clinicians, health systems, and payers. There is
substantial anecdotal evidence about the potential for AI to create value across the care delivery system,
but very little rigorous evidence on how that value is captured by different stakeholders. One area where
the dynamics are becoming clearer is ambient AI scribes, where a recent policy brief documented how
vendor marketing is shifting from burnout reduction to revenue-cycle performance, with business models
increasingly focused on more intensive coding rather than cost reduction for patients.109 This raises
questions about whether the current market is directing AI innovation toward genuine value creation.
The return on investment for AI also extends beyond financial metrics. Decreased clinician burnout,
improved staff retention, and enhanced workforce satisfaction are real benefits that take time to
materialize and are rarely measured in current cost-benefit analyses, yet they may ultimately prove more
important to sustainable AI adoption than short-term productivity gains. At the same time, cost reduction
through AI-driven automation of functions like call centers and revenue cycle management raises
legitimate concerns about workforce displacement, particularly in rural communities where healthcare
organizations are often a primary employer.
Priority Research Areas
The most critical gap in the AI evidence base is not algorithm development but implementation science.
The field has shifted from internal validation to external validation and deployment, yet federal research
funding continues to favor algorithm development.110 HHS is uniquely positioned to correct this
imbalance, and doing so would accelerate the private sector's ability to bring effective AI tools to patients.
First, implementation science research through AHRQ, NIH, and CMS would address the most urgent
gap, studying how to integrate AI into clinical workflows, manage organizational change, and sustain
adoption across diverse care settings. Pragmatic multicenter trials that evaluate AI in real-world
conditions, rather than controlled research environments, would generate the evidence that healthcare
delivery organizations need to make informed adoption decisions. This includes research on effective AI
governance structures for organizations of different sizes and resources, ongoing monitoring approaches
for detecting performance drift and emerging risks, decommissioning criteria for when and how
underperforming AI tools should be retired, and methods for assessing the behavioral changes AI
introduces in clinical decision-making.
Second, research on closing the capacity gap between well-resourced health systems and safety-net
providers would help ensure that AI benefits reach all patients. Studies on effective technical assistance
models, including hub-and-spoke approaches where resource-rich institutions support community
providers, would generate evidence on how to scale AI adoption beyond the academic medical centers
that currently dominate the field. Research on AI readiness, particularly connectivity and infrastructure

organizations while producing evidence that reflects the diversity of care delivery in the United States.
Fifth, emerging AI capabilities require new research methods. Agentic AI systems that can take
autonomous actions in clinical workflows, rather than simply providing recommendations, present novel
questions about impact assessment, downstream propagation of errors, and appropriate governance.
Research on systematic methods for evaluating these more autonomous AI applications, before they reach
widespread clinical deployment, would help the field stay ahead of the technology curve rather than
reacting after problems emerge.
