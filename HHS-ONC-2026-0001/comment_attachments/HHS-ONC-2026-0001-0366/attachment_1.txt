Dear Assistant Secretary Keane,
On behalf of OCHIN, thank you for the opportunity to comment on the Department of Health and
Human Services’ (HHS) Request for Information on “Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial
Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care.” Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to support earlier
intervention, improve chronic disease management, and increase efficiency across care delivery.
Achieving these outcomes at scale, however, requires that AI adoption be affordable, trusted, and
grounded in evidence generated through routine clinical care, particularly among rural and low-
resourced providers.
For rural and low‑resourced providers, affordability is inseparable from sustainable infrastructure. AI
adoption depends not only on the maturity of algorithms, but on access to modern health IT systems,
shared‑service operating models, strong governance, and a workforce prepared for new digital roles—
capabilities that are often cost‑prohibitive to build independently. Trust in clinical AI must be established
through real‑world evidence generated in local care settings, allowing performance, safety, and
workflow impact to be validated over time rather than inferred from isolated pilots. Without this
foundation, AI risks increasing fragmentation rather than productivity, particularly among providers in
rural and low resourced settings.
OCHIN: Shared Infrastructure Built for Trust, Affordability, and Scale
OCHIN is a nonprofit shared health IT and services organization supporting more than 300 independent
providers across 44 states, including rural health clinics, federally qualified health centers, tribal clinics,
and critical access hospitals. We support nearly 44,000 providers nationwide, generating real‑world
evidence from routine clinical care across rural, tribal, and other historically underserved communities.
Our experience demonstrates that AI tools that perform well in rural and low‑resourced settings—
where staffing is limited, workflows are stretched, and tolerance for disruption is low—represent the
true value proposition of clinical AI for the healthcare system as a whole. In October 2025 alone, OCHIN
network members used generative AI features nearly 700,000 times, and by the end of 2025, more than
20 AI‑enabled tools were live across the network.
Our shared‑services model enables thin‑margin providers in rural and least‑resourced communities to
access enterprise‑grade technology, cybersecurity, compliance, governance, and workforce training at a
fraction of the cost of building and maintaining those capabilities independently. Fixed costs are spread
across a national network. Duplication is reduced. Accountability is strengthened through centralized

governance where appropriate. Federal investments go further. This shared infrastructure is
fundamental to affordability at both the provider and system level. For rural and low‑resourced
organizations, shared services are not optional enhancements; they are the only viable path to
sustaining modern health IT and responsible AI adoption without diverting scarce resources away from
patient care. Our learnings from AI adoption in the OCHIN network are included in the Appendix.
Recommendations
We respectfully recommend that HHS prioritize the following policies for rural and low‑resourced
settings, leveraging existing trusted partners with established track records and mature operating
models:
• Coordinate across HHS agencies and with the Department of Labor to fund health IT workforce
development and AI skilling. As care delivery becomes increasingly digital, AI adoption depends on
a dependable operating backbone and a workforce equipped to implement, govern, and monitor AI
across clinical and operational workflows. Targeted investment enables AI to function as a true
capacity multiplier.
• Underwrite shared‑service models that support the validation, deployment, and ongoing
monitoring of clinical AI. Federal investment in network‑based AI services can reduce duplicative
cost and governance burden, enable consistent and locally appropriate evaluation of AI in real‑world
care settings, and create economies of scale that accelerate responsible adoption.
• Invest in modernized health IT systems for rural and low‑resourced providers operating on
antiquated platforms. AI‑enabled tools depend on interoperable data exchange, secure
infrastructure, and modern core systems. Targeted modernization investment reduces cybersecurity
risk and ensures AI can be deployed safely where need is greatest.
• Advance regional technical assistance resource centers to support AI adoption in clinical care.
Even when tools and infrastructure exist, many providers lack hands‑on expertise to integrate AI
into workflows and navigate evolving regulatory requirements. Regional centers can accelerate
learning and reduce implementation risk.
Conclusion
Healthcare is entering a period defined by constrained resources and increasing demand. The question
is not whether AI will be adopted, but whether it will be deployed in ways that increase productivity,
strengthen rural access, and improve long‑term outcomes. That outcome depends on AI adoption
grounded in affordable, trusted infrastructure and evidence generated through real‑world clinical use,
particularly in rural and low‑resourced settings that test whether innovation truly delivers value.
We appreciate the opportunity to provide input and welcome continued partnership in advancing
infrastructure‑based AI adoption that delivers measurable value for patients, providers, and taxpayers
alike.

supporting chronic disease risk stratification, care coordination, documentation efficiency, revenue cycle

low‑resourced environments where staffing shortages, broadband constraints, and complex patient

likely to scale safely and sustainably across the broader healthcare system.
What We Have Learned
• Trust in clinical AI is built through evidence generated under local conditions. Providers, patients,
and regulators gain confidence when AI performance is validated in care settings serving similar
populations, rather than extrapolated from pilots in well‑resourced systems.
• Healthcare faces sustained fiscal and workforce constraints alongside growing demand. AI must
help the system do more with less by increasing productivity, not by preserving legacy
administrative structures. AI adoption changes work. It does not eliminate the need for people, but
it does require new competencies in AI oversight, workflow redesign, data governance,
cybersecurity, preventive care coordination, and digital population health management.
• Organizational capacity, not algorithm maturity alone, determines whether AI reduces burden or
adds complexity. Rural and low‑resourced providers face the greatest constraints in margins and
staffing, limiting their ability to independently build the operational, governance, and validation
capabilities required for sustained AI use.
• Shared workforce development models scale effectively when delivered through trusted
infrastructure. Through the federally supported Health Center Controlled Network (HCCN) program
and national digital health training initiatives, OCHIN delivers health IT and AI workforce
development across rural and low‑resourced care settings, demonstrating that workforce readiness
can be advanced efficiently through shared models rather than site‑by‑site investment.
