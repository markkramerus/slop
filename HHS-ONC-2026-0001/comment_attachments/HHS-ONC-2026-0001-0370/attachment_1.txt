Aidoc’s response to HHS RFI
Aidoc’s experience demonstrates that AI has moved beyond fragmented “point solutions” to

diagnostic quality, and sustained innovation. Establishing clear standards for clinical AI
governance will help ensure reliable performance while enabling continued technological
advancement.

Regulatory and Standards Evolution

should support a safe, effective, and efficient quality framework for AI-enabled clinical solutions,
regardless of whether they are classified as medical or non-medical devices.
Payment Innovation
Establishing reimbursement pathways for AI-driven improvements in diagnostic quality could
help unlock broader clinical and population-level benefits. Innovative payment models may
support adoption of high-impact technologies that improve outcomes, enhance access, and
reduce downstream healthcare costs.
By aligning policy development with clinical implementation realities, HHS can help ensure that
technological innovation is deployed safely, effectively, and at scale for the benefit of both
clinicians and patients.

Responses to Individual Questions:
HHS question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation for AI
for healthcare and its adoption and use in clinical care?
Aidoc believes that the most significant barriers to private sector innovation in healthcare AI - and
to its responsible adoption in clinical care - are structural rather than technological. While AI
capabilities continue to advance rapidly, the surrounding healthcare ecosystem has not evolved
at the same pace. Reimbursement models, validation standards, implementation processes, and
workforce preparedness remain fragmented.
These structural gaps create uncertainty for developers, misaligned incentives for health systems,
friction during deployment, and inconsistent governance across the AI lifecycle. Collectively, they
slow innovation, limit investment, and delay patient access to clinically meaningful technologies.
The primary barriers fall into these interconnected categories:
● Potential regulatory opportunities and lifecycle governance gaps
● Misaligned reimbursement and financial incentives
● Fragmented evaluation and monitoring infrastructure
● Workforce readiness, trust, and liability concerns
Addressing these systemic challenges in a coordinated manner would accelerate responsible
innovation while strengthening patient safety, trust, equity, and long-term sustainability of AI-
enabled clinical care.
i. Potential Regulatory Opportunities and Lifecycle Governance Gaps
i.a Full AI Lifecycle Quality Governance
Innovation is constrained by the lack of harmonized federal governance across the
full AI Total Product Lifecycle (TPLC).
The current regulatory landscape is imbalanced: some AI tools are deployed with
minimal lifecycle oversight, while others face unclear or very robust requirements.
This creates market uncertainty—allowing some products to enter clinical use
without defined standards while potentially slowing innovation for others.
Aidoc believes foundational AI Quality Management System (QMS) principles
should apply across the AI lifecycle, including clear definition of intended use and
users, instructions for use, required user training, defined validation, post-

deployment surveillance, change management, and performance transparency &
explainability.
Establishing baseline AI lifecycle governance expectations would reduce
uncertainty, clarify liability and shared accountability, and most important - support
patient safety, similar to foundational controls applied to low-risk medical devices
(such as class I devices, which require a QMS, but a premarket review by FDA is
not needed). We believe that unified basic standardization would support medical
and non medical devices, and will also help a unified federal standard motion,
allowing to reduce state legislation and fragmentation in this domain.
i.b Risk–Benefit Frameworks That Better Capture System-Level Clinical
Value
Current regulatory benefit assessments often focus primarily on diagnostic
performance metrics, particularly in novel regulatory pathways (De Novo
submissions). However, AI-enabled clinical tools frequently generate value
through workflow optimization and system-level impacts that can influence patient
outcomes.
These efficiency-related impacts are often treated as operational rather than
quality outcomes, limiting their influence on regulatory risk–benefit determinations.
A more comprehensive framework incorporating system-level clinical impact would
better reflect real-world AI value.
i.c Alignment of Validation Pathways With Clinical Risk and Evidence
Maturity
Validation requirements remain inconsistent across device categories. In
particular, they can impose disproportionate burdens on multi-indication and
multimodal AI systems that are essential for scalable clinical decision support.
We appreciate that FDA has increasingly adopted innovative regulatory
approaches, and we strongly support the continued use and expansion of such
pathways. Further alignment between validation rigor, clinical risk, technological
maturity, and real-world evidence generation would reduce unnecessary
development friction while preserving high standards of safety and effectiveness.
ii. Misaligned Reimbursement and Financial Incentives
Current payment policies do not adequately incentivize improvements in front-end
diagnostic quality and preventive care.
The absence of clear reimbursement pathways for AI-enabled detection, triage, and care
coordination technologies limits health system investment in solutions that can prevent

high-harm diagnostic errors, reduce downstream treatment costs, and support health
equity.
Without aligned financial incentives, adoption decisions are often driven by short-term
operational return on investment rather than long-term system value.
This misalignment slows deployment of technologies that may generate substantial
population-level clinical and economic benefits but do not fit neatly within existing
reimbursement structures.
iii. Fragmented Evaluation and Continuous Monitoring Infrastructure
There remains fragmentation between initial regulatory clearance (when applicable, for
medical devices) and sustained real-world performance evaluation.
Developers and healthcare organizations lack standardized and widely adopted
frameworks for holistic AI evaluation that extend beyond traditional performance metrics.
Meaningful assessment should include:
● Patient outcome impact
● Workflow integration and operational effectiveness
● Longitudinal performance stability
● Detection and mitigation of model drift
● Equity and population health considerations
Fragmented evaluation structures lead to duplicated effort, slow knowledge sharing, and
reduced collective confidence in AI-enabled clinical systems.
A coordinated post-market evidence generation and monitoring infrastructure, which can
be established as part of an AI Total Product Lifecycle (TPLC) framework as mentioned
in item i.a above, would promote transparency, accelerate learning across healthcare
systems, and support continuous safety and effectiveness assurance.
v. Workforce Preparedness, Trust, and Liability Considerations
Workforce readiness is a critical component of safe AI adoption.
Most practicing clinicians receive limited formal training in AI literacy, which may reduce
confidence in interpreting AI outputs and exercising appropriate clinical oversight, in
addition to increasing the likelihood of usage-related risks, first and foremost, the risk of
automation bias.
Expanding AI literacy within clinical education and professional development programs
would support safer, more effective integration of AI technologies into clinical workflows.

Liability and accountability uncertainty further constrain adoption. Healthcare systems
often perceive regulatory and legal risk during early deployment phases, particularly when
governance boundaries between developer, institution, and clinician are not clearly
defined.
Clarified accountability frameworks and potential safe harbor mechanisms could reduce
adoption hesitation, especially for smaller or resource-constrained institutions such as
rural healthcare providers.
HHS question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design
changes should HHS prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care
and why?
We believe that HHS has a tremendous opportunity to adapt regulatory and payment policies to
meaningfully accelerate the impact of AI on clinical care.
i. Regulatory Policy:
ii.a Establish Harmonized AI Lifecycle Governance Across Clinical Settings
A central barrier to effective AI adoption is the lack of federally harmonized governance across
the full AI TPLC. Importantly, this gap does not operate in isolation - it directly contributes to
fragmentation in evaluation infrastructure and to uncertainty experienced by the clinical workforce.
HHS should prioritize establishing baseline lifecycle governance expectations for clinical AI tools,
regardless of whether they qualify as medical devices or undergo premarket review. Foundational
Quality Management System (QMS) expectations should include:
● Clear definition of intended use and intended users
● Transparent and actionable instructions for use
● Adequate clinical validation and performance characterization
● Post-deployment surveillance and safety monitoring
● Structured change management for model updates
● Appropriate explainability and transparency to end users
Without consistent baseline governance, safety and quality uncertainty increases, and interpretive
burden is shifted to healthcare institutions. In practice, health systems are forced to build ad hoc
oversight, monitoring, and validation structures—often duplicating efforts and applying
inconsistent standards. Standardized lifecycle expectations would clarify shared accountability
between developers and healthcare systems, reduce redundancy, and create the structural
foundation upon which coordinated evaluation systems and workforce confidence can be built.

We would like to further zoom in on two interrelated aspects that are inherent components of a
comprehensive TPLC framework: (1) fragmented evaluation and continuous monitoring
infrastructure, and (2) workforce preparedness, trust, and liability considerations. These are not
separate challenges, but downstream manifestations of incomplete lifecycle governance.
Addressing them explicitly within the TPLC structure would significantly strengthen safe and
scalable AI adoption.
Fragmented Evaluation and Continuous Monitoring Infrastructure
The absence of harmonized lifecycle governance contributes directly to fragmentation between
initial regulatory clearance (when applicable for medical devices) and sustained real-world
performance evaluation.
Where baseline expectations for post-deployment monitoring, performance reassessment, and
change management are not clearly defined at the federal level, developers and healthcare
organizations lack standardized and widely adopted frameworks for holistic AI evaluation. As a
result, evaluation efforts often remain siloed—focused narrowly on premarket performance
metrics rather than continuous, real-world effectiveness.
Meaningful assessment should include:
● Patient outcome impact
● Workflow integration and operational effectiveness
● Longitudinal performance stability
● Detection and mitigation of model drift
● Equity and population health considerations
Fragmented evaluation structures lead to duplicated efforts, slower knowledge sharing, and
reduced collective confidence in AI-enabled clinical systems. Critically, they also weaken the
feedback loop that should connect post-market evidence generation back into lifecycle
governance and model improvement.
A coordinated post-market evidence generation and monitoring infrastructure—established as
part of a comprehensive AI TPLC framework—would operationalize the governance principles
described above. By embedding structured surveillance, shared data standards, and continuous
learning mechanisms into the lifecycle itself, such an infrastructure would promote transparency,
accelerate cross-system learning, and support ongoing safety and effectiveness assurance.
Workforce Preparedness, Trust, and Liability Considerations
Lifecycle governance and monitoring fragmentation also have direct implications for clinician trust
and liability perception.
When governance expectations are unclear and post-market monitoring is inconsistent, clinicians
are left uncertain about the reliability, scope, and limitations of AI tools deployed in their workflows.

This uncertainty compounds existing gaps in AI literacy and increases the risk of inappropriate
reliance or automation bias.
Workforce readiness is therefore inseparable from lifecycle governance. Clear intended use
definitions, transparent validation standards, explainability requirements, and structured change
management processes all support clinicians in understanding when and how AI outputs should
inform decision-making.
At present, most clinicians receive limited formal training in AI literacy, which may reduce
confidence in interpreting AI outputs and exercising appropriate oversight. Expanding AI literacy
within undergraduate medical education, residency programs, and continuing professional
development initiatives would strengthen safe integration—but these efforts must be supported
by consistent governance signals and reliable monitoring infrastructure.
Liability and accountability uncertainty further constrain adoption. Healthcare systems often
perceive heightened regulatory and legal risk during early deployment phases, particularly when
governance boundaries between developer, institution, and clinician are not clearly defined. In
the absence of harmonized lifecycle standards, responsibility is diffused and often shifted
downstream.
Clarified accountability frameworks tied to adherence to defined lifecycle governance standards—
would reduce hesitation and align incentives. Such clarity is especially important for smaller or
resource-constrained institutions, including rural healthcare providers, which may lack the
capacity to independently construct robust AI governance ecosystems
ii.b Modernize Risk–Benefit Assessment Frameworks
Current regulatory benefit frameworks often focus narrowly on diagnostic accuracy metrics,
particularly in novel pathways such as De Novo classifications. However, AI-enabled clinical tools
frequently generate value through workflow optimization and system-level improvements that
influence patient outcomes.
For example, AI that accelerates identification and discharge of low-risk patients in crowded
emergency departments can reduce bed shortages, improve triage efficiency, and enable faster
care for critically ill patients. These impacts materially influence morbidity and mortality but are
often categorized as “efficiency” rather than “quality,” limiting their regulatory weight in risk–benefit
determinations.
HHS should encourage regulatory approaches that:
● Recognize workflow acceleration and throughput improvements as clinical quality
drivers
● Incorporate system-level outcomes into benefit assessment
● Evaluate downstream impact on time-to-treatment, diagnostic delay, and
resource allocation

A broader risk–benefit framework would better reflect real-world clinical value and reduce
disincentives for meaningful innovation.
ii.c Align Validation Pathways With Clinical Risk and Real-World Evidence
It is evident the FDA has been working tirelessly to innovate safe and effective methods of product
evaluation. We’d like to encourage that continued motion. Certain radiology software products
continue to rely heavily on pre market studies. While we believe in the importance of robust pre
market assessment, there are ways to balance the load better between pre and post market,
relying more heavily on real world evidence.
These requirements can disproportionately burden multi-indication and multimodal AI systems
that are essential for scalable clinical decision support.
HHS should prioritize:
● Expanded acceptance of real-world evidence in validation
● Alignment of evidentiary rigor with clinical risk and technological maturity
● Flexible validation pathways for multi-indication and adaptive AI systems
Reducing unnecessary development friction—while preserving high safety standards—would
accelerate responsible deployment of clinically meaningful AI technologies.
ii. Payment Policy:
CMS has taken important steps to create progressive reimbursement programs that can spur
technological innovation. The current ACCESS and TEMPO are key starting point initiatives that
may meaningfully catalyze collaboration between health systems and industry technology
partners to address care gaps and accelerate innovation. However, the current programs are
focused primarily on chronic conditions and ambulatory care.
CMS has an opportunity to extend this impact further to address the crisis of diagnostic error and
substantially mitigate serious harm. Today, an estimated 900,000 serious diagnostic errors occur
in the US annually, leading to nearly 400,000 deaths and serious disablements.4 These errors are
largely preventable, with nearly 90% of them occurring in a hospital setting (inpatient or ED). By
creating reimbursement pathways based on improving measurable front-end diagnostic quality
for high-harm conditions, CMS can elevate Diagnostic Harm to become a policy priority and
meaningfully reduce preventable harm related to diagnostic errors.
Current reimbursement pathways are slow to evolve, narrowly scoped compared to the
foundational infrastructure AI represents, and often poorly suited to AI-enabled care, particularly

when payment models require a direct physician work component rather than recognizing
automated diagnostic and coordination value.
One could argue that certain AI-enabled activities can be financed under existing fee-for-service
mechanisms. However, relying on fee-for-service alone creates structural barriers to adoption and
limits the public health impact of AI:
1. It weakens incentives to invest in earlier detection interventions where the return accrues
over years, such as cardiovascular disease or cancer, because the entity funding the
intervention is often not the entity capturing downstream savings.
2. This dynamic disproportionately disadvantages community hospitals that care for higher
proportions of Medicare and Medicaid beneficiaries and operate on thinner margins, even
though these institutions could realize outsized benefit from improved diagnostic quality
and proactive care pathways.
3. Fee-for-service does not consistently reward solutions that reduce total cost of care, which
reduces incentives for both developers and health systems to prioritize AI that lowers
avoidable utilization, prevents complications, and improves longitudinal outcomes.
As an example, heart disease is the leading cause of death in the United States, yet proven tools
to support population screening - such as coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring - remain
underused due largely to reimbursement gaps. Although nearly half of U.S. adults have
measurable coronary calcium and elevated CAC scores increase coronary event risk sevenfold,
most insurers do not routinely cover CAC analysis. As a result, access is limited and follow-up is
inconsistent, with approximately 30% of moderate-to-severe cases receiving no preventive
therapy. This creates a significant missed opportunity for early, equitable intervention before
costly and life-threatening cardiac events occur.
AI-enabled CAC analysis fundamentally changes the reimbursement equation by leveraging
imaging that is already being performed. Rather than requiring new tests, AI can retrospectively
and prospectively analyze existing chest CT scans - performed on millions of Americans each
year - to identify incidental coronary calcium at scale. When paired with AI-driven care
management workflows that ensure appropriate follow-up, this approach transforms CAC
screening from an underutilized diagnostic tool into a scalable preventive care pathway. Evidence
shows opportunistic screening significantly increases statin initiation, demonstrating clear
downstream clinical action and measurable value.
For CMS and other payers, reimbursing AI-based CAC analysis and management represents a
high-impact, infrastructure-light investment aligned with value-based care and health equity goals.
The cost of inaction is substantial - missed incidental CAC in the Medicare chest CT population
alone represents billions of dollars in first-year medical costs and lost life-years. By establishing
reimbursement pathways for AI-driven CAC analysis and follow-up management, for example,
CMS can incentivize health systems to close a major preventive cardiology gap, reduce avoidable
hospitalizations, and shift cardiovascular spending from emergency response to proactive risk
reduction.

The CAC example provided, is just an example, intended to illustrate that traditional code-based
reimbursement may not fully address these needs, but thoughtful innovation in payment design
could unlock adoption at national scale and deliver population-level impact unmatched by any
prior diagnostic reform.
Coupled with this opportunity, we recognize the complexity of modernizing payment policy. We
propose two potential paths forward:
First, CMS could modernize and expand existing payment pathways for emerging technologies,
particularly in hospital-based and emergency care settings where AI can meaningfully reduce
diagnostic delay and prevent harm. CMS could consider establishing pathways that allow certain
AI technologies to be recognized as distinct, separately payable services, rather than being
bundled into existing procedure or imaging codes or tied to physician work. Many AI systems
perform clinically meaningful, resource-intensive functions, such as independent data analysis,
risk stratification, and clinical decision support, that are not adequately reflected in current bundled
payments. Creating mechanisms to unbundle and appropriately value these technological
services would help ensure payment accurately reflects the resources involved, support sustained
innovation, and promote access to technologies that improve care quality, efficiency, and
outcomes.
Second, as an alternative to purely code-based reimbursement, CMS should consider
establishing a dedicated Diagnostic Quality Improvement payment pathway, potentially through
CMMI demonstration authority, that rewards measurable reductions in diagnostic delay,
preventable harm, and avoidable downstream utilization for high-impact conditions. Such a model
could be structured around performance-based payments tied to validated outcome metrics,
enabling health systems to adopt AI-enabled infrastructure that demonstrably improves front-end
diagnostic accuracy and care coordination. Thoughtful innovation in payment design would unlock
adoption at national scale and deliver population-level impact unmatched by prior diagnostic
reforms.
HHS question 3: For non-medical devices, what novel legal and implementation
issues exist, and what role should HHS play?
The use of AI in clinical care outside traditional medical device regulatory pathways introduces
novel legal, governance, and implementation challenges. These challenges primarily stem from
structural gaps in accountability architecture rather than from the intrinsic technological risk of AI
itself.
As clinical AI systems increasingly influence clinical workflows and decision-making processes,
policy focus should shift toward establishing baseline trust, safety, and responsibility frameworks
while preserving flexibility for innovation. In particular, clarifying governance expectations across
the AI lifecycle would help address many of the uncertainties associated with AI tools that fall
outside conventional medical device regulation.

We believe that if the recommendation outlined in Section II.A—establishing harmonized AI
lifecycle governance across clinical settings—is implemented, many of the legal and
implementation challenges associated with non-medical-device clinical AI applications could be
substantially mitigated. A consistent lifecycle governance framework would help define
accountability boundaries, support transparent validation and monitoring practices, and reduce
ambiguity regarding the roles of developers, healthcare institutions, and clinicians.
Accordingly, HHS can play a critical role in advancing baseline governance standards, promoting
workforce readiness, and facilitating shared evidence and monitoring infrastructures that support
safe and responsible deployment of clinical AI across regulatory categories.
HHS question 5: How can HHS best support private-sector activities (e.g.,
accreditation, certification, credentialing) to promote AI use?
We believe that thoughtful evolution of payment and reimbursement design could play a critical
role in enabling the national-scale adoption of clinical AI and supporting population-level clinical
and operational impact that cannot be achieved through technology advancement alone. Aligning
economic incentives with demonstrated clinical value and real-world performance may help
facilitate the responsible integration of AI into clinical care pathways.
In parallel, we believe that greater harmonization and standardization across the TPLC for clinical
AI developers could strengthen confidence in AI-enabled systems, thereby supporting broader
private-sector adoption and clinical reliance on validated technologies. Both of these dimensions
are further discussed in our response to Question 2 above.
HHS question 6: Where has AI in clinical care met or exceeded expectations, and
where has it fallen short? What novel tools hold the most promise?
AI tools in clinical care have most consistently met or exceeded performance and expectations in
well-defined, workflow-integrated use cases, particularly imaging triage, early detection of high-
risk conditions, and care coordination, where they have improved timeliness of diagnosis and

treatment5,6,7, reduced ED8, inpatient9,10, and ICU length of stay11,12 and improved follow-up and
intervention rates.13,14
Beyond the published literature, adoption trends reinforce this progress. On the Aidoc platform
alone, the average number of use cases deployed per health system has grown from roughly
three just a few years ago to more than a dozen today. Clinicians and administrators are
expanding deployment because they are seeing tangible clinical and operational value.
By contrast, AI has fallen short when there is misalignment between an algorithm’s intended use
and real world clinical workflows, or when financial incentives do not support the required changes
in practice. Even strong technical performance does not translate into clinical impact if ground
truth definitions are inconsistent, integration is weak, or adoption is superficial. A clear example
is incidental findings. As discussed in the payment reform section, many health systems choose
not to implement the workflow changes required to act on these findings because the financial
incentives are unclear or misaligned.
Adoption is also uneven. Community based systems, particularly those operating primarily in fee
for service environments, often struggle to identify sustainable payment pathways. As a result,
the benefits of AI are not being distributed uniformly across the healthcare system.
As a specific example: as AI technology continues to make rapid advancement, more
comprehensive detection becomes possible at scale. Broad adoption of, for example,
comprehensive imaging AI, would enable significant improvements to diagnostic speed and
accuracy, particularly in acute care settings.

HHS question 7: Which decision makers drive AI adoption within healthcare
organizations, and what are the administrative hurdles?
Aidoc’s experience shows that when a broad enough portfolio provides real clinical value, AI
adoption can shift from departmental "point solutions" to enterprise-wide strategy.
Such effective adoption requires a synchronized triple sponsorship model and a governance
structure that treats AI as a high powered always-on-assistant rather than just software.
i. Influential Roles and Decision-Makers
The adoption of clinical AI is driven by a multi-disciplinary trifecta of leadership that ensures the
technology is sharpening clinical judgment rather than replacing it:
● Triple Sponsorship: Successful integration requires three distinct sponsors:
○ Executive Sponsor: Owns the enterprise impact and provides the authority to
allocate resources and remove obstacles.
○ Clinical Sponsor: A physician champion (e.g., CMO, CMIO or Service Line Leader)
who provides direction on workflows and ensures the tool is designed for the "joy
of practice" and improvement of patient care.
○ IT/Technical Partner: Anticipates integration needs and owns the long-term
reliability and security of the system.
● Clinical Champions : These front-line clinicians are essential for generating excitement
and surfacing early challenges. They act as microphones to build conviction across the
medical staff.
● Enterprise AI Governance Committees: Many health systems are formalizing AI Councils
that include legal, ethics, and clinical stakeholders. Their role is to be the Chief Ethical
Officers of the organization's data, treating algorithmic bias with the same urgency as a
hospital-acquired infection.
ii. Primary Administrative Hurdles
It has been our experience and observation that even with strong clinical support, several
administrative bottlenecks frequently stall implementation:
● The Integration Gap & Change Fatigue: The greatest hurdle is dropping a technically
sound solution into a poorly understood clinical reality. If AI creates more noise or cognitive
load, adoption fails. Success requires mapping the end-to-end patient journey to ensure
the AI becomes an invisible collaborator.
● Vendor Credentialing Fatigue: Redundant security and compliance reviews currently take

6–12 months per facility. Aidoc addresses this through models like our Implementation
Palooza, an on-site working session that brings all stakeholders (clinical, IT, and vendors)
into one room to troubleshoot technical roadblocks in real-time.
● Governance Wait Times: Traditional governance often slows down routine changes.
Effective governance must be agile and enable speed with control and utilizing technology
for continuous monitoring so oversight is an ongoing practice rather than a one-time
approval.
● Lack of ROI framework: In today’s financial environment, AI adoption requires a clear
return on investment. The challenge is that clinical AI often delivers value downstream
and across silos. Earlier detection can reduce ICU length of stay, prevent complications,
and decompress the ED. These are enterprise level gains, but they rarely sit neatly within
one department’s budget. Lastly, oftentimes incentives are insufficient for improving longer
term care.
● Health systems that evaluate AI only on short term billing lift risk undervaluing it. The right
lens is enterprise ROI, including reduced length of stay, avoided adverse events, improved
throughput, performance under value based contracts, and workforce stability.
● Measurement of the Human Dividend: Organizations often struggle to move beyond
traditional ROI (like billing efficiency) to measure the human dividend such as the
reduction in burnout, concerns about what urgent findings may be waiting in a queue of
unread images, or the return of joy to clinical practice.
By supporting standardized certification and interoperable monitoring infrastructure, HHS can
help reduce these administrative frictions. This allows healthcare leaders to focus on the
clinical AI renaissance and reclaim the art of medicine by letting AI handle the science of data
while humans focus on the connection with the patient.
HHS question 9: What challenges do patients and caregivers want AI to address,
and what concerns do they hold?
Leveraging AI to improve diagnostic quality is a key focus of patients and caregivers. Diagnostic
error harm represents a significant but often under-recognized challenge in American healthcare.
Imaging AI can play a critical role in diagnostic workflows within emergency and inpatient settings
and is central to the diagnosis of several high-impact conditions that contribute substantially to
national error rates, including stroke, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism/deep vein thrombosis, and
lung cancer. Research suggests that many inpatient diagnostic errors are preventable, with nearly
85% of identified adverse outcomes potentially avoidable. Strengthening hospital-based
diagnostic workflows therefore represents a high-leverage opportunity to address the broader
diagnostic safety challenge.
Beyond safety, AI also offers an unprecedented opportunity to improve access to high-quality
care across diverse and underserved populations. Clinical AI systems can be deployed across
care environments- from large integrated delivery networks to remote rural hospitals. At a time
when healthcare systems are under pressure to expand access, AI can help level disparities by

enabling more consistent delivery of high-quality clinical decision support regardless of
geographic or institutional setting.
Federal initiatives focused on rural health transformation present an important opportunity to align
policy with emerging evidence. Care delays are not inevitable; coordinated policy, technology
deployment, and infrastructure development can reduce preventable harm and improve
outcomes. Ultimately, while a patient’s ZIP code may determine which hospital they enter, it
should not determine the sophistication of the technology used to support life-saving clinical
decisions.
Nevertheless, concerns regarding AI adoption remain, primarily around quality, effectiveness,and
safety. We believe these concerns can be meaningfully addressed through the establishment of
harmonized AI TPLC governance frameworks, as discussed in response to Question 2.
Another important consideration is cost. For solutions that address core pain points in the U.S.
healthcare system, innovative and flexible payment and reimbursement models may play a key
role in supporting sustainable adoption and scaling of beneficial AI technologies. While many
clinical AI tools have the potential to reduce total cost of care in aggregate—by preventing
downstream complications, shortening length of stay, and reducing avoidable utilization—their
value is often realized across different stakeholders than those responsible for initial procurement
and implementation. Federal leadership can help bridge this misalignment by advancing payment
models that recognize the longitudinal value of diagnostic accuracy, including transitional
reimbursement pathways, value-based incentives tied to measurable patient outcomes, and
mechanisms that support adoption in resource-constrained hospitals. Aligning reimbursement
with demonstrated clinical and economic impact would accelerate responsible AI deployment,
ensure equitable access across care settings, and enable sustained innovation while maintaining
fiscal stewardship.
HHS question 10: Are there AI research areas HHS should prioritize to accelerate
adoption?
a. Are there published findings about the impact of adopted AI tools and their use in
clinical care?
There are over 230+ peer-reviewed publications and scientific conference presentations
from health systems evaluating the real-world impact of adopted Aidoc solutions in clinical
care.
These studies demonstrate how AI can be integrated into radiology triage, care
coordination, and patient management workflows in order to reduce time to diagnosis and
treatment, decrease emergency department, inpatient, and ICU length of stay, improve
awareness of clinically significant findings, increase appropriate and guideline-directed
interventions, and reduce patient leakage and gaps in follow-up care.
While these findings are encouraging, important gaps remain in measuring AI’s impact on
diagnostic quality and patient outcomes. HHS could prioritize research that evaluates how

AI affects diagnostic accuracy, timeliness, and clinical outcomes in real-world care.
Although AI has the potential to usher in a new era of diagnostic quality, standardized
metrics and validated frameworks to quantify these improvements are still lacking. Federal
support for developing these measures will be critical to demonstrating clinical value,
building trust, and accelerating responsible adoption. A representative selection of
published studies is included as an Appendix.
b. How does the literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI as
part of clinical care?
The literature generally evaluates the costs and benefits associated with clinical AI using
budget impact models using retrospective economic analyses or interventional (pre/post)
studies.
These approaches typically assess financial impact at the patient encounter level and
across downstream services, rather than over lifetime horizons. This reflects the relatively
early stage of adoption and the limited availability of long-term outcomes and cost data.
Most studies therefore focus on near-term operational and financial effects, such as
changes in length of stay, procedural utilization, and follow-up adherence, which can be
directly linked to hospital costs and revenues.
