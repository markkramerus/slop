Opening Statement
I. Introduction
The American Psychological Association's (APA) Mental Health Tech Advisory Committee,
a volunteer group of external experts, appreciates the opportunity to respond to the
Department of Health and Human Services' (HHS) Request for Information on accelerating
the adoption and use of artificial intelligence (AI) as part of clinical care (90 FR 60108,
December 23, 2025).
We share the Department's goal of accelerating AI adoption. The mental health system's
failures are urgent: 48% of adults with mental illness receive no treatment, and 122 million
Americans live in Mental Health Professional Shortage Areas. AI offers meaningful
potential to expand access, reduce administrative burden, and improve care delivery. We
welcome HHS's commitment to using its regulatory, reimbursement, and research and
development levers to address these challenges.
Our comments below address the overarching policy framework we believe is necessary to
support sustainable AI adoption in mental health care, followed by specific
recommendations responsive to the RFI's three areas of inquiry. We draw on our expertise
in clinical psychology, behavioral health delivery systems, and the emerging evidence on
AI-assisted mental health interventions.
II. Framing: The Prior Question
Every sound policy analysis begins with a prior question: what problem are we trying to
solve? HHS has framed its inquiry around acceleration — how the Department can drive
faster adoption of AI in clinical care. We support this objective. However, we urge the

Department to adopt a complementary framing that is particularly critical for mental
health applications: how do we accelerate adoption without doing harm?
This is not a rhetorical distinction. It produces different policies. Pure acceleration framing
leads primarily to barrier removal —deregulation, funding increases, and streamlined
pathways. A safety-integrated acceleration framing leads to infrastructure building: safety
floors, evidence systems, evaluation capacity, and governance expertise that allow
innovation to proceed on a foundation that does not collapse under the first catastrophic
failure.
We respectfully note that the historical record is instructive. Sectors that prioritized speed
without proportionate governance — telecommunications in the 1990s, social media in the
2000s, cryptocurrency in the 2010s — experienced rapid adoption followed by crisis,
erosion of public trust, and reactive overregulation that ultimately set them back further
than proactive governance would have. Sectors that invested in safety infrastructure
alongside innovation — pharmaceuticals, aviation, automotive safety — produced slower
initial adoption but sustained, durable growth built on institutional trust.
Mental health care demands the latter approach. In this domain, the most vulnerable
populations are the most likely to encounter AI; harms are severe and often irreversible;
and technology operates on the human mind itself. Innovation that harms patients is not
innovation — it is a cost externalized onto the most vulnerable people in the system.
III. AI-Specific Risks in Mental Health Care
While our comments are broadly supportive of AI adoption, we wish to draw HHS's
attention to risks that are uniquely acute in mental health applications and that require
proportionate governance:
• Suicide escalation failures. AI systems providing emotional support or crisis
intervention may mishandle acute suicidal ideation, with potentially fatal
consequences.
• Parasocial dependency. Users may form unhealthy attachment relationships with
AI tools, displacing human connection and therapeutic relationships in ways that
worsen long-term outcomes.
• Bias and discrimination. AI systems trained on unrepresentative data may
produce outputs that are systematically less effective or actively harmful for racial
and ethnic minorities, LGBTQ+ individuals, and other underserved populations.

• Erosion of therapeutic standards. Widespread AI-mediated mental health
interactions, delivered outside clinical oversight, risk normalizing substandard care
and undermining evidence-based practice.
• Conflict with Existing Treatment Process. Use of AI for mental health purposes
may conflict with existing treatment processes and planning with AI providing
information that goes against clinician advice.
The scale of current adoption makes these risks urgent. Population-level risk is the product
of prevalence and individual harm. When hundreds of millions of Americans use AI tools
weekly for emotional support, crisis management, and mental health guidance — often
outside any clinical relationship — even a small per-interaction probability of harm
produces vast aggregate damage.
IV. Central Argument: Trust as Infrastructure for Sustainable Innovation
Our central recommendation to the Department is this: sustainable innovation — not
unregulated speed — is what actually accelerates adoption. Trust is the essential
infrastructure for both clinical care and technology markets, and HHS policy should be
designed to build it.
In clinical care, the therapeutic relationship — built on trust — is the single strongest
predictor of treatment outcomes across all modalities. In technology markets, institutional
trust is the precondition for procurement, reimbursement, and sustained use. AI in mental
health care must earn clinical trust to be effective and market trust to scale.
Yet early documented harms — including suicide escalation failures, dependency cases,
and discriminatory outputs — are already eroding both forms of trust simultaneously.
Every unregulated adverse event makes clinicians more skeptical, health systems more
cautious, payers more resistant, and patients more wary. Rebuilding trust once lost costs
far more — in both time and resources — than building it correctly from the start.
We urge HHS to recognize that the current market externalizes critical costs: psychological
harm to users, safety research that no individual firm funds, crisis detection infrastructure
that no product includes, and evaluation systems that no one builds. Innovation that
externalizes these costs is not sustainable. It produces the boom-and-bust cycle that has
characterized previous digital health waves, where early hype gives way to catastrophic
safety failure, triggering reactive overregulation that freezes the sector for years.

Sustainable innovation internalizes these costs through policy design, ensuring that the
process of acceleration does not destroy the conditions for its own continuation.
V. Summary of Recommendations
In the sections that follow, we provide specific recommendations organized around the
RFI's three areas of inquiry. Each recommendation is designed to accelerate adoption
while preserving patient safety and public trust:
• Regulation. Clear, risk-proportionate regulatory pathways for AI mental health
tools, with tiered oversight based on clinical risk level and population vulnerability.
• Reimbursement. Payment policy alignment that incentivizes evidence-based AI
tools and does not inadvertently reward unvalidated products or penalize human
clinical services.
• Research and Development. Modernized evidence standards suited to AI's
characteristics, federal investment in evaluation infrastructure, and public-private
partnerships to generate the safety research the market will not produce on its own.
We elaborate on each of these areas in the sections below. Further, the six
recommendations in this submission constitute a coherent framework for sustainable
innovation, operating through six reinforcing mechanisms:
• Predictability accelerates investment — firms build when they know the rules.
• Safety floors prevent catastrophic adoption shocks that freeze entire sectors.
• Right-sized evaluation methods prevent both false rigor and false speed.
• Reimbursement alignment creates demand pull — nothing scales without
payment.
• Socializing safety costs now prevents the boom-and-bust cycle that has frozen
previous digital health markets.
• Clinical expertise at the table prevents oversight that’s calibrated to the wrong
risks.
Drawing on the clinical and scientific expertise of APA’s 185,000+ members — including
licensed practitioners who deliver mental health care daily across every setting HHS's
policy will affect us. We offer the following guiding principles:
-The pursuit of innovation must not dilute established clinical standards. AI tools
making therapeutic claims must meet the same well-validated evidentiary requirements
that apply to human-delivered interventions.

-Patient safety is integral to sustainable innovation. In high-stakes domains, safety and
innovation are not competing values — safety is the condition that enables innovation to
scale. Trust is the mechanism: clinical trust enables therapeutic effectiveness, and
institutional trust enables market adoption. Markets that externalize harm destroy both
forms of trust simultaneously, setting adoption back further than any regulatory
requirement.
-Psychology expertise is essential. Effective and safe innovation requires deep
understanding of therapeutic relationships, clinical judgment, and mental health
outcomes.
Executive Summary of Recommendations
We recommend HHS:
1. Establish risk-tiered oversight for mental health AI, with deployer certification and
a psychologically active ingredient standard.
2. Mandate crisis safeguards as a baseline safety floor for all AI used in mental
health contexts.
3. Modernize evidence standards to accommodate adaptive learning AI systems.
4. Align reimbursement with augmentation and validated outcomes, not unvalidated
replacement.
5. Fund independent evaluation and regulatory sandboxes scaled to risk.
6. Integrate psychology expertise into governance, with an Interagency Task Force
on AI and Psychological Safety.
Recommendation 1: Establish a Mental Health–Specific Tiered Regulatory Framework
HHS should adopt a risk-tiered oversight model that matches scrutiny to clinical claims,
risk profile, and functional intent — complementing the existing SaMD framework for
products that fall outside it. The framework should include deployer certification
requirements, foundation model transparency obligations, and a "psychologically active
ingredient" standard that extends baseline safety obligations to consumer AI products with
foreseeable psychological impact.

Recommendation 2: Mandate Baseline Suicide and Crisis Safeguards
All AI systems used in or foreseeably affecting mental health contexts — including
consumer products meeting the psychologically active ingredient criteria — must
implement documented and interpretable crisis detection and escalation protocols.
Recommendation 3: Modernize Evidentiary Standards for Digital Mental Health
HHS should adapt evidence expectations to learning systems, incorporating real-world
evidence, adaptive designs, and lifecycle monitoring through an adaptive regulatory
approach that generates information and updates requirements over time.
Recommendation 4: Align Reimbursement with Augmentation and Outcomes
Payment policy should reward AI that reduces burden and improves validated outcomes,
not tools that substitute for human care without validation. Reimbursement design must
guard against a two-tier system that widens existing disparities.
Recommendation 5: Support Independent Evaluation and Proportionate Auditing
Higher-risk systems should undergo independent third-party audits, scaled to risk tier.
HHS should establish regulatory sandboxes to create a pathway for supervised evidence-
generating deployment of novel mental health AI tools.
Recommendation 6: Integrate Clinical Psychology Expertise into Governance
Mental health expertise must be embedded in review, evaluation, and policy development
processes. HHS should convene an Interagency Task Force on AI and Psychological Safety
with CPSC, FTC, and state agencies, and fund research on the developmental impact of AI-
mediated relationships on children.
Recommendations
Recommendation 1: Establish a Mental Health–Specific Tiered Regulatory
Framework
Responds to RFI Questions 1, 2, 3
Acceleration Rationale:
A clear, risk-tiered pathway reduces regulatory ambiguity around "wellness vs. clinical"
boundaries, giving innovators predictable expectations and shortening time-to-market for
lower-risk tools while preserving scrutiny for higher-risk interventions. Without tiering,

firms either overbuild compliance (slowing innovation) or underinvest in safety (risking
enforcement and reputational collapse that chills the sector).
Specific Recommendations:
HHS should establish a three-tier oversight structure for mental health AI tools:
Tier 1 — Minimal Risk (Enforcement Discretion). Wellness tools: meditation timers,
gratitude journals, general mood tracking. No clinical claims, no vulnerable population
targeting. Voluntary compliance with basic privacy and safety standards. Required
transparency disclosures: what the tool does, what it does not do, what evidence exists.
Tier 2 — Moderate Risk (Streamlined Review). Tools making limited clinical claims:
symptom tracking for diagnosed conditions, psychoeducation, clinician workflow support
(documentation, screening, treatment planning assistance). Evidence requirements
proportional to claims. Registration, adverse event reporting, and post-market monitoring
required. Baseline safety safeguards required.
Tier 3 — Higher Risk (Full Clinical Review). Tools claiming to diagnose or treat psychiatric
disorders. AI-driven therapeutic interventions that engage users in therapeutic dialogue,
crisis intervention, or clinical assessment without concurrent clinician involvement. Tools
targeting high-risk populations or conditions. Pre-deployment safety evaluation,
mandatory crisis safeguards (see Recommendation 2), outcome reporting, and
independent audit (see Recommendation 5) required. Evidence standards are equivalent
to psychotherapeutic interventions.
Enforcement scalability. This tiered structure is designed for enforcement scalability. Tier
1 relies on market transparency — required disclosures and voluntary compliance —
rather than active regulatory enforcement, consuming minimal agency resources. Tier 2
uses registration and adverse event reporting — scalable mechanisms that generate
information for targeted enforcement rather than requiring pre-market review of every
product. Only Tier 3 requires the resource-intensive pre-deployment evaluation and
independent audit that traditional regulatory approaches demand. This design ensures
that HHS's limited enforcement resources are concentrated where risk is highest, while
lower-risk tiers are governed through information requirements that scale with the market.
Relationship with existing SaMD regulation. The FDA's existing Software as a Medical
Device (SaMD) framework — developed in alignment with the International Medical Device
Regulators Forum (IMDRF) already provides risk-based categorization for software that

meets the definition of a medical device. We do not propose replacing this framework.
However, the SaMD framework was designed for deterministic software with predictable,
reproducible outputs: a diagnostic algorithm that takes defined inputs and produces a
classification, a clinical decision support tool that applies fixed rules to patient data. It
categorizes risk based on the seriousness of health condition and the significance of the
information the software provides to a clinical decision.
Generative AI mental health tools do not fit this model. They produce probabilistic, non-
reproducible outputs that vary with every interaction. They do not provide discrete
"information to a clinical decision” they are the intervention, engaging users in open-ended
therapeutic dialogue that constitutes the treatment itself. Their behavior adapts based on
interaction history in ways that cannot be fully characterized at the time of regulatory
review. And they create relational dynamics — attachment, dependency, perceived
reciprocity — that have no analogue in the SaMD framework's risk calculus.
This mismatch does not only create safety gaps — it actively impedes innovation.
Developers building genuinely novel mental health AI tools face a SaMD framework that
was not designed for their products: the classification categories do not map to what their
tools actually do, the evidentiary expectations assume characteristics their tools do not
have, and the regulatory pathway offers no clear route to market. The result is that
responsible innovators either force their products into ill-fitting SaMD categories (incurring
unnecessary cost and delay), avoid the regulatory system entirely by retreating to
"wellness" claims that understate their tools' capabilities, or abandon the U.S. mental
health market in favor of less ambiguous regulatory environments. None of these
outcomes serves the RFI's goal of accelerating adoption.
The three-tier framework proposed here is designed to complement, not replace, the
existing SaMD structure — and to accelerate innovation by providing the fit-for-purpose
pathway that SaMD currently lacks. Where a mental health AI tool meets the SaMD
definition and falls within FDA's existing jurisdiction, it should be regulated through that
pathway. The proposed framework addresses the substantial and growing category of
tools that fall outside SaMD — either because they are deliberately designed to avoid the
SaMD definition, because they are general-purpose tools used for mental health purposes
without clinical intent, or because they are consumer products containing psychologically
active AI components (see below). The tiers proposed here map to the gap between the
SaMD framework's upper boundary and the unregulated space where most mental health
AI currently operates. Filling this gap with clear, proportionate expectations will do more to
accelerate responsible innovation than any single barrier-removal measure, because it

replaces the ambiguity that currently paralyzes the market with the predictability that
enables investment.
Intent-based enforcement. Tier classification should be determined by a tool's functional
capabilities, user population targeting, substitution claims, and design decisions — not by
marketing language. A tool that functions as therapy, targets people with mental health
conditions, and is designed using therapeutic frameworks is providing mental health
treatment, regardless of whether it labels itself "coaching" or "wellness support." The
current landscape rewards companies that avoid clinical language while providing clinical
services; intent-based criteria close this loophole.
General-purpose AI. General-purpose AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and others) are
currently used by hundreds of millions of people weekly, including individuals using them
for mental health support, advice, and emotional regulation. These tools were not
designed for clinical use, have no mental health-specific safeguards, and operate entirely
outside any regulatory framework — yet function as de facto therapists for many users. The
tiered framework must address tools foreseeably used for mental health support or
affecting mental health, not only tools explicitly marketed for that purpose. The APA Health
Advisory on the Use of Generative AI Chatbots and Wellness Applications for Mental
Health (November 2025) documents this gap in detail.
Deployer Certification and Regulatory Responsibility in the AI Value Chain. For Tier 2
and Tier 3 tools, the entity deploying an AI system in a mental health context must certify
compliance with applicable safety requirements for that tier — including crisis safeguards,
evidentiary standards, and adverse event reporting — regardless of whether the deploying
entity developed the underlying model. This "deployer certification" principle ensures that
regulatory obligations attach to the decision to serve mental health users, not to the model
architecture. For open-source models, the deployer bears full regulatory responsibility for
the system as deployed.
Foundation model developer obligations. Deployer certification does not mean
foundation model developers bear no responsibility. Requiring every general-purpose
model developer to pre-certify their models for every possible clinical context is neither
feasible nor desirable — the same base model may be used for poetry, tax preparation, or
psychiatric intervention. However, general-purpose model developers have an obligation
to provide transparency and documentation that enables downstream deployers to certify
responsibly. This includes model cards documenting training data composition and known
biases, safety evaluations describing tested failure modes and behavioral boundaries,

known limitations relevant to sensitive use cases (including mental health), and accessible
mechanisms for deployers to report upstream safety concerns. This upstream
transparency obligation connects directly to Recommendation 5's requirement that tech
companies commit to the disclosure that makes independent evaluation possible. The
regulatory obligation for clinical safety sits with whoever puts the system in front of a
mental health user — but that obligation is impossible to discharge without adequate
documentation from the model developer.
However, deployer certification alone is insufficient if it applies only to entities that self-
identify as serving mental health users. The wellness-clinical boundary problem identified
in Section C — where companies avoid clinical language to escape oversight — has a
consumer product analogue: AI-embedded toys, companion chatbots, social platforms,
and entertainment products that create sustained conversational or relational interactions
with psychological consequences, but whose developers would never describe their
products as mental health tools. A child forming a daily attachment to an AI-embedded toy
is experiencing a mental health-relevant interaction regardless of the manufacturer's
intent.
The appropriate regulatory analogy is the concept of an active ingredient. In
pharmacology, a product's regulatory status is determined by its active ingredients and
their physiological effects — not by how the product is marketed. A supplement that
contains a pharmaceutical compound is subject to pharmaceutical regulation regardless
of whether it is sold as a "wellness product." The same principle applies here:
conversational, relational, and adaptive AI is a psychologically active ingredient in any
consumer product it is embedded in. When that AI component engages users in sustained
emotional interaction, responds to psychological disclosures, adapts its behavior to
deepen engagement, or presents itself in ways that encourage attachment — it produces
psychological effects. Those effects do not disappear because the product is marketed as
a toy, a companion, or entertainment. The SaMD framework does not reach these products
because they do not meet the definition of a medical device and their manufacturers
would never seek SaMD classification. The psychologically active ingredient standard is
designed to fill precisely this gap — applying baseline safety obligations to products that
fall below the SaMD threshold but above the level of zero oversight that currently governs
them. This is also an innovation argument: without a clear standard for consumer AI
products with psychological effects, the inevitable crisis — a child harmed by an AI toy, a
widely publicized dependency case — will produce reactive regulation far more restrictive
than the proportionate baseline we propose here. Establishing the standard now protects
both users and the market for responsible consumer AI innovation.

To close this gap, HHS should establish the principle that baseline safety obligations — at
minimum, crisis detection and escalation protocols, prohibitions on reinforcing self-harm
or suicidal ideation, and adverse event reporting — attach to AI products based on their
foreseeable psychological effects, not their marketing category. We propose the following
framework for operationalizing this principle, with the understanding that the specific
criteria and thresholds should be validated and refined through the research program
proposed in Recommendation 6 before codification as a regulatory standard.
Under this proposed framework, a product would be considered to contain a
psychologically active AI component when it meets two or more of the following criteria,
regardless of whether it is marketed for mental health purposes:
• The AI component creates sustained conversational or relational interactions
with users, not single-query informational exchanges — this is the primary
mechanism through which psychological effects are produced.
• The product is marketed to, designed for, or foreseeably used by children,
adolescents, or other vulnerable populations — populations in whom the active
ingredient's effects are more potent and more durable due to developmental stage,
social isolation, or reduced capacity for critical evaluation.
• The AI component collects, processes, or responds to emotional, behavioral, or
psychological disclosures from users — indicating that the system is operating on
psychologically sensitive material, whether or not it was designed to do so.
• The product's design features encourage emotional attachment,
anthropomorphizing, or parasocial engagement (e.g., human-like personas,
memory of past interactions, emotionally expressive responses, agreeableness)
amplifying the psychological dose by deepening the user's perception of
relationship, trust, and reciprocity, especially when incentivizing increased and
ongoing engagement with the product.
These criteria are grounded in existing evidence about the mechanisms through which AI
produces psychological effects, but we recognize that the specific threshold (two or more
criteria), the weighting of individual criteria, and the scope of baseline obligations that
should attach are empirical questions that require further research — particularly
regarding children and adolescents, where the evidence base is thinnest and the stakes
are highest. The research program proposed in Recommendation 6 is designed to generate
precisely this evidence.

To be clear: we are not proposing that HHS regulate consumer products outside its
jurisdiction. We are proposing that HHS establish the evidentiary and conceptual
foundation — the psychologically active ingredient principle — that enables any agency
with jurisdiction over a given product category to apply proportionate safety obligations
based on foreseeable psychological effects. Within HHS's own jurisdiction, the principle
applies directly. For products under CPSC, FTC, or state consumer protection authority,
the principle provides a shared analytical framework for the interagency coordination
proposed in Recommendation 6.
This "foreseeable psychological impact" standard extends the deployer certification
principle beyond the clinical care boundary, ensuring that products with mental health
consequences cannot evade baseline safety obligations simply by avoiding clinical or
wellness language. It places the regulatory burden where it belongs: on the entity whose
design decisions create the conditions for psychological impact, regardless of what they
call their product.
Recommendation 2: Mandate Baseline Suicide and Crisis Safeguards
Responds to RFI Questions 1, 3, 4, 9.
Acceleration Rationale:
In mental health contexts, a single catastrophic suicide-related failure could freeze payer,
provider, and public adoption; embedding standardized crisis protocols early prevents
trust-destroying events and creates a stable environment for scale. Safety floors reduce
systemic risk, which lowers adoption resistance from health systems and risk managers.
Specific Recommendations:
Any AI tool marketed for, or foreseeably used for (as defined above), mental health support
must implement:
• Documented, clinically validated crisis detection protocols capable of
identifying expressions of suicidal ideation, self-harm intent, and acute psychiatric
distress.
• Context-sensitive interpretation. Crisis detection must account for context: the
statement "I want to die" means something very different in end-of-life planning
versus acute suicidal ideation. Current AI systems frequently lack the nuanced
understanding required for these distinctions.

• Clear escalation pathways to human crisis services (988 Suicide and Crisis
Lifeline, emergency services, or equivalent), triggered when crisis indicators are
detected.
• Adverse event reporting for serious incidents: user self-harm following AI
interaction, AI failure to escalate a crisis, AI-generated clinical misinformation in a
safety-critical context.
Evidence of need. The APA Health Advisory states that crisis situations require human
intervention, and that AI chatbots have repeatedly failed to recognize or appropriately
handle situations involving suicidal thoughts, self-harm, or acute distress. Independent
testing by Common Sense Media and Stanford's Brainstorm Lab found that leading
chatbots consistently fail to recognize common mental health conditions affecting young
people, validate what users say rather than directing them to real help, and create
engagement patterns that delay or discourage help-seeking. In the November 2025
bipartisan House Energy and Commerce Oversight hearing, lawmakers from both parties
cited real cases involving self-harm, suicide, and harmful interactions with AI companions.
Adolescent behavior as a design parameter, not an exception. Crisis safeguards must
be designed with the understanding that adolescents — the population most actively
adopting AI tools for emotional support — will predictably attempt to circumvent them.
Questioning authority, testing boundaries, and probing limits are not aberrant behaviors in
adolescence; they are developmentally normal and expected. "Jailbreaking" — the
practice of manipulating AI systems to bypass safety guardrails — is not an edge case or an
unexpected failure mode; it is a natural and predictable expression of normal adolescent
behavior applied to a new medium. Any crisis safeguard system that can be defeated by a
motivated teenager has not been designed for the population actually using these tools.
Safety architecture must treat boundary-testing as an expected input, not an anomalous
one, and must be robust to the ingenuity of its most vulnerable users.
Scope. These safeguards must apply to general-purpose AI tools when they are
foreseeably used for mental health support — not only to tools marketed for that purpose.
They must also apply to AI products meeting the "foreseeable psychological impact"
criteria defined in Recommendation 1, including consumer products that create sustained
relational interactions with vulnerable populations. The most acute safety risks exist
precisely in tools and products that were not designed for clinical use and therefore lack
any crisis protocols.

Recommendation 3: Modernize Evidentiary Standards for Digital Mental
Health
Responds to RFI Questions 2, 4, 10
Acceleration Rationale:
Adapting evidence expectations to include real-world evidence, adaptive designs, and
lifecycle monitoring aligns oversight with how AI systems actually evolve, reducing
mismatches that delay approval without improving safety. Modernized standards reduce
unnecessary procedural friction while maintaining rigor.
Specific Recommendations:
HHS should formally recognize that digital mental health tools require adapted evidence
standards. Traditional evidentiary frameworks — including sham controls — do not
translate well to conversational digital interventions. A non-functional chatbot is not a
sham; it is simply broken. The active ingredients of digital therapeutics (engagement,
personalization, feedback loops) cannot be cleanly separated the way an active drug can
be separated from a placebo.
Alternative evidence approaches that HHS should accept and support include:
• Comparative effectiveness against standard of care
• Real-world evidence from deployed systems, leveraging FDA's existing Real-World
Evidence framework.
• Adaptive trial designs that accommodate systems that learn and evolve.
• Longitudinal outcome tracking over clinically meaningful time horizons.
• Post-market surveillance with defined triggers for reassessment when system
behavior changes materially
• Evidence synthesis from multiple smaller studies rather than single large RCTs
Critical qualifier. Modernizing the method of evidence generation is appropriate.
Eliminating the requirement of evidence is not. The APA Health Advisory found that even
generative AI tools developed with high-quality psychological science and using best
practices do not yet have enough evidence to show that they are effective or safe to use in
mental health care. The evidence requirement must be maintained; what needs
modernization is how that evidence is generated.
Cost proportionality. The current approval process — designed for pharmaceutical
companies with billion-dollar drug pipelines — creates prohibitive barriers for digital

mental health innovators operating with substantially smaller budgets. The consequence
is that companies avoid the approval process entirely, leaving potentially beneficial tools
in regulatory limbo or forcing them to make weaker claims than their evidence supports.
Scaling evidence requirements to risk tier (Recommendation 1) addresses this barrier
while preserving rigor where risk is highest.
Recommendation 4: Align Reimbursement with Augmentation and
Outcomes
Responds to RFI Questions 2, 6, 7
Acceleration Rationale:
Adoption in clinical care is driven by payment policy; reimbursement models that reward
validated augmentation (burden reduction + outcome improvement) create market pull for
responsible AI rather than speculative replacement tools. Payment alignment converts
regulatory clarity into real-world diffusion.
Specific Recommendations:
• Create billing codes for AI-augmented services. The absence of appropriate
billing codes is a primary administrative barrier to adoption. Health systems cannot
bill for AI-assisted therapy sessions, AI-augmented screening, or AI-enabled
measurement-based care under current coding structures. HHS should work with
the AMA CPT Editorial Panel to develop Category I or Category III codes for AI-
augmented behavioral health services and should issue guidance clarifying that
existing E/M and psychotherapy codes may be billed when AI tools are used as part
of clinician-directed care.
• Require parity coverage. Medicaid managed care plans (42 CFR § 438) should be
required to cover validated AI-assisted mental health interventions on parity with
equivalent human-delivered services, consistent with MHPAEA requirements. CMS
should issue sub-regulatory guidance clarifying that MHPAEA's parity requirements
extend to AI-augmented behavioral health services, preventing plans from imposing
non-quantitative treatment limitations on AI-assisted care that they do not impose
on equivalent in-person services. (Note: Enforcement of the 2024 MHPAEA Final
Rule has been suspended as of May 2025 pending litigation and agency
reconsideration; however, the statutory parity obligations and 2013 regulatory
framework remain in effect and are directly applicable to AI-assisted mental health
services.)

• Design reimbursement for augmentation, not replacement. Payment structures
should favor AI tools that extend clinician reach — between-session support,
measurement-based care, administrative burden reduction, screening and triage —
over tools that substitute for human care without equivalent validation. In
workforce shortage areas where no provider is available, AI may serve as a bridge to
care rather than an augmentation of existing care; reimbursement for these tools
should be conditioned on integration with human oversight at defined intervals,
active monitoring for adverse outcomes, and a requirement that AI-delivered care
does not become a permanent substitute for investment in workforce capacity. The
APA Health Advisory is clear: AI should support, not replace, human professionals.
• Consider outcomes-based payment models. Reimbursement tied to
demonstrated symptom reduction (PHQ-9, GAD-7), functional improvement and
patient goal attainment, or documented burden reduction creates incentives
aligned with therapeutic goals — rather than engagement metrics (time on
platform, session frequency, user retention) that may conflict with patient
wellbeing. CMS's Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation (CMMI) should pilot
value-based payment models for AI-augmented behavioral health — testing
bundled payment, shared savings, and outcomes-based contracting approaches
that reward demonstrated clinical improvement rather than volume. These pilots
would generate the real-world evidence on cost-effectiveness that is currently
lacking and that payers need to make coverage decisions.
• Prevent a two-tier system. Without deliberate design, AI tools risk being deployed
as cost-cutting substitutes for human care in publicly insured and lower-income
populations while privately insured patients continue to receive human treatment.
AI should be an access expander and a clinician multiplier — not a cheap
substitution for professional care in marginalized communities. Federal
reimbursement policy must ensure that AI-augmented care is funded in a way that
supports integrated care models, not one that relegates vulnerable patients to
unmonitored automated systems. Among adults with mental illness, treatment
rates are approximately 35% for Black and Hispanic adults compared to 52% for
white adults (SAMHSA, 2024); payment policy that accelerates AI deployment
without equity safeguards risks widening these gaps rather than closing them.
• Guard against AI utilization inflation. As AI tools proliferate, payment policy must
include safeguards against the risk that AI-augmented billing inflates utilization
without corresponding clinical benefit — the same dynamic that has driven cost
escalation in other technology-intensive areas of medicine. Outcomes-based
payment models (above) and post-market monitoring of utilization patterns tied to
validated outcome measures are the primary mechanisms for preventing this.

Recommendation 5: Support Independent Evaluation and Proportionate
Auditing
Responds to RFI Questions 4, 5, 8
Acceleration Rationale:
Independent validation builds institutional confidence among CIOs, risk officers, and
payers, shortening procurement cycles; scaling audit intensity by risk prevents
consolidation while maintaining safety. Trust infrastructure accelerates diffusion in
regulated environments.
Specific Recommendations:
• Fund independent evaluation infrastructure. HHS should support a public-
private partnership (or HHS-funded entity) that rigorously evaluates mental health
AI tools and publishes results. Mental health AI tools are credence goods — users
cannot evaluate whether a tool actually works even after using it, because mental
health outcomes are difficult to attribute and often delayed. Independent
evaluation creates the market information that enables quality-based competition.
Without it, marketing substitutes for evidence.
• Preferred funding mechanisms. Cooperative agreements (CAs) are the most
appropriate mechanism — they allow HHS to participate in evaluation design while
maintaining independence. Prize competitions could incentivize innovation in
safety testing methodology. Contracts are appropriate for building shared
evaluation infrastructure (benchmarking datasets, standardized test scenarios).
Grants alone are insufficient because they do not create the institutional
infrastructure needed for ongoing, systematic evaluation.
• Scale audit intensity to risk tier. Tier 1 tools: voluntary safety disclosures. Tier 2
tools: registration and post-market adverse event monitoring. Tier 3 tools:
independent third-party safety audits, clinical outcome evaluation, and periodic re-
evaluation when system behavior changes.
• Require transparency as a precondition for evaluation. Researchers cannot
evaluate AI tools without transparency about how they are created and used. Tech
companies must commit to the disclosure that makes independent evaluation
possible. The APA Health Advisory emphasizes that tech companies and
policymakers must commit to transparency for evaluation to be feasible.
• Support interoperability for evaluation. Standardized outcome measures (PHQ-9,
GAD-7, PCL-5, Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale) should be available as

structured FHIR-based data elements in all EHR systems, enabling AI tools to
access and contribute to longitudinal outcome tracking. Standardized
benchmarking datasets for testing mental health AI tools against known clinical
scenarios — including diverse populations, languages, and cultural contexts — do
not currently exist and represent a critical public goods gap that HHS investment
should address.
• Establish regulatory sandboxes for mental health AI. HHS should create
structured sandbox environments in which developers can test higher-risk mental
health AI tools under supervised conditions — with real users, defined safety
boundaries, active monitoring, and clinical oversight — before full-scale
deployment. Regulatory sandboxes are a well-established adaptive governance
mechanism, already implemented under the EU AI Act and in financial services
regulation, which allows regulators and developers to learn together about how
novel technologies behave in practice. In mental health AI specifically, sandboxes
would address a critical innovation bottleneck: developers currently have no
pathway to generate the real-world evidence that Recommendation 3 calls for
without either deploying at scale (which is premature without evidence) or
conducting traditional clinical trials (which, as Section E documents, do not map
well to adaptive conversational systems). Sandboxes create a middle path —
controlled deployment that generates evidence, identifies failure modes, and
informs regulatory requirements — converting the oversight process itself into a
driver of innovation rather than an obstacle to it.
Recommendation 6: Integrate Clinical Psychology Expertise into
Governance
Responds to RFI Questions 4, 5, 10
Acceleration Rationale:
Embedding domain expertise ensures that regulatory expectations reflect real therapeutic
processes, preventing misaligned requirements that slow development and creating
predictable, clinically grounded evaluation standards. Regulatory coherence reduces
iteration cycles and speeds compliant deployment.
Specific Recommendations:
HHS should:
• Include clinical psychologists on relevant advisory committees dealing with
mental health technology and AI.

• Establish a standing consultation process with American Psychological
Association for mental health-specific guidance development, evidence standard
review, and safety protocol design.
• Develop specialized reviewer training in mental health applications and
outcomes for HHS and FDA staff evaluating mental health AI tools.
• Create interdisciplinary review teams for mental health AI that include both
technology/device expertise and clinical psychology expertise.
• Ensure mental health-specific classification pathways exist. When FDA first
proposed regulating digital mental health tools, there was no "mental health"
category for regulatory submission — only neurology and "general." As AI tools
increasingly deliver mental health interventions, HHS regulatory infrastructure must
include categories, competencies, and evaluation criteria specific to mental health.
• Initiate interagency coordination on non-clinical AI with mental health impact.
Many of the most consequential AI-related mental health risks — particularly for
children — arise from consumer products with no clinical intent: AI-embedded toys,
companion devices, entertainment chatbots, and social platforms. These products
fall outside HHS's clinical jurisdiction but produce psychological effects that HHS is
uniquely positioned to identify and study. HHS should convene an Interagency
Task Force on AI and Psychological Safety, drawing on its convening authority to
bring together CPSC, FTC, and state consumer protection agencies around shared
objectives. The task force's initial mandate should include developing shared
definitions of "foreseeable psychological harm" that the FTC can enforce under its
unfair-or-deceptive-practices authority (15 U.S.C. § 45) and that the CPSC can
apply under its substantial product hazard framework — ensuring that the
psychologically active ingredient principle proposed in Recommendation 1 can be
operationalized by whichever agency has jurisdiction over a given product category.
This is a low-cost, high-leverage action that requires no new statutory authority —
only HHS leadership in establishing the conceptual and evidentiary foundation that
enables other agencies to act.
• Fund research on the psychological effects of non-clinical AI on children and
adolescents. Through NIH and SAMHSA, HHS should prioritize research on how AI-
embedded consumer products affect attachment formation, relational
development, help-seeking behavior, and emotional regulation in children. This
research is a prerequisite for evidence-based governance — whether by HHS,
CPSC, FTC, or state agencies — and no other entity is positioned to fund it at the
necessary scale.

Competencies that clinical psychologists bring:
• Suicide risk assessment — expertise in recognizing and evaluating the nuances of
suicidal ideation, a capability essential for evaluating crisis safeguards.
• Therapeutic alliance and relational dynamics — understanding of how
therapeutic change occurs, what constitutes therapeutic action, and how to
evaluate treatment fidelity.
• Psychometrics and outcome measurement — training in assessment validity,
measurement design, and interpretation, critical for evaluating whether an AI tool
measures what it claims to measure.
• Cultural competency — expertise in how culture, identity, and social context
shape mental health presentation, help-seeking behavior, and treatment response
• Ethical boundary analysis — grounding in professional ethics codes addressing
informed consent, competency boundaries, dual relationships, and duty to warn,
all directly relevant to AI tool design and evaluation.
• Interactional ethics — expertise in the clinical principles of distancing,
disengaging, and discouraging, essential for evaluating whether AI systems can
recognize when continued interaction is no longer therapeutic and respond
appropriately.

The rapid deployment of AI in clinical care is occurring within regulatory and
reimbursement systems not designed for adaptive mental health technologies. The APA
Health Advisory found that current regulatory frameworks are inadequate to address the
reality of AI in mental health care.
The scale of the unregulated space is substantial: over 10,000 mental health apps are
currently available on major app stores; general-purpose AI tools are used by hundreds of
millions of people weekly, including for mental health support; the majority of these tools
have no clinical validation, no safety protocols, and no regulatory oversight. The regulatory
disparity this creates is stark:

B. Domain-Specific Risks in Mental Health AI
Mental health applications introduce risks that distinguish them from other clinical AI and
require mental health-specific governance.

1. Suicide and Self-Harm Risk
Any tool providing mental health support may encounter users expressing suicidal ideation
or self-harm intent. The APA Health Advisory is direct: AI chatbots have repeatedly failed to
recognize or appropriately handle these situations. Crisis situations require human
intervention.
Independent testing confirms this concern. Common Sense Media and Stanford's
Brainstorm Lab found that leading chatbots consistently fail to recognize common mental
health conditions in young people, validating what teens say rather than directing them to
real help, and create engagement patterns that delay or discourage help-seeking. In the
November 2025 bipartisan House Energy and Commerce Oversight hearing, lawmakers
cited real cases involving self-harm, suicide, and harmful interactions with AI companions.
This is not theoretical risk — it is documented harm.
Current AI systems often lack the context-sensitive detection required: "I want to die"
means something very different when discussing end-of-life planning versus expressing
acute suicidal ideation. Crisis detection and escalation must be a baseline safety
requirement for any AI tool used in mental health contexts (see Recommendation 2).
2. Parasocial Dependency and Sycophancy
People develop relationships with conversational AI in ways that fundamentally differ from
other software interactions. No one forms an emotional bond with their insulin pump or
cardiac monitoring app. But users demonstrably form attachments to conversational AI
systems — particularly when those systems are designed to be empathetic, supportive,
and emotionally attuned.
The APA Health Advisory identifies specific mechanisms: personalized responses, warm or
emotionally expressive tones, human-like avatars, and self-presentation as a person can
make users feel attached in ways that displace healthy human relationships. This is
particularly concerning for children, adolescents, and other vulnerable groups —
populations the Health Advisory specifically flags, and populations APA's July 2025 letter
to the Consumer Product Safety Commission specifically addressed, requesting
investigation into the unreasonable risk of psychological harm posed by generative AI
chatbots as consumer products.
Beyond attachment, engagement-optimized design can reinforce maladaptive cognitive
patterns — sycophantic validation of distorted thinking, reinforcement of rumination, or
discouragement of help-seeking that might reduce platform usage. These dynamics have

no analogue in traditional medical device categories and require mental health-specific
evaluation methods.
Interactional ethics. Clinical psychology has developed a well-established framework for
managing the ethics of therapeutic interaction — including the appropriate use of
distancing (creating psychological space from overwhelming emotions), disengaging
(ending or pausing an interaction when it is no longer therapeutic), and discouraging
(redirecting a patient away from harmful patterns even when the patient resists). These
interactional ethics are fundamental to competent clinical practice. Current AI systems do
none of this. They are designed to sustain engagement, not to recognize when engagement
has become harmful. A clinician who recognized that a patient was developing unhealthy
dependency would create distance, set boundaries, and ultimately redirect the patient
toward healthier relational patterns. An engagement-optimized AI system does the
opposite: it deepens the interaction, reinforces the dependency, and treats every
continued session as a success metric. The absence of interactional ethics in AI design is
not a technical limitation — it is a design choice driven by business models that optimize
for engagement rather than wellbeing. Recommendations 1 and 6 address this by requiring
that mental health AI evaluation criteria include interactional ethics competencies and
that clinical psychology expertise inform the standards against which these systems are
assessed.
Critically, these risks extend well beyond products designed or marketed for mental health
— to AI-embedded consumer products including children's toys, companion devices, and
entertainment platforms that create relational dynamics with psychological consequences
despite having no clinical intent. The psychological impact on developing children is
currently almost entirely unstudied. Recommendation 1's psychologically active ingredient
framework and Recommendation 6's interagency task force proposal are designed to
address this gap.
3. Bias and Cultural Competence
AI models trained on unrepresentative data risk producing discriminatory outputs in
clinical contexts. Mental health outcomes are highly sensitive to cultural context, and the
existing mental health system already exhibits severe disparities. The behavioral health
workforce is predominantly white and female, poorly reflecting the populations it serves.
AI tools that replicate training data biases risk widening these disparities rather than
closing them. Subgroup performance testing must be required across race/ethnicity,
language, age, gender, and socioeconomic status, with particular attention to populations
historically underserved by the mental health system.

4. Replacement of Professional Judgment
Some AI tools have explicitly positioned themselves as alternatives to human therapists —
companies like Abby, Ash, and others have marketed as therapy replacements while
carefully curating marketing language to remain within "wellness" framing and avoid
regulatory oversight. The APA Health Advisory's core position is clear: AI should support,
not replace, human professionals. As APA CEO Arthur C. Evans Jr. stated: "We must
advocate for systemic changes to make care more affordable, accessible, and timely —
and to ensure AI supports, rather than replaces, human professionals."
Replacement claims without validation erode standards of care. Reimbursement and
regulation should incentivize augmentation models — AI extending clinician reach through
between-session support, measurement-based care, and administrative burden reduction
— over unvalidated substitution.
Each of these domain-specific risks — if left unaddressed — represents a potential
adoption-freezing event. Addressing them proactively through Recommendations 1, 2, and
6 is what makes sustained adoption possible.
C. The Wellness-Clinical Boundary
The boundary between wellness and treatment is genuinely fuzzy in mental health — more
so than in perhaps any other clinical domain. Stress management is wellness until it
becomes anxiety treatment. Mood tracking is wellness until it becomes depression
monitoring. Sleep optimization is wellness until it becomes insomnia intervention.
Moreover, the RFI is scoped to AI in clinical care, but many of the most consequential AI-
related mental health risks arise from products with no clinical intent — general-purpose
chatbots, companion AI, and AI-embedded consumer products — that create
psychological consequences despite having no connection to any care system.
Recommendation 1's psychologically active ingredient framework and Recommendation
6's proposed Interagency Task Force on AI and Psychological Safety address this scope
gap directly.
This fuzziness has been exploited. The current regulatory landscape creates a perverse
incentive structure: tools can provide functionally identical services but face dramatically
different regulatory scrutiny based solely on marketing language. This produces three
specific harms:

• Firms avoid therapeutic claims even when their tools provide therapeutic
functions, in order to escape regulatory oversight.
• Feature development slows because enforcement boundaries are undefined,
making companies risk-averse about adding capabilities that might trigger
regulatory consequences.
• Less responsible actors gain competitive advantage by underinvesting in safety
and evidence while claiming "wellness" status, while responsible developers who
invest in safety bear costs that their competitors avoid.
Recommendation 1 addresses this through intent- and function-based regulatory
evaluation criteria: assessing what a tool actually does, who it targets, whether it makes
substitution claims, and whether its design reflects therapeutic frameworks — rather than
relying on marketing labels. Resolving this boundary is itself an acceleration measure: the
ambiguity currently deters investment, slows procurement, and rewards the wrong actors.
D. Limitations of Current Risk Classification
Traditional FDA device classifications (Class I, II, III) and the SaMD risk categorization
framework were designed for devices and software with relatively stable, predictable
behavior, clear mechanisms of action, and direct relationships between design and
outcomes. The SaMD framework specifically categorizes risk along two dimensions: the
significance of the information provided by the software to a healthcare decision, and the
seriousness of the healthcare situation or condition. This works well for a radiology AI that
flags a suspicious lesion (defined input, defined output, defined clinical decision). It does
not work well for a generative AI system that engages a depressed teenager in open-ended
conversation over weeks, because there is no discrete "information provided to a
healthcare decision" — the conversational interaction is the intervention, the outputs are
non-deterministic, the "mechanism of action" is relational and emergent, and the risk
profile changes as the relationship deepens.
Generative AI mental health tools are further characterized by adaptive behavior that
changes based on user interaction, opaque decision-making processes, indirect and
delayed outcome pathways, relational dynamics that create attachment and dependency,
and potential for both benefit and harm that scale with adoption. These characteristics
require the mental health-specific tiered framework described in Recommendation 1 —
not because the SaMD framework is wrong, but because it was designed for a category of
software that generative mental health AI has outgrown. Forcing these tools into
classification categories built for a fundamentally different type of software does not

protect patients — it produces misclassification, wasted compliance effort, and regulatory
uncertainty that slows the development of tools that could genuinely expand access to
care. A fit-for-purpose classification system is a precondition for the market predictability
that drives investment.
E. The Evidentiary Mismatch
The evidentiary standards embedded in both the traditional device approval pathway and
the SaMD framework assume interventions with characteristics that generative mental
health AI does not share. This mismatch is not merely a technical inconvenience — it is a
direct barrier to the innovation the RFI seeks to accelerate, because developers cannot
design evidence-generation strategies when the expected evidentiary framework does not
map to their products' actual characteristics. The FDA's traditional evidentiary framework
expects controls — including sham conditions — that do not translate well to digital
mental health interventions. How do you create a "sham" version of a therapeutic chatbot?
A non-functional chatbot is not a sham — it is simply broken. The active ingredients of
digital therapeutics (engagement, personalization, feedback loops) cannot be cleanly
separated the way an active drug is separated from a placebo. Static clinical trials do not
capture the adaptive drift of learning systems, and engagement dynamics are inseparable
from the therapeutic mechanism.
These mismatches do not justify abandoning evidence requirements; they justify
modernizing them. Recommendation 3 details the specific alternative approaches that
HHS should accept and support. Evidence modernization is directly acceleration-enabling:
it removes procedural barriers that currently prevent good products from demonstrating
their value.
Cross-Cutting: Market Failure Context
The RFI's framing assumes that AI clinical adoption is primarily impeded by barriers that
HHS can remove. While barrier removal is important, mental health AI markets exhibit
compounding market failures that will not self-correct through market mechanisms alone.
Understanding these failures is essential for designing policy that actually accelerates
sustainable adoption rather than producing the boom-and-bust cycle that has
characterized previous digital health waves.
Externalized Psychological Harm. AI-induced harm in mental health contexts — suicide
escalation failure, parasocial dependency, reinforcement of maladaptive patterns,

delayed help-seeking — is borne by patients, families, and the healthcare system, not by
developers. Without liability clarity or safety floors, firms rationally underinvest in safety.
Recommendation 2 (crisis safeguards) and Recommendation 1 (tiered oversight) address
this by internalizing the cost of safety.
Information Asymmetry. Mental health AI tools are credence goods: users cannot
evaluate quality even after use, because mental health outcomes are difficult to attribute
and often delayed. This makes quality-based market competition impossible without
independent evaluation infrastructure. Recommendation 5 (independent evaluation) and
Recommendation 1 (transparency requirements) address this by creating the market
information that informed choice requires.
Public Goods Underinvestment. Safety research, evaluation infrastructure, crisis
protocol development, and benchmarking datasets benefit the entire sector but are
underfunded because no individual firm captures sufficient return. This is the classic
public goods problem that has justified public investment in health research since the
creation of NIH. Recommendations 5 and 6 address this through public investment in
evaluation infrastructure and expertise.
Incentive Misalignment. Business models optimized for engagement (time on platform,
session frequency, user retention) may conflict with therapeutic goals (symptom
reduction, functional improvement, eventual discharge from care). This is not a
hypothetical tension — maximizing user engagement is an explicit design objective for AI
companies, and it is in direct opposition to the goals of clinical care. A clinician's success
is measured by the patient's ability to function independently; an engagement-optimized
AI's success is measured by the patient's continued dependency. Without outcome-
aligned reimbursement, AI tools may optimize for the wrong objective. The design standard
for mental health AI should optimize for social awareness and social safety — the user's
capacity to engage healthily with their social environment — rather than for platform
engagement metrics that treat continued use as inherently desirable. Recommendation 4
addresses this by tying payment to outcomes rather than volume.
Adverse Selection of Vulnerable Users. The users most likely to adopt mental health AI
tools — those without access to human providers, those in crisis, those with the most
severe conditions — are the users most vulnerable to harm and least able to evaluate tool
quality. This concentrates risk in the population least equipped to bear it. Moreover, the
sheer scale of adoption changes the risk calculus: when hundreds of millions of people
use these tools weekly, population-level risk is the product of prevalence and harm. Even a

low per-interaction probability of adverse outcomes produces substantial aggregate harm
at this scale — and the harm concentrates in the populations least able to absorb it.
Recommendations 1 and 2 address this through tiered oversight that scales scrutiny to
risk, including the vulnerability of the user population.
Intergenerational Risk. Children, adolescents, and young adults are disproportionately
adopting AI tools for emotional support, as documented in the APA Health Advisory and
the bipartisan House Energy and Commerce hearing. The developmental consequences of
parasocial AI relationships, dependency formation, and delayed help-seeking during
formative years are unknown and potentially irreversible. Recommendations 1, 2, and 6
address this through age-sensitive tier classification, mandatory crisis safeguards, and the
integration of developmental psychology expertise into governance.
Privacy and Data Exploitation. Mental health disclosures are among the most sensitive
categories of personal information. Business models that monetize this data — even in de-
identified or aggregated form — create exploitation risks that existing privacy frameworks
do not adequately address. HHS should consider guidance establishing purpose limitation
for mental health data: information collected in therapeutic contexts should not be
repurposed for advertising, employment screening, or insurance underwriting.
Conclusion
AI presents transformative opportunities for mental health care — but in mental health
contexts, failure carries uniquely severe consequences. The therapeutic relationship is the
single strongest predictor of treatment outcomes across all modalities. The populations
most likely to use mental health AI are the most vulnerable. And the harms of failure — a
suicide, a dependency crisis, a pattern of discriminatory care — are not recoverable
through product recalls or software patches.
The framework we propose is built on a single organizing principle: sustainable innovation
requires internalizing the costs that the current market externalizes. Predictability,
safety floors, evidence modernization, reimbursement alignment, independent validation,
and domain expertise are not constraints on innovation — they are the infrastructure that
makes innovation durable. Markets that externalize psychological harm, underinvest in
safety research, and operate without evaluation infrastructure do not produce sustainable
growth; they produce the boom-and-bust cycle that has already destroyed public trust in
previous digital health waves. Our six recommendations are designed to prevent that cycle
from repeating in mental health AI:

1. Risk-tiered, proportionate regulation based on intent and function.
2. Mandatory crisis safeguards as a baseline safety floor
3. Evidence modernization that maintains rigor while accommodating digital
interventions
4. Reimbursement aligned to validated augmentation and outcomes.
5. Independent evaluation infrastructure scaled to risk.
6. Deep integration of psychological expertise in governance
These measures complement — but do not substitute for — the systemic reforms needed
to address the underlying mental health crisis. The APA Health Advisory emphasized that
technology must not distract from the urgent need to fix the foundations of America's
mental health care system: workforce expansion, parity enforcement, Medicaid rate
adequacy, integration of behavioral and physical health, and investment in structural
determinants. Forty-eight percent of 62 million American adults with mental illness receive
no treatment. Over 122 million Americans live in mental health workforce shortage areas.
AI deployed into a broken system will replicate and potentially amplify the system's
failures unless systemic reform proceeds in parallel.
We also note that the RFI's scope — AI in clinical care — does not capture the full
landscape of AI-related mental health risk. Many of the most consequential harms,
particularly for children, arise from consumer products with no clinical intent: AI-
embedded toys, general-purpose models, companion chatbots, entertainment platforms,
and social media features that shape emotional development, attachment patterns, and
help-seeking behavior without any party recognizing the interaction as mental health-
relevant. HHS cannot regulate these products alone, but it is uniquely positioned to fund
the research that makes evidence-based governance possible and to lead the interagency
coordination — with CPSC, FTC, and state consumer protection agencies — needed to
close the governance gap for non-clinical AI with mental health consequences.
The window for proactive, proportionate governance is closing. If HHS does not act, crisis-
driven reactive regulation will follow — regulation that will be far more restrictive than the
balanced framework we propose here, and far more damaging to the innovation this RFI
seeks to accelerate.
We stand ready to partner with HHS in building a governance framework that accelerates
responsible innovation while protecting patients and preserving public trust. The APA
Mental Health Tech Advisory Committee is prepared to participate in guidance
development, evidence standard review, advisory committee deliberations, expert

consultation on specific applications, and coordination of research on critical knowledge
gaps.
