Dear Secretary Kennedy,
The Blue Cross Blue Shield Association (BCBSA) appreciates the opportunity to provide
comments on the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS)’s Request for Information
regarding accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence (AI) as part of clinical care,
as published in the Federal Register on December 23, 2025 (90 FR 60108).
The Blue Cross Blue Shield Association is a national federation of independent, community-
based and locally operated Blue Cross and Blue Shield companies (Plans) that collectively
provide health care coverage for one in three Americans. BCBS Plans contract with 97% of
hospitals and 83% of doctors across the country and serve those who are covered through
Medicare, Medicaid, an employer, or purchase coverage on their own.
BCBSA and BCBS Plans share the Administration’s commitment to advancing responsible AI
innovation that improves patient outcomes, enhances clinical decision-making and reduces
administrative burdens—while upholding patient privacy, civil rights and civil liberties. AI
technologies are already transforming health care by supporting more efficient care delivery,
and improved patient engagement and transparency. AI offers revolutionary solutions to support
chronic disease prevention and management through earlier risk detection, timely interventions
and better care coordination. At the same time, trust in these tools depends on clear
governance and accountability, robust protections for patients, thoughtful transparency, and
confidence that AI performs reliably across diverse clinical settings and populations.
We appreciate HHS’s emphasis on a coordinated approach across regulation, reimbursement,
and research & development to support AI use in clinical care. A predictable, risk-based
approach, paired with strong cross-agency coordination, can reduce the uncertainty and
complexity that impedes innovation, while ensuring safeguards are commensurate with real-
1

world risk. As HHS considers how best to use these levers to accelerate adoption, it will be
important to prioritize approaches that build public trust, emphasize quality and continued
improvement, support real-world implementation and avoid duplicative or conflicting
requirements. With that in mind, BCBSA offers the following key recommendations:
• Harmonize and coordinate federal AI policy, particularly health-specific policies.
HHS should provide strong federal leadership to harmonize AI-related frameworks,
guidance and templates with widely accepted standards and existing health care
requirements, including alignment across state and federal jurisdictions where feasible.
This will reduce regulatory fragmentation, avoid duplicative compliance burdens and
provide clearer expectations for organizations developing and deploying AI in clinical
care.
• Adopt flexible, risk-based approaches to policy. HHS should anchor AI policy in
consensus-based, voluntary standards and risk management frameworks that scale
governance and accountability to the likelihood and severity of potential consumer harm.
A flexible approach avoids one-size-fits-all mandates while ensuring more rigorous
safeguards for high-risk clinical use cases.
• Align reimbursement with demonstrated value. HHS reimbursement policies should
reward clinically valid AI that improves outcomes, safety and administrative efficiency,
and should not increase payment solely because AI is used. Tying payment to
measurable real-world impact will promote competition based on results and accelerate
adoption of AI tools that deliver meaningful patient and system value.

BCBSA’S DETAILED COMMENTS ON THE REQUEST FOR INFORMATION:
ACCELERATING THE ADOPTION AND USE OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AS PART OF
CLINICAL CARE
Recommendation #1:
BCBSA recommends that HHS provide strong federal leadership and coordination to ensure its
AI-related regulatory frameworks, implementation activities, guidance documents and any
associated templates (e.g., model documentation, attestations, reporting formats, and best-
practice playbooks) are harmonized with widely accepted standards and align across federal
and state approaches where feasible.
Rationale:
A harmonized approach is essential to reducing uncertainty and accelerating responsible AI
adoption in clinical care. Stakeholders are already navigating a rapidly evolving landscape of
federal and state initiatives, Executive Orders, and sector-based standards. When requirements
and expectations diverge across policies, it creates potentially conflicting obligations and
interpretation and implementation challenges for regulators, enforcement entities, developers,
deployers and consumers—slowing adoption and increasing compliance costs.
BCBSA generally supports the key definitions in the National Association of Insurance
Commissioners (NAIC) AI Model Bulletin, which align well with the National Institute of
Standards & Technology (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), a widely accepted
standard. Aligning HHS materials to these common reference points would promote clarity and
consistency across the health care ecosystem, enabling organizations to build repeatable
governance, risk management and documentation practices that work across programs and
settings. Similarly, recognition of health care quality and accreditation efforts, including those
emerging from the National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA) can help ensure HHS
efforts are not duplicative or conflicting.
Harmonization must also account for the unique regulatory environment governing health care
and health coverage. Health care differs from many other industries because existing legal
frameworks already establish meaningful requirements that directly affect data and AI
governance. For example, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)
provides a comprehensive privacy and security framework for HIPAA-regulated covered entities
and their business associates. Greater federal coordination and alignment around appropriate,
risk-based requirements that build on these existing obligations would help streamline
responsible innovation while accelerating the adoption of AI in clinical care. Aligning HHS AI-
related materials with established obligations, rather than layering inconsistent or duplicative
requirements, will support responsible innovation and reduce the risk of a patchwork of differing
state and federal requirements creates duplicative compliance burdens.
Recommendation #2:
BCBSA recommends HHS adopt and promote a flexible, risk-based, standards-driven approach
as the foundation for responsible use of AI in clinical care.
Rationale:
Consensus-based, voluntary standards developed by accredited standards organizations
grounded in risk-based governance are essential to trustworthy AI. Because AI tools differ
3

significantly in function, context and potential impact on patients, there is no one-size-fits-all
approach to measuring, managing or mitigating risk. Accordingly, HHS policies should scope
accountability expectations based on the severity and likelihood of potential consumer harm.
Rather than imposing prescriptive requirements, such as mandating specific tests or detailed
technical specifications, HHS should leverage and align with flexible, consensus-based risk
management frameworks, such as the NIST AI RMF, that allow organizations to tailor risk
mitigation to the unique considerations of each AI system. This approach supports innovation
while ensuring that safeguards are commensurate with risk, with more rigorous governance,
oversight and mitigation measures applied to higher-risk use cases that could materially affect
patient safety and clinical outcomes.
Recommendation #3:
BCBSA recommends that HHS establish a structured, ongoing stakeholder engagement
process to inform HHS AI policy development and implementation.
Rationale:
Participation should include a diverse group of stakeholders, including consumers and
consumer groups, technical experts and private-sector entities that will be directly and indirectly
impacted—including health insurers. AI policy is being developed in a rapidly changing
environment where the knowledge base is evolving quickly and where there is still limited real-
world experience regulating and operationalizing AI in clinical settings. A broad, diverse cross-
section of stakeholders is essential to ensure HHS approaches are workable across different
care settings and to avoid requirements that inadvertently create barriers to adoption or impose
burdens that do not meaningfully improve outcomes or protections. Because AI is highly use-
case specific, “real-world” input is critical to designing requirements that can be implemented
consistently and at scale.
Recommendation #4:
BCBSA recommends that HHS support education and workforce readiness initiatives to
facilitate responsible implementation of AI in clinical care.
Rationale:
Successful adoption of AI in clinical care depends not only on the technology itself, but also on
the readiness of the workforce and the availability of practical implementation support. Many
organizations face challenges integrating AI into real-world clinical workflows, establishing
appropriate governance, and ensuring staff understand how to use AI tools safely and
effectively. Targeted education and workforce readiness efforts can help clinicians,
administrators and other users develop the competencies needed to evaluate AI outputs, apply
appropriate human oversight and respond to limitations or performance issues. HHS can also
reduce uncertainty by advancing additional study and implementation-focused research that
identifies effective practices across settings and populations, including smaller and rural
providers.
Recommendation #5:
BCBSA recommends that HHS focus AI-related public policy and any resulting requirements to
artificial intelligence systems (AIS), rather than broadly regulating “algorithms.”
4

Rationale:
Algorithms can describe an extremely broad set of processes, including simple business rules
and statistically driven empirical approaches that do not necessarily involve AI or advanced
computing. If HHS policies are written to cover “algorithms” broadly, they risk sweeping in
commonplace operational logic and established analytical methods that do not present the
same governance, transparency or safety issues as AIS used in clinical care. The focus should
remain on the functional autonomy and emergent risk of the system rather than its underlying
algorithmic components. An overly broad application can drive inconsistent interpretation across
regulators and increase compliance costs without improving consumer protections. This can
also dilute oversight by spreading attention across low-risk, non-AI tools, rather than focusing on
AIS uses that may meaningfully affect clinical decisions, patient experiences, and outcomes. A
clear AIS-focused approach better supports HHS’s goals of predictability and proportionality, it
enables HHS to tailor expectations to risk, strengthen trust where it matters most and reduce
uncertainty that can impede innovation and adoption.
Recommendation #6:
BCBSA recommends that HHS support and operationalize systematic, continuous real-world
monitoring of medical devices that use AI through aligned expectations, practical infrastructure
and incentives that promote routine post-deployment performance evaluation and transparency
over time.
Rationale:
AI tools used in clinical care must perform safely and effectively in the diverse real-world
settings and patient populations where they are deployed, not only in pre-deployment testing
environments. Yet, oversight today remains centered on premarket clearance and limited post
market surveillance, providing only snapshots of performance at a single point in time. This gap
is particularly important for payers, clinicians and patients, because coverage, quality and care
decisions depend on longitudinal evidence that the tool continues to perform as intended over
time.
Continuous post-deployment monitoring is especially important for medical devices that use AI
because performance can change as clinical workflows evolve, input data shift, vendor updates
occur or patient demographics change. Without systematic monitoring, it is difficult to identify
and address model drift, strategic drift (i.e., when an AIS gradually stops reflecting an
organization’s clinical policies and ethical guardrails over time, even if accuracy metrics appear
stable), degraded accuracy or emergent safety issues. This can undermine public trust and
create uncertainty for organizations considering adoption, precisely the uncertainty HHS seeks
to reduce. HHS is uniquely positioned to accelerate real-world monitoring by encouraging
common expectations and reusable approaches across the Department’s policy levers. More
consistent real-world monitoring would also help promote market competition based on
demonstrated performance and value and improve confidence in AI-enabled care.
Recommendation #7:
BCBSA recommends that HHS advance a risk-based, lifecycle approach to testing, evaluation,
validation, and verification (TEVV) for AI used in clinical care, coordinated with NIST and other
standards-setting organizations, so that TEVV expectations are practical, consistent and
proportionate to an AI tool’s clinical impact, intended use, and potential risk.
5

Rationale:
A lifecycle TEVV approach is essential because AI tools are not “set-and-forget” technologies. A
risk-based lifecycle TEVV approach, covering both pre-deployment evaluation and ongoing
reassessment after deployment, helps ensure safeguards are commensurate with real-world
risk. This proportionate approach supports public trust while avoiding burdens that could slow
adoption of lower-risk tools. Coordination with NIST and other standards-setting organizations
can help establish consensus frameworks for real-world AI evaluation that prioritize
transparency and risk alignment. This coordination should build on NIST’s foundational AI RMF
and identify opportunities for these frameworks to be meaningfully leveraged across the AI
lifecycle in clinical settings. Alignment around widely accepted frameworks is key to minimizing
compliance friction. Lifecycle TEVV supports HHS’s reimbursement and R&D objectives by
strengthening the evidence base on benefits, costs and tradeoffs associated with AI in clinical
care. When TEVV is tied to real-world evaluation and implemented proportionately to risk, it can
help ensure that adoption is driven by demonstrated value and that patients are protected.
Recommendation #8:
BCBSA recommends HHS structure AI clinical care reimbursement policies to promote adoption
of clinically valid AI tools that demonstrate improved patient outcomes, patient safety and care
team efficiency. Reimbursement should support adoption and scaling of AI that measurably
reduces administrative burden and improves the delivery of care, not simply the additional
presence of AI.
Rationale:
AI is increasingly embedded in clinical care, but not all AI-enabled tools deliver meaningful
improvements in outcomes or efficiency. Payment policies that provide additional
reimbursement simply because AI is used risk encouraging adoption based on tool novelty
rather than patient value and can create unintended incentives that increase spending without
commensurate benefit. Aligning reimbursement with existing expectations and principles for
other clinical interventions, such as demonstrated clinical validity and measurable real-world
impact helps ensure patients benefit from AI that supports safer decision-making, improves
quality of care and reduces delays in appropriate care. Reimbursement policy should also
recognize value where AI streamlines clinical and administrative workflows in ways that improve
provider experience, including by reducing the “context tax,” or the time and resource burden
clinicians face synthesizing fragmented patient information across systems. Structuring
reimbursement around demonstrated value and patient-centered outcomes can help accelerate
adoption of AI tools that improve care and affordability.
Recommendation #9:
BCBSA recommends HHS encourage adoption of high-value AI through value-based payment
models and performance-based approaches that reward measurable improvements in quality,
efficiency, and patient outcomes.
Rationale:
Value-based payment models are well suited to promote AI adoption because they align
incentives around outcomes and efficiency rather than volume. When providers are accountable
for quality and total cost of care, they have stronger incentives to deploy AI tools that improve
6

care coordination, reduce avoidable utilization and streamline workflows in ways that benefit
patients. Incorporating AI-related performance expectations into value-based benchmarks can
also promote competition among developers based on real-world impact, rather than creating
standalone reimbursement that rewards AI use regardless of benefit. This approach helps
ensure that adoption is driven by tools that deliver measurable improvements for patients and
the health care system, while reducing the risk of spending increases tied to add-on
technologies that do not improve outcomes.
