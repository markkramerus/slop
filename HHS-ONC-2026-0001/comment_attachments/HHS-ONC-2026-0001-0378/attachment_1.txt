1. Biggest barriers to AI innovation and adoption in clinical care
From Reveleer’s work with health plans and risk‑bearing providers in
value‑based care, the primary barriers are:
• Fragmented data and limited interoperability. Despite
interoperability policies, clinical and administrative data remain
scattered across an array of systems EHRs, claims platforms, HIEs,
point solutions, and document repositories, with critical signal
buried in unstructured notes. There is some adoption of
transmission standards through standard FHIR based APIs but this
only covers part of the data needs required for AI to
comprehensively and reliably gather the context for an accurate
response. Given the variety of formats driving the source data,
when AI is deployed on this landscape without first standardizing
and normalizing the inputs, it tends to amplify gaps, increase
hallucination risk, and produce inconsistent outputs that
clinicians do not trust.

• Lack of an AI‑ready evidence layer. Many organizations still depend
on partial claims feeds, limited EHR connections, and manual chart
retrieval, preventing the creation of longitudinal,
provenance‑managed member records that can support risk adjustment,
quality, member eligibility, and care management consistently
across the care continuum.
• Insufficient governance, explainability, and auditability. Leaders
are wary of “black box” AI, hallucinations, bias, and opaque
decision paths—especially where AI touches coding, quality
reporting, or point‑of‑care decision support. Many offerings lack
strong MLOps, traceability to source documents, or clear
human‑in‑the‑loop controls. Explainable AI requires new approaches
like our “clinical corpus” model that delivers reliable medical
evidences in a language familiar to clinicians and auditors to
reduce the cognitive load driving administrative burden. By using
this approach, evidence is clear and defensible.
• Misaligned incentives and unclear measurement. Value‑based
contracts increasingly emphasize digital quality, equity, and
downside risk, but organizations lack standardized outcome and ROI
frameworks to compare AI‑assisted versus human‑only workflows. This
uncertainty slows investment and scale. By using incentive
management solutions like our Care Gap Manager, value alignment
streamlines the coordination between payer and provider to focus
on patient outcomes leading to lower costs for all.
• Workflow disruption and provider abrasion. AI tools that require
new portals, double documentation, or low‑precision suspect lists
create extra work and “audit anxiety,” leading clinicians and
coders to disengage even when underlying models are accurate. AI
prediction and recommendation engines like our Hybrid AI, are
built to support the placement of high value clinical next best

actions in the right interface at the right time. EMRs must allow
these tools into their ecosystems so that the richest and most
reliable insight can drive the best patient outcome.
• Regulatory uncertainty and overlapping oversight. Rapidly evolving
rules around interoperability, PHI use, and expectations for
transparency and safety create perceived legal and compliance
risk, particularly for non‑device clinical AI that does not fit
neatly into existing FDA, CMS, HSS, and TEFCA paradigms.
As noted above, the Reveleer EVE framework —a predictive, explainable
AI layer operating over a unified evidence foundation—is designed to
mitigate these barriers, but they remain systemic obstacles to broader
private‑sector innovation and adoption.
2. Regulatory, payment, and programmatic changes HHS should prioritize
Reveleer recommends that HHS prioritize the following changes to
accelerate effective, safe AI use in clinical care, especially for
non‑device AI used in risk adjustment and quality workflows.
2.1. Codify expectations for explainable, evidence‑linked AI
• Establish industry-wide guidance that clinical AI used in coding,
quality measurement, and decision support must provide traceable
links from each recommendation back to specific clinical evidence
(e.g., note, lab, report) and maintain reproducible audit trails.
• Encourage architectures that explicitly separate evidence
extraction (which may use generative or agentic techniques) from
deterministic clinical reasoning to establish a clear boundary
separating AI from diagnoses generation or authoritative narrative

content but instead organizes existing evidence and suggestions
for human review. This is core to Reveleer’s design of EVE Hybrid
AI, which combines two sequenced services—agentic evidence
extraction and clinician‑authored rules in a human managed
clinical corpus—to produce audit‑ready suspects.
Possible regulatory anchors (illustrative):
• Under 42 CFR part 422 (Medicare Advantage) and 42 CFR part 438
(Medicaid managed care), clarify that AI‑assisted risk adjustment
and quality workflows are acceptable when diagnoses and quality
elements meet existing documentation standards, maintain
traceability to source records, and are validated by human
experts.
• Through ONC’s health IT rulemaking, align deregulatory steps on
certification with explicit expectations around standards‑based
APIs, provenance metadata, and logging sufficient to support
explainable AI.
2.2. Align reimbursement and program design with AI‑enabled workflows
• Recognize AI‑assisted digital abstraction, prospective risk
suspecting, and real‑time quality gap closure as acceptable methods
of documentation and reporting under CMS quality programs (e.g.,
eCQMs, Star Ratings, HEDIS‑aligned measures), so long as evidence
and audit standards are met.
• Create value‑based payment model options (e.g., new Medicare
Advantage and ACO model tests through CMMI) that explicitly reward
investment in AI‑enabled evidence layers and prospective risk
adjustment—rather than only retrospective chart review—by linking
shared savings or bonuses to improvements in risk accuracy,
quality performance, and avoidable utilization attributable to

AI‑enabled workflows. Then manage those incentives through
certified VBC incentive management applications like Reveleer Care
Gap Manager which is designed specifically to guide providers and
plans through annual VBC programs to ensure both parties
understand and agree on the program performance and outcome each
year.
Specific references (non‑exhaustive and illustrative):
• 42 CFR part 422 Subpart G (Payments to Medicare Advantage
Organizations): clarify that AI‑assisted coding and suspecting are
permissible when diagnoses conform to existing medical record
documentation rules and remain subject to RADV and other audit
requirements.
• 42 CFR parts 412 and 413: acknowledge AI‑enabled documentation
support as a form of clinical decision support and workflow
optimization, without treating these tools as autonomous decision
makers.
2.3. Strengthen, but simplify, interoperability
• Maintain and refine requirements that payers and providers expose
standardized APIs (e.g., FHIR‑based patient access and provider
directory APIs under CMS Interoperability and Patient Access
rules) while avoiding overly prescriptive certification that
impedes innovation. Emphasize outcome‑oriented metrics such as
latency, uptime, and data completeness that directly enable safe
AI.
• Encourage payer and provider adoption of interoperable evidence
layers that support reuse of curated clinical data across risk,
quality, and care management programs, not just passive
“warehousing.”

• Require EHRs to make it easy for third party AI solutions to
provide enriched evidence, suggestions, and potential diagnosis to
relevant places within the provider workflow to enable choice of
vendor for the best clinical insights and guidance.
2.4. Create unified expectations for non‑device AI oversight inside HHS
• Issue cross‑agency guidance clarifying the roles of ONC, CMS, OCR,
OIG, and FDA for non‑device AI systems that support coding,
quality, and decision support. This would allow organizations to
build AI governance programs anchored in a single, coherent set of
expectations instead of anticipating overlapping or conflicting
requirements.
These changes would directly support platforms like EVE, which already
implement evidence‑linked, human‑in‑the‑loop AI within HIPAA‑ and
HITRUST‑aligned governance frameworks.
3. Novel legal and implementation issues, and HHS’s role
For non‑device AI used in clinical workflows, Reveleer sees several
emerging legal and implementation issues:
• Attribution of liability in human‑AI collaboration. When AI
surfaces a suspected diagnosis or quality gap that a clinician
accepts or rejects, questions arise about responsibility if
subsequent audits or outcomes suggest that the AI contributed to
over‑ or under‑coding, or to missed care. Provide clear guidance
for how accepted recommendations should be logged in systems and
rules as to the significance a single instance can have on the
continuous tuning of a model.

• Use of PHI for training and cross‑client learning. Organizations
must navigate HIPAA, state privacy laws, and contract terms when
using PHI to train, fine‑tune, or adapt AI models. The line between
de‑identified and identifiable data, and between client‑specific
and shared models, is often unclear in practice. Expand on minimum
necessary guidance to include the use of AI and explicit consent
by the patient for this information to be used.
• Black‑box models and due process. When AI models are opaque, plans
and providers may struggle to demonstrate to regulators and courts
how coding or care decisions were reached, raising due‑process and
fairness concerns in payment disputes or audits. Establish rules
or guidance blocking the use of black box models in clinical use
cases.
• Indemnification and vendor risk allocation. Contracts increasingly
need to allocate risk where AI‑assisted workflows contribute to
errors in submissions or clinical decisions, especially when both
vendor AI design and client configuration choices interact.
• Dynamic model behavior and “drift.” Continuous learning and
retraining can change AI behavior in ways that are hard to
document and explain to auditors absent robust MLOps, versioning,
and change‑management practices.
HHS can help by:
• Publishing model contractual clauses or high‑level guidance that
clarifies shared responsibility among vendors, payers, and
providers in AI‑assisted workflows, emphasizing human final
decision‑making and traceability to source evidence.
• Clarifying under HIPAA that PHI may be used for training and
validation of non‑device clinical AI subject to strict purpose

limitation, de‑identification where feasible, and robust access
controls, and by encouraging standardized language in business
associate agreements to cover AI use.
• Encouraging or requiring versioned records of datasets, model
artifacts, and configurations used in AI‑assisted clinical
workflows, so that AI behavior is reproducible and auditable over
time.
• Recognizing human‑in‑the‑loop, evidence‑linked AI as a distinct
category from fully autonomous systems, with commensurate
expectations for liability and oversight. This aligns with EVE’s
design, which treats AI as clinical decision support rather than
as a replacement for clinician judgment.
4. Evaluation methods, metrics, robustness testing, and human‑centered
evaluation
For non‑device AI integrated into clinical workflows, Reveleer uses and
recommends a layered evaluation approach.
4.1. Technical and robustness evaluation
Pre‑ and post‑deployment, models should be evaluated on:
• Precision, recall, and accuracy for task‑specific outputs (e.g.,
suspected diagnoses, chart prioritization, quality data element
extraction), segmented by population, contract type, and data
source. This should include standard definitions for selected
benchmark metrics to prevent misinterpretation of model quality in
the market.

• Robustness and drift, via continuous monitoring for performance
degradation, shifts in input distributions, and outlier confidence
scores, with automated triggers for investigation and retraining.
• Fairness, via segmented performance analysis across demographic
and clinical subgroups to detect systematic differences in
sensitivity, specificity, or error rates.
4.2. Workflow and operational evaluation
Because AI’s value is realized in workflow, Reveleer tracks:
• Charts reviewed per coder per day, charts eliminated from need for
first-pass review, and reductions in time per chart in AI‑assisted
versus non‑assisted workflows.
• Noise rate (the amount of inaccurate or in appropriate suggestions
presented to the provider) and provider acceptance (the amount of
accepted recommendations by the provider) are the two key metrics
driving effective AI-driven suspecting. EVE Hybrid AI, for
example, has shown up to three‑fold noise reduction compared with
legacy NLP in prospective risk adjustment, which directly improves
provider engagement and reduces abrasion.
• Cycle times for risk and quality programs, including speed from
data ingestion to action at the point of care.
4.3. Clinical, financial, and user‑experience evaluation
Reveleer’s measurement framework covers four domains:
• Clinical outcomes, including condition detection and documentation
rates, adherence to evidence‑based care pathways, and avoidable
utilization.

• Financial performance, including RAF accuracy, Star Ratings,
medical loss ratio, and ROI for AI‑enabled programs (e.g.,
increased value per chart, reduced administrative costs).
• Operational efficiency, including abstraction throughput, error
rates, and end‑to‑end cycle time for key value‑based workflows.
• User experience and AI trust, including coder and clinician
satisfaction, agreement rates with AI recommendations, and
adoption analytics.
HHS should support these processes by:
• Funding multi‑site evaluation consortia and pragmatic trials that
use standardized measurement frameworks to compare AI‑assisted and
human‑only processes in risk adjustment and quality.
• Supporting prize competitions and cooperative agreements for tools
that provide open benchmarking harnesses, synthetic test sets, and
measurement standards for non‑device AI in clinical care.
• Using federal contracts and grant conditions to require
evidence‑linked, explainable AI and continuous monitoring in
HHS‑funded pilots, normalizing these evaluation practices.
5. Supporting private‑sector accreditation, certification, and testing
To promote responsible innovation while avoiding duplicative
regulation, HHS could:
• Endorse or co‑develop voluntary AI assurance frameworks for
non‑device clinical AI that emphasize transparency, evidence
linkage, bias monitoring, and human oversight—analogous to HITRUST
and SOC 2 for security and privacy.

• Partner with industry on “trustmarks” for AI‑ready clinical data
and interoperable evidence layers, signaling that organizations
meet standards for data quality, provenance tracking, and
standardized APIs necessary for safe AI.
• Support independent testing labs or collaboratives that benchmark
AI tools against standardized test cases (including synthetic
data) for risk adjustment, quality abstraction, and care gap
detection, with results published in a transparent registry.
• Leverage CMS contracts and quality programs to reward
participation in such accreditation schemes—for example, by
recognizing accredited AI workflows as meeting certain
documentation sufficiency criteria for quality programs, while
retaining audit rights.
These actions would reinforce the direction Reveleer has already taken
with EVE—emphasizing explainable, audit‑ready outputs and rigorous
governance—while giving the broader market clear reference points for
trustworthy AI.
6. Where AI has met or fallen short; high‑impact future tools
6.1. Areas where AI has met or exceeded expectations
Reveleer and its clients have observed strong performance where AI is:
• Powering prospective risk adjustment and clinical intelligence,
enabling more accurate risk stratification, earlier detection of
undiagnosed conditions, and improved provider documentation at the
point of care. Organizations using such tools have seen better
provider engagement and measurable RAF improvements.

• Supporting digital quality abstraction and care gap closure,
increasing throughput and precision in quality measures and
improving Star Ratings through better gap targeting and closure.
• Improving operational efficiency and staffing resilience, allowing
organizations facing workforce shortages to process more charts
per coder with higher yield, effectively increasing coding and
abstraction capacity without proportional staffing increases.
• Delivering favorable cost‑effectiveness, as broader literature
shows AI can reduce screening costs, unnecessary procedures, and
readmissions when seamlessly integrated into clinical workflows.
6.2. Areas where AI has fallen short
Common shortfalls include:
• Noisy, non‑explainable suspect lists. Legacy NLP systems often
produce high volumes of low‑specificity suspects without clear
evidence links, overwhelming providers and eroding trust. This has
been a key driver of provider abrasion.
• Workflow misalignment and low adoption. Tools that sit outside
core EHR workflows, require extra log‑ins, or lack role‑specific UX
tend to underperform regardless of model accuracy.
• Insufficient measurement and drift management. Many
implementations lack rigorous pre/post evaluation and ongoing
monitoring, obscuring true impact and allowing subtle performance
degradation over time.
6.3. High‑potential future tools
Reveleer believes the most impactful future non‑device AI tools will:

• Operate over unified, multimodal evidence layers, combining
structured data, unstructured content, and in the future images
and signals, to deliver richer, context‑aware clinical intelligence
to help clinicians deliver the right care at the right moment from
the right suggestion.
• Use hybrid, agentic evidence extraction with deterministic
clinical reasoning for a growing number of clinical workflows, as
EVE Hybrid AI does today for prospective risk adjustment, enabling
audit‑ready, configurable suspects with drastically reduced noise
and rapid adaptation to new risk models and documentation
standards.
• Embed digital quality measurement directly into care pathways
rather than treating quality as a standalone reporting workflow,
surfacing in‑encounter opportunities to close gaps while
maintaining measure‑aligned data and provenance for eCQMs and
Stars.
• Combine ambient AI and prospective AI, where ambient tools reduce
point‑of‑care documentation burden and EVE‑like engines orchestrate
pre‑ and post‑encounter planning and panel management across the
year. Leverage ambient data as a data set in prospective AI to add
another data dimension to recommendations leading to a virtuous
cycle of ever improving care.
7. Organizational roles and administrative hurdles
In Reveleer’s experience, AI adoption decisions in clinical care are
typically driven by:

• Chief Medical Officers and clinical leadership, who assess
clinical validity, workflow fit, and provider trust.
• Chief Information Officers and IT/data leaders, who own data
integration, interoperability, and infrastructure readiness.
• Value‑based care and risk/quality program leaders, who are
accountable for RAF, Star Ratings, and total cost of care and must
see clear ROI and compliance alignment.
The primary administrative hurdles include:
• Fragmented governance across clinical, IT, compliance, and
finance, which slows decisions and leads to inconsistent AI
policies.
• Lack of structured AI change‑management programs (stakeholder
mapping, communication, training, escalation paths), which can
cause confusion about roles, responsibilities, and safety
oversight.
• Procurement and contracting processes that are not adapted to AI
(e.g., difficulty evaluating explainability, data usage terms, and
model lifecycle commitments).
• Unclear internal measurement standards, making it hard to
prioritize AI investments or justify scaling pilots.
Reveleer has found that formal AI steering committees—combining
clinical, operational, IT, and compliance leadership—and clear,
outcomes‑based measurement frameworks are critical to overcoming these
hurdles and enabling durable adoption of an enterprise AI engine like
EVE.
8. Interoperability opportunities

Enhanced interoperability would materially widen AI market
opportunities and accelerate development in several areas:
• FHIR‑based, near‑real‑time exchange of clinical data across payers
and providers, beyond minimum USCDI, so AI engines can access
timely, rich evidence for risk adjustment, quality, and care
management rather than relying on delayed batch files and manual
chart retrieval.
• Standardized representation of data provenance and evidence links,
including where each data element came from, how it was
transformed, and which documents support a given diagnosis or
quality data element. This directly supports explainable AI and
auditability.
• Shared, de‑identified benchmarking datasets for risk adjustment,
quality measures, and care gap detection, enabling vendors and
health systems to benchmark AI performance and robustness fairly.
• Interoperable “evidence layers” across payers and providers, with
standard interfaces and vocabularies for longitudinal records that
can be reused across retrospective and prospective risk, quality,
and utilization management.
• Open EHRs to support AI-based insights tools to power the best
intelligence within the provider workflow. Closed or pay-to-play
EHR systems limit choice for the providers to the AI initiatives
within the EHR infrastructure and in parallel contributes to the
provider abrasion when new innovative tools are activated but must
operate outside of the primary clinical workflow tools.
The Reveleer platform already uses interoperable clinical repositories
and standardized APIs to support EVE across risk adjustment and
quality solutions; strengthening national interoperability in these

areas would significantly expand the impact and safety of similar AI
engines.
9. Patient and caregiver challenges and concerns
9.1. Challenges patients and caregivers want AI to address
From the vantage point of value‑based care operations, patients and
caregivers most want AI to help with:
• Fragmented care and incomplete information. Patients want
clinicians to see the full picture of their health across settings
to ensure the care and advice they are getting considers their
health conditions and needs uniquely. AI operating over unified
evidence layers can help ensure important conditions and care gaps
are not overlooked.
• Delays and access barriers. AI‑enabled risk stratification and care
gap identification can prioritize high‑risk patients for outreach,
ensuring timelier care and reducing avoidable hospitalizations.
But knowing that healthcare is local, these systems need to
account for regional factors (medical personnel, geographic
isolation, socioeconomic status, and population demographics) and
physician-specific approaches to care.
• Administrative burden and confusion. Patients benefit when AI
reduces clinician documentation burden and back‑office
inefficiencies, freeing more time for communication and shared,
personalized decision‑making.
• Health equity and tailored care. AI can help identify disparities
in access and outcomes and support more targeted interventions,

provided fairness is monitored and addressed. As above, need to
account for regional factors (medical personnel, geographic
isolation, socioeconomic status, and population demographics) and
physician-specific approaches to care.
9.2. Concerns about AI in clinical care
Common concerns include:
• Loss of human judgment and relationship. Patients and caregivers
worry that AI may replace, rather than augment, clinician
judgment. Reveleer deliberately positions EVE as clinical decision
support, with non‑binding recommendations and required human
validation.
• Privacy, data security, and secondary use. There is concern that
AI requires extensive sharing and repurposing of sensitive health
data. Reveleer addresses this with HIPAA‑ and HITRUST‑aligned
controls, de‑identification or redaction where appropriate, and
strict purpose limitations on data use.
• Bias and fairness. Patients may fear that AI will perpetuate or
worsen inequities. Continuous fairness monitoring and transparent
performance metrics by subgroup are essential to mitigate this
risk.
• Opacity and lack of recourse. Without explainability and clear
appeal mechanisms, patients may feel that algorithmic decisions
are unchallengeable. Evidence‑linked AI and human‑in‑the‑loop
workflows provide a foundation for explainable decisions and
remediation.
HHS can support trust by emphasizing evidence‑linked, human‑supervised
AI and promoting patient‑facing transparency about when and how AI is
used in care decisions.

10. Priority AI research areas; literature on costs, benefits, and
transfers
10.1. Priority research areas
Reveleer recommends that HHS prioritize research in:
• Hybrid AI architectures for non‑device clinical decision support.
Comparative studies should evaluate hybrid
agentic‑plus‑deterministic approaches (like EVE Hybrid AI) against
pure generative or black‑box models in terms of accuracy,
auditability, provider acceptance, and regulatory compliance in
risk adjustment and quality workflows.
• AI‑enabled evidence layers and continuous learning systems. Studies
should examine how unified, provenance‑rich evidence layers coupled
with continuous‑learning AI engines affect outcomes, costs, and
resilience of value‑based care programs over multi‑year horizons.
• Digital quality measurement embedded in care. Research should
develop and validate methods for AI to maintain measure‑aligned
quality data and surface in‑encounter opportunities to close gaps,
instead of treating quality reporting as an after‑the‑fact
exercise.
• Human‑AI collaboration and workflow design. Empirical work should
focus on UX patterns, alerting strategies, and feedback loops that
maximize adoption, trust, and safety in AI‑augmented coding and
clinical workflows.
• Equity and fairness in AI‑enabled value‑based care. Research should
investigate how AI affects care across different populations and

payment models, including potential unintended consequences in
risk selection and resource allocation.
10.2. Published findings on impact, costs, benefits, and transfers
The emerging literature on AI in clinical care shows:
• Across multiple clinical domains (e.g., screening, chronic disease
management, imaging), AI interventions have demonstrated strong
cost‑effectiveness or cost‑saving potential, often via reduced
unnecessary procedures, improved early detection, and fewer
readmissions.
• Evaluations of AI‑powered prospective risk and care management have
found improvements in documentation completeness, reduced
readmissions, and better alignment of reimbursement with true
disease burden, while lowering administrative costs through
automation of chart review and suspecting.
• Economic evaluations typically combine clinical outcomes
(sensitivity, specificity, readmission rates, adverse events) with
utilization changes (length of stay, number of procedures,
outpatient versus inpatient mix) to derive budget impact and ROI.
Implementation costs such as integration, training, and model
maintenance are explicitly accounted for, along with indirect
effects on workflow and provider satisfaction.
HHS‑funded research can extend this evidence base by focusing
specifically on non‑device AI in value‑based care, using standardized
frameworks to ensure that comparisons of AI‑assisted and traditional
workflows are fair, transparent, and actionable for regulators and
program designers.
