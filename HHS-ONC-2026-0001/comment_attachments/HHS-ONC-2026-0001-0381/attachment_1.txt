Dear Secretary Kennedy,
Fresenius Medical Care (FME) appreciates the opportunity to comment on the Request for Information
(RFI) from the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Office of the Deputy Secretary in
collaboration with the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy and the Office of the National
Coordinator for Health Information Technology (ASTP/ONC) seeking feedback on policies to accelerate
the adoption of AI in clinical care. FME is the largest integrated supplier in the country of services and
products for individuals undergoing dialysis due to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Operating over 2,600
outpatient renal dialysis facilities, FME provides dialysis services to more than 200,000 people with
kidney failure in the United States each year. In addition, we are the leading dialysis medical supply and
equipment manufacturer, and we develop and deploy technologies that leverage machine learning to
enable real-time care optimizations that streamline care, assist clinicians, and ensure that life-sustaining
dialysis treatments are tailored to each patient.
As a leader in dialysis-related AI technology, we view artificial intelligence as transformative in
improving the quality, safety, and efficiency of care for our patients. For instance, we are actively
exploring AI applications to streamline patient referrals and reduce documentation burden, predict
clinical complications, optimize dialysis treatment outcomes through real-time monitoring, and
accelerate medical research for dialysis care.1 However, the successful integration of these
advancements relies upon the safe deployment of AI tools built on reliable, high-quality data, supported
by clear frameworks that outline the responsibilities among AI users including clinical care teams.
Furthermore, a healthcare ecosystem that fosters the safe adoption of innovative AI requires a
reimbursement infrastructure that appropriately compensates providers and incentivizes manufacturers
to invest in these transformative technologies.

Our responses to HHS’ detailed questions are below.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
Data and Technical Barriers
Incomplete data and technical fragmentation can impede the development and clinical adoption of
dialysis-specific AI technologies. High‑quality, comprehensive training data is essential for developing AI
tools that can reliably detect risk, personalize treatment, and support clinical decision‑making. Because
of the varied nature of dialysis care, longitudinal data for patients with ESRD is dispersed across
nephrology practices, hospitals, skilled nursing facilities, transplant centers, and dialysis clinics, and no
single entity maintains a comprehensive clinical profile. Consequently, AI models trained on isolated EHR
data frequently overlook critical hospitalizations, medication changes, and acute events that
fundamentally drive patient outcomes. This challenge is compounded by a lack of structured data within
EHRs. Essential clinical indicators like patient symptoms, functional status, caregiver support, and
dialysis or medication adherence challenges are often relegated to free-text fields or omitted from EHR
data entirely. Furthermore, limited interoperability between dialysis centers and hospital systems
results in inconsistent or delayed data exchange, frequently leaving providers without access to real-
time information unless they are members of a specific Health Information Exchange (HIE).
Infrastructure gaps also persist within Encounter Notification Service (ENS) feeds, which vary
significantly in quality and timeliness across jurisdictions. Although the frequent follow-up care required
by the ESRD population presents a unique opportunity for coordination, inpatient facilities are not
currently mandated to notify dialysis centers of admissions, which is a stark contrast to the notification
2

requirements already in place for primary care providers.2 Finally, while Fast Healthcare Interoperability
Resources (FHIR) application programming interfaces (APIs) and the United States Core Data for
Interoperability (USCDI) standards are vital for interoperability, they are primarily designed for
mainstream hospital settings and often do not include critical dialysis‑specific data elements. Although
dialysis providers share extensive treatment data through Continuity of Care Documents (CCDs),
hospitals often do not reciprocate because this information falls outside current United States Core Data
for Interoperability (USCDI) standards, undermining the continuity of care. Ensuring that AI is both safe
and clinically valuable will require HHS to support the development and exchange of dialysis‑relevant
data elements that reflect the realities of patient care.
Regulatory and Reimbursement Barriers
Regulatory and reimbursement barriers also limit the development and use of AI in dialysis care. The
ESRD Prospective Payment System (ESRD PPS) has not been modernized to create incentives to adopt
high‑value, safe AI that improves clinical outcomes. Dialysis organizations do not share any savings
generated when AI reduces hospitalizations or improves care coordination. Further, the ESRD Quality
Incentive Program (QIP) functions solely as a penalty program, offering no rewards for clinics that use AI
to drive measurable improvements in patient care. Finally, the fully bundled structure of the ESRD PPS
does not allow for differential reimbursement for AI‑enabled technologies, limiting providers’ ability to
invest in tools that could enhance outcomes, and CMS has likewise not established a reimbursement
framework for software‑as‑a‑service tools within the ESRD payment system, even though similar
mechanisms exist in other areas of Medicare payment policy.
Dialysis relies on standing orders for anemia, fluid management, and mineral bone disease. Introducing
AI algorithms into these pathways raises questions about when a tool qualifies as a medical device and
who is responsible when clinicians act on AI‑supported recommendations. Furthermore, HIEs serve as a
key information highway that dialysis providers rely on to receive timely hospitalization alerts, discharge
summaries, laboratory results, and other clinical updates needed to manage fluid status, infections, and
care transitions. These exchanges also function as valuable repositories of longitudinal clinical data that
can serve as the basis for developing high‑quality AI models tailored to the needs of ESRD patients.
However, constraints on data use beyond treatment, payment, and operations restrict the ability to use
HIE data for AI, research, or broader quality improvement activities. The high cost and uneven access to
HIE and ENS further limit the availability of timely hospitalization data, particularly for smaller hospitals
and dialysis facilities that cannot afford participation fees. These data limitations reduce the
completeness and reliability of the underlying data, making AI tools harder to implement and less
accurate for the ESRD population.
Operational and Workforce Barriers
Operational and workforce barriers also limit the feasibility of AI adoption in dialysis care. Nurse
workload and persistent staffing shortages mean that any AI system adding tasks, alerts, or
documentation requirements is unlikely to be workable in a setting where nurses simultaneously
manage multiple patients throughout treatment. We caution tools that increase cognitive or
administrative burden risk reduction, rather than enhancing, clinical efficiency. Furthermore, physician

2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or
programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
Please provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
Update ESRD Conditions for Coverage to Clarify Role of AI
HHS should update the ESRD Conditions for Coverage to clarify how AI‑enabled tools can be
appropriately integrated into dialysis standing orders and clinical workflows. As noted above, dialysis
care relies heavily on standing orders that guide anemia management, fluid management, and mineral
bone disease treatment, yet current regulations do not address how AI‑generated recommendations fit
within these established protocols. To support safe and effective adoption, HHS should clarify the
circumstances under which AI-enabled tools may be incorporated into standing orders remain within
the scope of required “medical direction,” what documentation and oversight obligations apply when
nursing staff follow AI-assisted protocols, and how to distinguish between clinical decision support tools
and regulated medical devices in dialysis settings. Clear regulatory guidance in these areas would reduce
uncertainty for providers and manufacturers while ensuring that AI is deployed in a manner consistent
with patient safety and existing requirements.
Modernize HIPAA for AI Training and Inference
HIPAA provisions addressing use of identifiable data were not designed to accommodate AI training or
inference and were developed before current AI use cases emerged. The HIPAA Privacy Rule does not
clearly address whether longitudinal data from HIEs or ENS feeds may be used to develop or refine AI
models intended to improve care quality. HHS should update HIPAA guidance to clarify when and under
what circumstance data obtained through HIEs and ENS feeds may be used for AI‑enabled quality
improvement activities, including the development and training of models that support care
coordination, improve clinical outcomes and reduce healthcare costs. HHS should provide clear
guardrails governing de‑identification and use of limited data sets in AI training and inference contexts.
Align Payment Under the ESRD PPS
HHS should align payment incentives under the ESRD Prospective Payment System (PPS) to support the
adoption of AI‑enabled tools that improve clinical outcomes. This includes modifying the ESRD PPS to
allow shared savings for reductions in hospitalizations, emergency department visits, or other
potentially avoidable complications attributable to AI‑enabled care coordination. HHS should also clarify
payment policies for innovative software‑as‑a‑service tools under the ESRD PPS, including those with
embedded AI capabilities, consistent with the progress made in other Medicare payment systems.3

Considerations for ESRD CMMI Models
Future CMMI models for ESRD care should explicitly allow for AI‑supported dialysis interventions that
target key drivers of morbidity and cost. These models should incorporate, for example but not limited
to, the use of AI for early detection of fluid overload, improved anemia management,4 vascular access
creation and maintenance, reduction in intradialytic distress and optimization of hemodiafiltration dose,
enabling more proactive and individualized care. Integrating these capabilities into model design would
support innovation while advancing the goals of improved outcomes and reduced acute care utilization.
CMMI has an opportunity to embed these capabilities into the next generation of ESRD models as the
CKCC model sunsets in 2027.
Medicare Advantage
Recent congressional scrutiny5 6has highlighted plan practices of using AI‑driven prior authorization tools
in ways that may delay or deny medically necessary care, including repeated or premature denials
within the same episode of care, applying opaque internal criteria, and failing to account for changes in
a patient’s condition. We agree with other stakeholders who have called for stronger CMS oversight
over MA plan use of AI to ensure transparency, accountability, and alignment with Medicare coverage
standards so that AI is not used as a tool to further impede access to needed dialysis services.
ASTP/ONC Interoperability Frameworks
HHS should strengthen interoperability requirements to ensure dialysis organizations receive timely,
actionable clinical information needed for safe and coordinated care. This should include requiring
hospitals to provide real‑time Admission, Discharge, and Transfer (ADT) notifications to dialysis
organizations at no cost, establishing standardized ENS data formats and minimum content
requirements, and mandating FHIR‑based APIs that support dialysis‑specific data elements such as
treatment parameters, vascular access type, dry weight, and ultrafiltration rates.

4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and post-
deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered
evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so, which
mechanisms would be most impactful (e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or
prize competitions)?
Promising pre‑deployment evaluation methods for AI tools in dialysis care include multi‑site
retrospective validation across diverse patient populations to assess generalizability and safety;
simulation using historical treatment data (such as ultrafiltration rates, intradialytic hypotension events,7
and hospitalization patterns) to test performance under realistic clinical scenarios; and structured
human‑factors testing with nurses and physicians to evaluate usability, workflow fit, and risks of misuse
or over‑reliance.
Post‑deployment, AI tools should be supported by ongoing monitoring of key clinical and operational
outcomes, including hospitalization rates, intradialytic hypotension, infection rates, and treatment
adherence, along with drift‑detection methods that flag performance changes associated with seasonal
variation, staffing shifts, or changes in patient mix. Regular bias audits across race, ethnicity, disability,
and geography, combined with human‑centered and workflow evaluations that measure, for example,
time saved, clicks reduced, handoffs improved, clinician burden reduction, and patient‑facing outcomes
such as comprehension of AI‑informed decisions, perceived fairness, and trust, are essential.
HHS can accelerate the safe and effective use of AI in dialysis care by supporting rigorous evaluation
across the full lifecycle of these tools. This includes funding targeted grants, cooperative agreements,
contracts, and prize competitions that prioritize multi‑site validation, human‑centered design, and
robust post‑market surveillance in high‑risk populations. HHS can further strengthen the evidence base

A particularly important addition to the evaluation framework is the use of cluster randomized trials
(CRTs) to assess clinical effectiveness and real-world impact. Because dialysis care is delivered in facility-
based settings with shared workflows and team-based decision-making, randomizing at the level of the
dialysis unit (rather than individual patients) is often the most practical and scientifically rigorous
approach. Cluster randomized trials allow evaluation of patient-level outcomes (e.g., intradialytic
hypotension rates, hospitalizations, infection rates, mortality, treatment adherence) while accounting
for workflow integration, clinician behavior, and facility-level variation. They also reduce contamination
bias that can occur when clinicians apply AI-informed practices to both intervention and control patients
within the same unit. Embedding pragmatic CRTs into routine dialysis operations can generate high-
quality causal evidence on safety, equity, effectiveness, and clinician burden in real-world settings,
making them a critical component of deployment evaluation for AI tools in dialysis care.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification, industry-
driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care?
Because the clinical use of AI in ESRD care is still rapidly evolving, HHS should be cautious about
mandating specific certification programs before the underlying standards are mature and
evidence‑based. Premature accreditation and certification requirements could lock in frameworks that
do not yet reflect real‑world clinical workflows, equity considerations, or the unique needs of high‑risk
populations such as ESRD patients.
HHS should instead encourage the development of credible, third‑party AI safety and effectiveness
certification programs tailored to clinical use. These programs should focus on safety within clinician
workflows, transparency of training data and model provenance, bias and equity testing, and reliable
integration with EHR systems. To create meaningful incentives without imposing premature mandates,
HHS can tie certification to preferential scoring in CMMI models and eligibility for shared‑savings
opportunities. Finally, federal guidance should support staged implementation to ensure safe and
sustainable integration.
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the
greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help
reduce costs?
Where AI has Performed Well and Fallen Short
AI tools have performed well in domains where data are structured, abundant, and closely tied to
pattern‑recognition tasks. This includes imaging and signal interpretation, algorithmic dosing and other
forms of optimized clinical decision support, ambient documentation tools that reduce clinician burden,
and operational optimization such as staff or patient scheduling. These applications have generally met
or exceeded expectations because they operate in well‑defined workflows with clear outcome
measures.
7

By contrast, AI has fallen short in areas that depend on complex clinical pathway integration. Even
technically accurate models have had limited clinical impact (most notably for predictive risk scores)
when deployed into fragmented data environments that lack complete, longitudinal, and interoperable
data or workflows that cannot absorb additional alerts. Alert fatigue and workflow misalignment remain
major barriers: tools that are accurate in isolation but poorly integrated into clinical routines are
frequently overridden or ignored, limiting their effectiveness. These issues are exacerbated when the
recommended clinical interventions are not well defined and clinical staff are not trained on how to
interpret or act on the alerts. Moreover, clinicians should have a mechanism to provide feedback on
alert usefulness or accuracy.
High-Potential Future Tools
Several categories of AI hold significant promise for improving outcomes and reducing costs in ESRD
care and other high‑risk populations. High‑value opportunities include tools that integrate claims, EHR,
social determinants, unstructured text, and patient‑reported data to dynamically coordinate
multi‑condition care plans, including social services; AI for polypharmacy and deprescribing that
identifies harmful interactions, unnecessary medications, and personalized deprescribing pathways; and
tools that identify transplant opportunities and flag delays in referral. Additional high‑impact
opportunities include AI that predicts dialysis vascular access failure before thrombosis or infection
occurs,8 and AI‑augmented home and community‑based care models that provide continuous
monitoring and triage for frail, disabled, or high‑risk patients.
7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations have
the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary
administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
Within healthcare organizations, the individuals with the greatest influence over AI adoption include the
CEO, Chief Medical Officer, Chief Clinical Officer, Vice President of Nursing, and leaders in Legal,
Compliance, Privacy, IT, and Cybersecurity.
Key administrative hurdles to AI adoption include lengthy procurement and contracting cycles that slow
the evaluation and onboarding of new tools; limited clinic‑level bandwidth and resources for workflow
integration, training, and ongoing enhancements; and staffing shortages and competing regulatory
burdens that reduce the capacity to adopt and sustain new technologies. Adoption is also constrained
by the need for AI outputs that are straightforward, clinically appropriate, and usable within
nurse-supported, protocol-driven care environments.

9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by the
adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers have
related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
Patients and caregivers consistently express interest in AI tools that help prevent avoidable
hospitalizations, reduce treatment‑related symptoms such as cramps, hypotension, and fatigue, and
improve coordination between dialysis clinics, hospitals, and physician offices. They also want AI to
support home dialysis and transplant by identifying upstream barriers earlier and providing clearer,
actionable guidance. Further, patients value AI that helps deliver meaningful, personalized treatment
plans that reflect their goals, preferences, and day‑to‑day realities.
Patients and caregivers also raise concerns about AI making decisions without their understanding or
consent, and about the potential for bias to influence transplant eligibility or home dialysis
recommendations. Data privacy is a major worry, particularly for vulnerable patients with limited digital
literacy. Similarly, patients express fears that AI systems could misuse their data in ways that affect
insurance coverage or employment. Many patients also express concern that AI could contribute to the
dehumanization of care if not implemented thoughtfully.
10. Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the adoption of
AI as part of clinical care?
HHS should prioritize AI research that directly addresses the highest‑impact clinical and operational
challenges in ESRD care. Key opportunities include AI for fluid management and cardiovascular risk
reduction, AI for infection prevention such as catheter‑ and access‑related infections, and AI that
strengthens home dialysis support and remote monitoring. Additional priorities include AI that improves
transplant referral and evaluation pathways, reduces disparities in ESRD outcomes, and supports
personalized, care for patients.
HHS should also invest in research that evaluates AI effectiveness and safety in real‑world settings
through pragmatic trials and quasi‑experimental designs that measure actual outcome changes rather

effects on workflows, staffing models, burnout, and cost structures, is essential to understanding the
