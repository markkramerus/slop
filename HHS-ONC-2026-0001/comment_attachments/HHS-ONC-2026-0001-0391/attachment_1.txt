Re: Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial
Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care; RIN 0955–AA13
Dear Assistant Secretary Keane and Deputy Secretary O’Neill:
I’m grateful for the opportunity to comment on the Department of Health and Human
Services (HHS), in collaboration with the Office of the Deputy Secretary and Assistant
Secretary for Technology Policy/Office of the National Coordinator for Health
Information Technology (ASTP/ONC), Request for Information on Accelerating the
Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) as part of Clinical Care from the patient,
carepartner, and caregiver perspective as we work towards building safe, effective,
collaborative, and accessible AI in clinical care for all patients and families.
From the perspective of a board-certified patient advocate, the primary carepartner to
two disabled adults, and a patient myself, I have experienced firsthand, personally and
professionally, both the promise and the failures of our healthcare system. In 2008, I
was misdiagnosed with advanced lymphoma. Because I knew to request my medical
records and seek a second opinion, months of testing, invasive procedures, endless
imaging, labs, video-assisted thoracic surgery, and waking up in recovery after surgery
in isolation for suspected tuberculosis ultimately revealed that I did not have cancer, but
rather a fungal infection acquired on my honeymoon that required no treatment, only
monitoring. Looking back, I can’t help but wonder if an AI powered software to read my
radiology could have provided a differential diagnosis that could have prevented months
invasive and traumatic testing and procedures as well as unnecessary, costly care. That
experience shaped my understanding of what is at stake when information,
interpretation, and decision-making tools fail, or succeed, in clinical care.
Executive summary
AI is already shaping clinical decisions across the health system, yet the patients whose
lives those decisions affect are largely excluded from visibility, participation, and

oversight. The greatest barriers to responsible private-sector innovation are not solely
technical or regulatory but rather they are structural: unclear accountability, misaligned
incentives, reimbursement gaps, workforce readiness challenges, privacy concerns, and
insufficient mechanisms for real-world validation and patient partnership. Patients face a
paradox: AI is influencing our care while we often cannot access, understand, or
question the outputs used to make decisions about us.
Large language models and AI powered tools are transforming how patients,
carepartners, and caregivers engage with our health information, interpret diagnoses,
and navigate daily clinical realities while waiting weeks or months for appointments. Our
use of AI represents patient engagement, empowerment, and personalized
operationalization of care decisions in real time. Like any tool, these technologies carry
both promise and risk, which is precisely why patient, carepartner, and caregiver voices
must be embedded not only in listening exercises but in co-creation, co-development,
governance, and funded leadership roles that shape how AI is designed and deployed.
HHS is uniquely positioned to close this trust and governance gap by aligning policy,
payment, evaluation, and transparency expectations so innovation scales alongside
safeguards. Designed, implemented, and governed intentionally with patients as
partners, AI can improve outcomes, reduce costs, strengthen trust, and enable
clinicians to focus on what matters most: caring for people. Healthcare does not occur
only within the four walls of medicine, and neither should the governance, oversight, or
design of the technologies that increasingly shape it.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care
and its adoption and use in clinical care?
After having the privilege of serving on numerous multi-stakeholder efforts and
taskforces on AI in health care, including the National Academy of Medicine’s AI Code of
Conduct project and the Digital Medicine Society’s AI Playbook work, the most
commonly discussed barriers to private sector innovation that I am hearing from various
stakeholders in AI include concerns about regulatory uncertainty, implementation
challenges, lack of reimbursement frameworks, ownership of liability for consequent
harm, privacy concerns, and lack of appropriate workforce and workforce training to
support assessment.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS
regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to
develop or use AI in clinical care? Please provide specific changes and applicable
Code of Federal Regulations citations.

As we carefully define what effective use of AI in clinical care looks like, there are
concerns and problems defined by the patient, carepartner, and caregiver stakeholder
voice that need to be considered as we examine regulatory, payment policy, and
programmatic changes the HHS should prioritize to incentivize collaborative, trusted use
of AI in the healthcare ecosystem.
AI outputs and clinical decision support have been successfully used in patient care for
well over the last decade. Outputs in the form of, for example, risk prediction scores,
diagnostic support outputs, treatment optimization recommendations, early warning
alerts, medication safety intelligence, and the likes of prognostic and trajectory
modelling are routinely used to make informed decisions about patient care.
Building trust and transparency in AI is one of the cornerstone conversation topics in US
and global healthcare sectors. Ironically, patients and families have essentially little
knowledge of and no access to any of the AI intelligence that runs behind the scenes of
our care. AI derived outputs that are used to make decisions about an individual’s care
or coordination of care are not documented in our medical records. Not having access
to AI derived outputs has a broad spectrum of consequences, including but not limited
to:
• robbing us of opportunities to participate in shared decision-making in our or our
loved one’s care using intelligence from AI derived outputs.
• depriving us of clarity about the impact of use of AI in our care on the costs of our
care, and anecdotally, leading to growing concerns about our out-of-pocket costs
increasing as a result of the deployment AI powered technologies, such as
ambient listening.
• preventing us from learning about the transformative power of AI in our care and
outcomes and the celebrating positive impacts AI powered tools can bring;
downstream effects include denying us the opportunities to become trusted
sources of information in evangelizing and educating our families, friends, places
of worship, and communities about the positive impacts of AI.
• shielding us from the inconsistencies, hallucinations, errors, and deviations of AI
outputs that are experienced by our physicians and care teams, preventing us
from fully appreciating the art of medicine; downstream effects include denying
us the opportunities to become trusted sources of information in evangelizing and
educating our families, friends, places of worship, and communities about the
potential harms and negative impacts of AI.
• denying us the opportunities to inspect and curate the outputs and intelligence
generated for potential errors; patients, carepartners, and caregivers can help
assist in reducing “garbage in and garbage out” concerns.

• being routinely excluded, overlooked, and denied opportunities to participate as
partners in the co-design, implementation, and integration of AI tools and
technologies that are going to impact our lives.
As we examine what HHS regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to
augment use of AI our clinical care, I strongly recommend the following considerations.
AI-derived outputs used to make decisions about an individual’s care or coordination of
should be recognized as EHI (45 CFR § 171.102) and hence the designated record set
(45 CFR § 164.501) and consequently, the Right of Access (45 CFR § 164.524) should
apply. Individuals should have a right to access AI outputs as part of our medical record.
Measures should be taken to include access to AI outputs as in scope for
considerations of information blocking (45 CFR Part 171).
Programmatically speaking, patients, carepartners, and caregivers can help advance
the effective use of AI in our care. Listening to patient voices is not enough. Including
patients on stages to talk about our experiences with AI is not enough. Budgeting for
patient stakeholder voice partnership and leadership is necessary as we explore
specific changes that can be implemented programmatically at scale. Currently there
are zero federally patient designated AI governance, engagement and strategy focused
leadership roles. Innovation means doing things a new way. The time is now to fund the
roles that are needed to do the work.
At minimum, patients need to know where AI is used in our care, when it mattered, how
we can interpret it, how it impacts our costs of care, and how to operationalize on the
knowledge obtained. It should be documented as to how our doctors adjudicate
disputes, i.e., model output stands but input corrected, output rejected because of
patient history, output recalculated to include missing patient records.
It is well documented that errors in our medical record are common. The downstream
effects of these rampant errors in our records contribute to outputs that could cause
harm. Patients, carepartners, and caregivers should have a right to not only access AI
outputs used in our care and amend errors in our records, but also address errors in
potential AI outputs used in our care. A feedback loop is missing that can help support
post-deployment monitoring. As a patient, where can I go to report an error on an AI tool
that was implemented in my care? Who do I report that to at my healthcare system or
cancer center? I’ve yet to find a contact or point person or feedback portal.
Standard of care fails or in some cases isn’t even available for patients living with life-
altering, life-limiting conditions, rare disease, or multiple comorbidities. There are

structured, standardized pathways, i.e., Right to Try or Expanded Access
(compassionate use), where patients who have exhausted all approved treatment
options can make an informed decision to try innovative treatment approaches that
have yet to be FDA approved when all standard of care has been exhausted or no
standard of care is available. We currently do not have comparable pathways for AI or
digital health for patients with serious or immediately life-limiting conditions. Innovation
can not only move from top-down. Patients and families are playing proactive roles in
our care and many are pleading for access to various innovations to manage their
condition only to be told by their health system, physician, cancer center, “we don’t do it
that way here.” I strongly encourage colleagues at FDA to explore Right to Try and/
Expanded Access type pathways for patients to access promising AI powered
innovations who are unable to participate in a clinical trial, or where a clinical trial may
not (yet) be available, to access certain investigational digital health treatment or
precision medicine options.
We are currently witnessing a metamorphosis of the doctor-patient relationship.
Programmatically, doctors and care providers need training for engaging with patients,
carepartners, and caregivers who are utilizing AI to drive our informed decision making.
There needs to be programming to also best support and educate patients, our
carepartners and caregivers, on how to best use AI in our care. These necessary
updates in education and training must be co-designed with the patient and family
stakeholder voice. Individuals who share their lived experience and experience to co-
create and reimage physician and nursing training as well as co-creating credible
educational resources to level the playing field for all patients should be fairly
compensated using models such as the National Health Council’s Fair Market Value
calculator or frameworks from PCORI.
We need to compensate and reimburse for the human work and the art of medicine that
ensures AI is safe and effective, including oversight, patient education, and shared
decision-making. We want to be able to discuss AI in our care with the doctors and care
teams we trust with our care. We need to reimagine how we virtually, digitally, and
asynchronously engage in our care. Patients need safe, trusted, secure spaces to
collaborate with our physicians, care teams, community health workers, social workers,
etc., in LLMs. I can invite a patient, carepartner, or caregiver to collaborate in a shared
google doc, but we could be more efficient if I could collaborate within an LLM shared
working space, where everyone can see my prompting, the outputs, the interrogations,
the feedback, the hallucinations and errors instead of me sending screenshots in group
texts. ChatGPT has a feature to start a group chat that in its current state, is useless
and misleadingly named. I am unable to invite my fellow patient advocates, patients,

carepartners, caregivers, or colleagues in medicine into my deep dives on any given
day of patient advocacy work. The time is now to dream bigger.
America’s AI Action plan mentions sandboxes for research and testing. Using patient
and care team-led sandboxes to test payment policies that reimburse clinician time to
validate and interpret results, exploring the impact of shared decision-making
conversations where AI outputs guide choices. Multi-disciplinary reviews, such as
tumor-board style governance for predictive tools, could be implemented as another
sandbox use case. Payment should be paired with minimal documentation standards,
i.e., what output, how it impacted care, shared decision-making, and impact on
outcomes.
3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may
raise novel legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance
and accountability structures (e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy,
and security). What novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if
any, should HHS play to help address them?
Patients, carepartners, and caregivers are encountering a new category of risk in AI-
enabled clinical care: decisions affecting our care may be influenced by systems we
cannot see, question, understand, or collaborate with. The core issue is not whether AI
can be useful, but whether governance has kept pace with deployment. Current legal
and operational frameworks do not clearly define responsibility when AI contributes to
clinical decision-making, how errors should be investigated, or how patients can
challenge outputs derived from incorrect or incomplete data. Privacy concerns are
equally urgent. AI development and deployment rely on extensive health data flows that
patients often cannot easily track or meaningfully understand, creating legitimate
concerns about misuse, secondary use, and security vulnerabilities. HHS is uniquely
positioned to close these governance gaps by setting consistent national expectations
for transparency, documentation, and accountability when AI informs clinical care.
Patients should be able to know when AI influenced a decision, understand the basis for
its output, and access mechanisms to question or correct it when necessary. Federal
leadership is essential to ensure that innovation scales alongside safeguards so that
advances in clinical AI strengthen patient trust rather than undermine it.
4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods
(pre- and post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and
human- centered evaluation methods for clinical care? Should HHS further
support these processes? If so, which mechanisms would be most impactful
(e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or prize competitions)?

From the patient and care partner perspective, HHS should ensure that non-device AI
used in clinical care is evaluated across its lifecycle for real-world safety, equity,
transparency, and usability, and technical accuracy through standardized pre-
deployment validation, post-deployment monitoring, and robustness testing. Patient-
partnered sandbox environments described above can be impactful and should be
strongly considered. There should be clear disclosure in clinical workflows, especially in
patient-facing disclosure when AI informs care decisions. CMS should align payment
and quality programs to support validated, monitored AI and the clinician time required
for safe use, oversight, patient education, and shared decision-making.
HHS could support real-world AI evaluation networks with patients as funded partners
by prioritizing this work within existing appropriations and authorities. HHS could
prioritize cooperative agreements and grants to build real-world evaluation networks
with patients as funded partners and co-creators, supplemented by targeted contracts
and prize competitions to accelerate techquity, explainability, and safety testing. CMS
demonstration and innovation models could fund real-world testing through CMMI pilots,
alternative payment models, i.e. ACCESS, and quality-reporting incentives to move
evaluations real-world practice. If patients are to be meaningfully placed in charge of
our care, federal policy must support us with the tools to evaluate the technologies
shaping clinical decisions about us and our lives. A dedicated funding stream should be
established for patient-driven AI evaluation networks to ensure empowerment is paired
with evidence, accountability, and safety.
HHS should align AI policy with established federal patient-engagement frameworks
(e.g., PCORI, FDA PFDD, AHRQ models) that treat patients, carepartners, and
caregivers as end-users and partners in design, evaluation, and oversight, while fairly
compensating individuals for their time, expertise, and lived experiences.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation,
certification, industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative
and effective AI use in clinical care?
From the patient and care partner perspective, HHS can best support private-sector
accreditation, certification, testing, and credentialing efforts for clinical AI by establishing
clear national expectations for transparency, safety monitoring, and real-world
performance while requiring meaningful patient, carepartner, and caregiver involvement
in design, evaluation, implementation, and governance. HHS should align payment,
certification, and procurement policies to incentivize adoption of independent validation
programs that assess not only technical accuracy but also techquity, usability, patient

comprehension, and metrics that include patient perspectives on value. Federal
agencies can further accelerate responsible innovation by supporting public-private-
patient testing collaboratives and publishing model evaluation criteria that embed
patient stakeholder participation as a core standard. Trustworthy clinical AI is built not
only on strong algorithms, but on structured inclusion of the patient, carepartner, and
caregiver voices it is designed to serve.
6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance
and cost expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI
tools would have the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give
new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
AI tools have demonstrated the greatest real-world impact when they address access
gaps, reduce administrative burden, or enable earlier clinical action within patient care
workflows. Some examples of AI tools that were deployed and met or exceeded in
clinical care, performance, and cost expectations include:
• Autonomous diabetic retinopathy screening in primary care
FDA-authorized autonomous AI systems for diabetic retinopathy have
demonstrated accurate detection of referable disease in primary care settings,
enabling point-of-care identification and referral without requiring immediate
specialist interpretation, which can help close screening gaps where specialty
access is limited.
• Ambient clinical documentation tools
Ambient listening has been documented to demonstrate meaningful reductions in
clinician documentation time and administrative burden as well as improving
clinician well-being.
• Hospital deterioration prediction models
AI-driven early-warning models analyzing EHR data in real time have reduced
adverse events by identifying clinical decline earlier and enabling earlier clinical
intervention.
• 4. AI clinical decision support for unnecessary testing (SmartAlert system)
Significant volume reductions of repeat laboratory testing demonstrating cost
savings through eliminating low-value care.
With respect to novel tools that could have the greatest potential for improving
healthcare outcomes comes to mind a Waze for Healthcare that harnesses the power
multi-stakeholder data intelligence and crowd-sourced peer health support in real-time
to help patients make informed decisions about the next steps of their care journeys
where they are.

Patients and families are currently experiencing harm from automated or AI-assisted
payer systems used to process claims at scale, including batch denials and algorithm-
supported downcoding of reimbursements. When coverage or payment determinations
are automated but correction pathways remain slow, opaque, or difficult to access,
patients can face delayed care, unexpected costs, and administrative barriers that
directly affect health outcomes. Oversight, transparency, and clear accountability
standards must keep pace with automated decision tools so that efficiency gains do not
come at the expense of patient access, provider sustainability, or medically necessary
care.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel
research, and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider
specific data types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
The greatest opportunities for AI advancement lie where patient data still remains
siloed. Interoperability that connects clinical records, claims, imaging, labs, genomics,
wearable data, and patient-reported outcomes would dramatically expand research
capability, improve model accuracy, and allow real-world validation across diverse
populations as well as enable robust patient-navigation technologies. Standardized
formats and terminologies such as FHIR, USCDI, LOINC, and SNOMED CT make it
possible for systems to exchange information safely and meaningfully, while
benchmarking infrastructure and shared evaluation datasets enable transparent
performance comparisons. At the same time, new or expanded standards are needed to
support consistent exchange, interpretation, and documentation of AI-derived outputs,
such as risk scores, predictions, recommendations, confidence levels, and model
provenance, so that these outputs can move across systems, be understood by
clinicians and patients, and be evaluated for safety and accuracy over time. Patients
experience the consequences of fragmented data every day; when our information
cannot move, innovation cannot scale. Interoperability is therefore not only a technical
priority but a patient safety and equity imperative.
9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see
addressed by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns
do patients and caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical
care?
Patients and caregivers want AI in clinical care to address persistent system failures
and gaps in care that routinely negatively impact our outcomes, including delayed or
missed diagnoses, fragmented care coordination, administrative burden that limits our
clinician’s time, inequitable access to specialty expertise, preventable medical errors,

and financial toxicity. Tools that accelerate time to diagnosis, close care gaps, identify
risk earlier, reduce patient administrative burden, streamline treatment planning and
clinical trial navigation are examples of solutions viewed as high-value because they
improve both safety and the lived experience of our or our loved one’s care. At the
same time, patients and caregivers have significant concerns about the adoption of AI,
particularly when its role in decision-making is not transparent, when outputs cannot be
accessed, reviewed, or discussed, when systems may encode bias or worsen
disparities and gaps in care, or when accountability is unclear if harm occurs. Duty of
care and do no harm are key. There is also concern that poorly implemented AI could
increase cognitive burden on clinicians, generate alert fatigue, or shift decision
responsibility onto patients without adequate support. Many patients do not have a
primary carepartner or caregiver, may not be digitally savvy or may have language and
accessibility need. Patients consistently emphasize that trust depends on clear
disclosure of when AI is used, understandable explanations of how it informs care, the
ability to correct or challenge outputs, and assurance that human clinical judgment
remains responsible for final decisions. Patients do not oppose AI in care; we oppose
invisible, unaccountable AI that affects our health, our care, and our lives without our
knowledge or voice.
As we critically examine the concerns of the patient stakeholder voice, I strongly
encourage all of HHS agencies to read the AI Rights for Patients. This document was
created by a cohort convened by The Light Collective, a national community of patient
activists, clinicians, and health data experts committed to building tech and digital rights
for patients. There are no assumptions of what patients want in this rights framework as
this was fully co-created with 100% patient voices.
Closing
I applaud HHS's leadership in advancing responsible AI adoption in patient care. The
patient, carepartner, caregiver, and consumer stakeholder perspectives are critically
important in the advancement of responsible adoption of AI in patient care. Patient care
happens not only within the four walls of medicine, but also in the privacy of our homes,
places of work, worship, and community, and play.
I applaud HHS’s leadership in advancing responsible AI adoption in clinical care and for
recognizing that the voices of patients, carepartners, caregivers, and consumers are
essential to getting this right. Thank you for the opportunity to contribute to this
important national conversation. Patients are not asking to slow innovation, we are
asking to strengthen it. We want to help build an AI-enabled health system that is safer,
more transparent, more equitable, and more humane than the one we have today. I
urge the Department to build on this moment by ensuring that patient partnership is not
symbolic but structural, embedded in policy design, evaluation, governance, and
funding priorities. With intentional policy, meaningful partnership, and shared
accountability, AI can fulfill its promise not just as a technological advance but as a

public good. Patients, carepartner, and caregivers stand by ready to partner, to
collaborate, and lead with our lived experiences and expertise. The question is not
whether we are prepared to lead alongside you, but whether the system is ready to
support and prioritize authentic partnership.
