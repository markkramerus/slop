Dear Secretary Kennedy and Principal Deputy Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy Posnack,
On behalf of Veradigm, we appreciate the opportunity to comment on HHS’s Request for Information
regarding actions the federal government can take to accelerate the adoption of artificial intelligence
(AI) in clinical care. Veradigm has a proven history of delivering clinical and revenue cycle solutions that
help independent practices improve outcomes and financial performance. Our AI-forward data and
technology also help over 20,000 provider practices connect with health plans and biopharma to
eliminate inefficiencies, close care gaps, and create a more affordable health system. These
organizations play an essential role in American healthcare and often face distinct constraints,
including limited staff, limited capital for digital transformation, and a disproportionately high
administrative burden, which must be directly considered in any federal strategy to support scaled,
responsible clinical AI adoption and use.
We believe AI has the potential to meaningfully improve patient care, clinician workload, and financial
sustainability, but only when it is deployed with the right guardrails, supported by coherent regulatory
expectations, and accessible to providers regardless of size or location. For this reason, Veradigm
strongly supports a clear, harmonized federal framework for clinical AI that recognizes the unique
operational realities of small, rural, and independent practices. Our comments reflect real-world
implementation experience and align with our internal governance approach to AI at Veradigm. In
general, our comments accentuate and expand on five (5) core themes:
1. We support adoption of a consistent, risk-based regulatory approach that differentiates
high-risk clinical workflows. AI use cases in healthcare range from administrative automation
to high-risk diagnostic support. Regulation should be proportionate to potential patient harm,

focusing the highest scrutiny on tools that directly influence diagnosis or treatment, while
enabling rapid innovation in low-risk domains. We recommend the use of established risk
analysis frameworks and clearly defined tiers and applicability thresholds.
2. Preserve clinical judgment and human-in-the-loop safeguards. AI should augment, not
replace, clinical judgment. Core safeguards should include transparency standards related to
model purpose, intended use, performance bounds, limitations, and training data
characteristics. We also support design expectations that retain human decision-making in
high-risk workflows and preserve clinician autonomy to override or dismiss AI
recommendations when workflows are more automated or lower risk.
3. Support practical and scalable governance expectations for providers and developers.
Governance expectations must be scalable to diverse care settings, from large health systems
to small independent practices. We recommend flexible compliance pathways, such as
simplified oversight structures for small practices; alignment of developer requirements with
standard software development lifecycle practices; and harmonized federal guidance to reduce
fragmentation across states and agencies.
4. Promote transparency without creating new liability exposure or barriers to innovation.
Developers should be responsible for transparency and sound development practices; clinicians
remain responsible for clinical use. HHS should clarify that transparency expectations do not
reassign clinical liability and consider safe-harbor provisions for organizations that implement
recognized best practices such as external validation, bias monitoring, limitation disclosures,
and post-deployment monitoring. De-identified, aggregate analysis of AI performance and
overrides should be encouraged and/or required for oversight and/or quality improvement
initiatives.
5. Strengthen federal investments in AI research, benchmarking, and post-deployment
monitoring. To accelerate safe adoption, HHS should prioritize applied research on model drift
detection and calibration, shared benchmarking datasets and evaluation methods, and public–
private partnerships to study real-world performance, fairness, and usability in clinical
workflows.
RFI Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
From Veradigm’s perspective, the most significant barriers fall into several related categories:
regulatory ambiguity, governance expectations that do not reflect the realities of resource constrained
providers, misaligned payment incentives, and workflow readiness challenges.
2

Independent and rural providers are heavily affected by regulatory fragmentation, as they seldom have
compliance teams capable of interpreting varying state and federal frameworks. Uncertainty about
how existing oversight applies to any use of AI in a practice often results in delayed decisions, even
when AI has clear clinical value. A harmonized federal posture that differentiates between device and
non-device AI, and that scales requirements to risk and organizational capacity, would substantially
reduce hesitation among small providers. The deregulation efforts in the proposed HTI-5 rule that
remove source attribute and model card requirements from Certified Health IT create uncertainty for
both vendors and providers around what kind of information to expect and how to compare the
various AI tools and functionality that might be available. This uncertainty increases risk for developers
and clinicians evaluating clinical decision support tools.
In addition, Veradigm has observed that governance frameworks proposed in various policy discussions
often assume the presence of cross functional oversight committees or formalized validation processes
that small practices simply cannot support. While robust governance is essential, prescriptive
structures risk creating barriers for precisely the organizations that could benefit most from burden
reducing tools. Rightsizing expectations for deployers based on organizational scale and the risk profile
of the AI tool would advance adoption while protecting patient safety. Payment model misalignment
presents another barrier. Independent and rural practices operate on thin margins, making it difficult
to invest in even high value AI tools without a clear path to recoup the investment. Payment model
reform that could meaningfully impact the use of AI is especially critical to incentivize the adoption and
use of AI for independent practices.
RFI Question 2: What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations,
policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care?
Veradigm encourages HHS to establish a formal federal framework tailored specifically to high-risk
clinical AI, one that supports innovation while ensuring patient safety and preserving clinical judgment.
Such a framework should provide clear expectations for transparency, documentation, risk
assessment, and lifecycle monitoring while explicitly scaling requirements to reflect organizational size.
This is particularly important for small and independent practices that cannot absorb the cost of large
governance programs.
HHS can also accelerate adoption by harmonizing oversight across agencies including ASTP/ONC, FDA,
and CMS, reducing administrative complexity and creating predictable expectations for developers and
providers alike. This harmonization should include consistent definitions, align documentation
requirements, and coordinate expectations for intended use statements, monitoring practices, and
safety claims.
Equally critical is payment modernization. CMS should consider add-on payments, AI specific credits
within quality programs, and pathways in alternative payment models that reward demonstrated
clinical value, burden reduction, or cost savings. Clear reimbursement signals are necessary for small
and rural providers who otherwise cannot justify the upfront investment.

RFI Question 3: For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel
legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures
(e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel legal and
implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to address them?
The ongoing development of clinical AI that is considered Non-Device CDS by FDA introduces new
questions related to liability, contracting, data use under HIPAA, and end user readiness. For
independent and rural providers that lack legal and compliance resources, this uncertainty can pose a
significant adoption barrier.
A key challenge is the allocation of responsibility among developers, implementers, and clinicians when
AI recommendations inform care decisions. Because these tools do not fall under FDA’s traditional
regulatory structure, EHR vendors like Veradigm look to requirements for Certified Health IT that clarify
obligations around clinical decision support tools that are embedded in the EHR. The recent FDA
Guidance for Industry and Food and Drug Administration Staff, published on January 29, 2026, related
to clinical decision support in non-medical devices provides clear and meaningful examples of
functionality that falls in and outside of the FDA purview, and is helpful in terms of understanding what
may be outside the scope of FDA oversight, but there remains a gap in terms of the specifics of
documentation and information that must be provided to clinicians in order for them to be considered
fully informed about a tool and/or how it was developed. We welcome more certainty on the
obligations of EHR vendors who are developing or deploying AI, as well as additional guidance that can
provide clarity on novel AI workflows and functionality to ensure uniform understanding of the
obligations developers have to end users.
HHS could further support responsible adoption by convening stakeholders to develop model liability
frameworks and safe-harbor approaches for documenting AI-informed decisions, ensuring
accountability aligns with real-world roles. We encourage any stakeholder group to include
independent physician practices and ambulatory EHR developers to ensure adequate representation of
the specific nuances of AI use in these care settings.
Additionally, there remains ambiguity around permissible uses of deidentified and limited data sets for
AI development and monitoring under HIPAA. HHS clarification of allowable pathways, coupled with
encouragement of privacy preserving methods such as federated learning, would help providers
support AI safety without compromising patient privacy.
Finally, the move toward AI augmented workflows raises questions related to training, credentialing,
and scope of practice. Alignment across CMS participation requirements, HRSA workforce programs,
and ONC safety expectations would provide much needed clarity for organizations with limited training
resources.
Question 4: For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods (pre- and
post-deployment), metrics, robustness testing, and other workflow and human-centered evaluation
methods for clinical care? Should HHS further support these processes? If so, which mechanisms
would be most impactful (e.g., contracts, grants, cooperative agreements, and/or prize
competitions).
4

Evaluation of non-device AI tools must include a combination of technical validation and real-world
workflow assessment. Before deployment, developers and providers should define intended use,
validate models across representative populations and conduct human factors testing in realistic EHR
workflows. After deployment, monitoring for drift, calibration, disparities, and user trust should
continue, but in a way that small practices can feasibly manage. We advocate for flexible regulations
that allow institutions, both developers and deployers, to develop safeguards that align with
organizational priorities, capabilities and the commensurate risk of the AI functionality that is being
developed and/or used. Overly prescriptive pre- and post-deployment testing runs the risk of stifling
innovation and adding unnecessary burden for low-risk AI tools that have the potential to improve
administrative burden for provider practices.
HHS can support consistency and feasibility by funding shared evaluation frameworks, national
benchmarks, and transparent reporting templates that organizations of all sizes can adopt.
Question 5: How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification,
industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical
care?
Some independent practices, including many Veradigm customers, are already developing internal
governance and safety frameworks. However, these efforts are uneven and can be difficult for small
providers to navigate and maintain. HHS recognition of voluntary accreditation and certification
models, aligned with federal principles, would strengthen these frameworks and create more uniform
expectations across the market. Requiring uniform and standardized documentation requirements
would also promote consistency, transparency and trust.
Question 7: Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations
have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary administrative
hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
Across the healthcare system, decisions about adopting AI tools typically rest with clinical leadership, IT
departments, compliance teams, and quality and safety committees or some combination thereof.
However, in independent physician practices, small provider groups, and many rural health systems,
these responsibilities are often consolidated into one or two individuals who are already managing
significant clinical and administrative workload. This creates a structural barrier to evaluating,
procuring, and safely implementing new AI tools.
HHS can play a pivotal role in reducing these hurdles by establishing clear, scalable federal
documentation and governance expectations that small practices can implement without requiring
dedicated committees or specialized staff. In addition, HHS could encourage or standardize pilot/test
environments that allow smaller practices to evaluate AI safely without committing immediately to full
deployment, something many decisionmakers view as essential but often lack the infrastructure to
support. HHS could also align CMS program requirements and procurement expectations, reducing
uncertainty around what evidence or documentation a practice must maintain when adopting AI.

Finally, federal support for simple, EHR embedded guidance, implementation toolkits, and workflow
ready templates would materially aid independent practices that rely on vendors for practical, turnkey
solutions that fit within existing budget cycles and operational constraints.
Question 8: Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and
accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data types, data
standards, and benchmarking tools.
AI’s effectiveness depends heavily on consistent, high-quality data, yet many practices lack access to
the robust interoperability infrastructure that large systems use. HHS can address this by continuing to
advance consistent, enforceable API requirements and continuing to advance data elements supported
by USCDI ensuring that AI developers and clinicians can rely on predictable and complete data sources.
To further support responsible AI adoption, HHS should accelerate expansion of data elements
represented in USCDI and specialty or domain specific data elements such as those in USCDI+ that
reflect real clinical needs for AI use cases. Additionally, HHS can drive equity and transparency by
funding and maintaining national benchmarking criteria and evaluation datasets and enabling
providers of all sizes to compare model performance with consistent criteria. These steps would
significantly strengthen model reliability, external validation, and fairness while reducing the burden
on small practices that cannot independently generate or curate large-scale evaluation datasets or
domain-specific data elements that reflect real clinical needs.
Question 9: What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed by
the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and caregivers have
related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
For clinicians, particularly those in independent practices, meeting patient expectations for
transparency and privacy expectations when AI is used at the point of care requires guidance,
educational resources, and clarity about communication obligations.
HHS can support these needs by developing plain language, patient-facing educational materials
explaining when and how AI may be used, what protections should be in place, how privacy is
safeguarded, and what recourse or options patients have if they have questions or concerns. Federal
leadership could also establish baseline expectations for transparency and clinician communication,
giving providers, especially those with limited staff, clear, accessible resources that build trust without
adding undue administrative burden. Additionally, HHS can reinforce patient confidence by clarifying
how existing privacy protections apply to AI workflows and by supporting common sense safety, and
performance monitoring requirements that reassure patients their care remains under appropriate
oversight.
Question 10: Are there specific areas of AI research that HHS should prioritize to accelerate the
adoption of AI as part of clinical care?
AI research has historically focused on algorithmic performance in controlled settings, but widespread
and equitable adoption requires deeper understanding of how AI performs in the real world, especially
in diverse practice settings such as independent clinics and rural communities. HHS can address this
6

need by funding longitudinal real-world performance and safety studies that assess calibration, drift,
disparities, and clinical outcomes across different care environments. This is critical for providers who
lack the data science capacity to independently evaluate these issues.
HHS should also invest in economic impact analyses that quantify how AI affects clinician workload,
administrative burden, operational efficiency, and cost of care; evidence that is especially important
for small practices with limited financial margin. Finally, HHS can advance understanding of human–AI
teaming and workflow integration, providing evidence-based guidance on expectations for AI systems
that meaningfully support clinicians rather than adding new burdens. These federal research
investments would create a stronger foundation for payment policy, regulatory expectations, and
industry best practices.
Conclusion
Veradigm appreciates HHS’s leadership in developing a forward-thinking and equitable approach to
clinical AI. With harmonized expectations, scalable governance, strengthened transparency, support
for privacy preserving data use, and modernized payment pathways, HHS can create an environment
where AI improves outcomes, reduces burden, and enhances sustainability across all care settings.
We stand ready to collaborate with HHS to support the safe, effective, and equitable deployment of AI
nationwide.
