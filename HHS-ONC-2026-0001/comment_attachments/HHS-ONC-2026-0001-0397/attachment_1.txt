Background and Experience

cleared, AI-enabled imaging technologies for breast, lung, prostate, and other cancers—conditions that
collectively account for approximately 70 percent of cancers amenable to imaging-based screening.
AI-enabled imaging technologies already are improving patient care today. With the appropriate
regulatory clarity, reimbursement alignment, and data infrastructure, AI can significantly improve
diagnostic accuracy, reduce unnecessary downstream utilization, lower costs, and expand access to
high-quality care. Case in point, optimizing AI technology is essential to bridge the gap between the
demand for imaging and the supply of radiologists, especially those with the appropriate subspeciality
training. Rather than pushing studies out further and further which delays care and contributes to
burnout, the future potential of AI is to make the radiologists (and radiological technologists) we do
have able to work faster, smarter, and more accurately. The earlier disease detection that flows from
the above comments will benefit our healthcare system by becoming far more accessible and less
expensive.

RadNet’s Comments on Specific Issues and Questions from the RFI
I. Regulation
RadNet supports predictable, risk-based oversight without creating duplicative requirements.
AI-enabled clinical technologies—particularly AI incorporated into medical devices—are already
subject to rigorous federal oversight. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has established a
mature, risk-based regulatory framework for AI-enabled medical devices, including premarket review,
post-market surveillance, and requirements related to safety, effectiveness, and performance across
diverse populations. ONC and ASTP should avoid creating duplicative or conflicting regulatory regimes
that would add uncertainty, slow innovation, or discourage provider adoption. Instead, HHS should:
 Reinforce reliance on FDA’s existing authority for AI-enabled clinical tools that meet the definition
of a medical device.
 Avoid duplicative or overlapping regulatory regimes that would create confusion or conflicting
requirements for developers and providers.
 Promote inter-agency alignment, ensuring that ONC policies related to health IT, interoperability,
and transparency complement—rather than complicate—FDA oversight.
 Ensure regulatory requirements are proportionate to risk, recognizing that most imaging AI tools
function as clinical decision support intended to assist clinicians, not replace them.
Over-regulation or fragmented oversight risks delaying patient access to AI tools that are already
demonstrating measurable improvements in diagnostic accuracy, efficiency, and outcomes—contrary
to the goals articulated in this RFI.
II. Reimbursement
Payment policy should align with clinical value to drive adoption.
Reimbursement remains one of the most significant barriers to the adoption of clinically validated AI
technologies. Developing, validating, and maintaining high-performing healthcare AI is resource-
intensive. Absent clear and predictable reimbursement pathways, providers—particularly
independent imaging centers and community-based practices—will be slow or unable to adopt these
technologies, regardless of their clinical value.
A prime example, the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule (MPFS) is particularly challenging for AI
innovators. CMS previously had considered most AI services to be a physician office “indirect practice
expense,” akin to an overhead cost like a computer system or rent for office space. However, many, if
not most, AI services are provided to patients on a per-patient, per-service basis, which is best
accounted for by a per-use payment, the same way that other medical services are valued and paid.
Payment for AI-enabled medical technology under the MPFS is particularly difficult because CMS does

 Encourage coverage policies that recognize downstream savings, including reductions in

while reducing false positives and unnecessary callbacks—benefits that accrue to patients, providers,
and payers. Payment policy should reflect these system-level efficiencies, consistent with the
objectives outlined in this RFI.
III. Research & Development
Enable Responsible Access to High-Quality Data.
Access to large, diverse, and high-quality datasets is essential to the development of safe, effective,
and unbiased healthcare AI. Radiology has been a leader in this area due to widespread digitization,
PACS adoption, and adherence to the DICOM standard. However, even in imaging, AI development
would be significantly enhanced by improved access to relevant clinical context, such as pathology
results, surgical history, and longitudinal outcomes.
RadNet recommends that HHS:
 Promote interoperability and standardized data exchange across care settings, enabling AI tools
to incorporate relevant clinical context.
 Balance privacy protections with innovation, ensuring policies do not unnecessarily restrict the
use of appropriately de-identified data for AI training, validation, and monitoring.
 Encourage real-world performance evaluation, allowing AI tools to be assessed and improved
based on how they function in diverse clinical environments.

IV. Specific Questions in the RFI
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption
and use in clinical care?
The most significant barriers to private sector innovation and adoption of AI in clinical care
include:
 Regulatory uncertainty and fragmentation, particularly when multiple HHS agencies consider
overlapping requirements for clinical AI already regulated by FDA.
 Lack of predictable reimbursement pathways, even for FDA-cleared AI tools with
demonstrated clinical benefit.
 Limited access to high-quality, representative clinical data, particularly longitudinal outcomes
and non-imaging clinical context.
 Interoperability gaps that prevent AI tools from integrating seamlessly into clinical workflows.
 Administrative burden on providers, including contracting, IT integration, and compliance
obligations that disproportionately affect community-based providers.
Absent clarity and alignment across regulation, payment, and infrastructure, even high-performing
AI tools face slow and uneven adoption.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What regulations, policies, or programs
should be revisited?
HHS should prioritize policies that reduce uncertainty, avoid duplication, and align incentives
with clinical value, including:
 Regulatory Priorities
o Deference to FDA oversight for AI-enabled medical devices, including diagnostic imaging
AI, which are already subject to risk-based review and post-market surveillance.
o Avoidance of duplicative ONC certification or disclosure requirements that overlap with
FDA authorities.
o Clear delineation of ONC’s role around interoperability, transparency, and data
exchange—not clinical performance validation.
 Payment Policy Priorities

3. For non-medical devices, what novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role should
HHS play?
For non-medical device AI used in clinical care—such as workflow optimization, triage, scheduling,
or operational decision support—novel issues include:
 Unclear liability and accountability frameworks, particularly when AI influences but does not
determine clinical decisions.
 Privacy and data security concerns, especially where AI tools rely on large-scale aggregation
of clinical and operational data.
 Contractual complexity, including indemnification and risk allocation between providers and
AI developers.
HHS can play a constructive role by:
 Providing guidance on governance and accountability structures for non-device AI.
 Promoting best practices for procurement, contracting, and risk management.
 Ensuring privacy and security requirements are clear, consistent, and technology-neutral.
4. For non-medical devices, what evaluation methods and metrics are most promising? Should HHS
support these processes?
Promising evaluation approaches for non-medical device AI include:
 Pre-deployment validation using real-world clinical workflows, not artificial test
environments.
 Post-deployment monitoring of performance drift, user interaction, and workflow impact.
 Human-centered evaluation, assessing how clinicians interact with AI outputs and incorporate
them into decision-making.

 Targeted grants or cooperative agreements focused on real-world evaluation methodologies.

5. How can HHS best support private sector activities such as accreditation, certification, and
industry-driven testing?
HHS should support—but not replace—private sector efforts by:
 Recognizing industry-led validation and accreditation programs where appropriate.
 Avoiding mandatory federal certification regimes that could crowd out innovation or
duplicate FDA oversight.
 Encouraging voluntary, outcome-focused standards that promote quality while allowing
flexibility.
Private sector leadership has been critical to progress in radiology IT and AI, and similar models
should be encouraged for broader clinical AI adoption.
6. Where have AI tools met or fallen short of expectations? What tools show the greatest potential?
AI tools have met or exceeded expectations in areas such as:
 Imaging-based cancer screening, where AI improves sensitivity, reduces false positives, and
supports earlier diagnosis.
 Workflow optimization, including worklist prioritization and operational efficiency.
They have fallen short where:
 Integration into clinical workflows is poor, or
 Payment and infrastructure barriers prevent consistent use.
AI tools with the greatest future potential include those that:
 Support early disease detection and risk stratification,
 Reduce unnecessary utilization, and
 Improve consistency and quality at scale.

7. Who influences AI adoption within health care organizations and what are the primary
administrative hurdles?
AI adoption is influenced by:
 Clinical leadership (e.g., radiologists and other physicians),
 Health system executives, and
 IT and compliance teams.
Key administrative hurdles include:
 Contracting and procurement complexity,
 Integration with existing IT systems,
 Unclear reimbursement and return-on-investment calculations, and
 Regulatory and compliance uncertainty.
Reducing these hurdles would materially accelerate adoption.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability accelerate AI development and adoption?
Enhanced interoperability would significantly advance AI adoption by enabling access to:
 Longitudinal clinical outcomes data,
 Pathology and laboratory results linked to imaging, and
 Standardized metadata and annotations.
Radiology’s success with PACS and DICOM demonstrates how standardized data exchange can fuel
innovation. Expanding similar interoperability across clinical domains would widen market
opportunities and improve AI performance.
9. What challenges do patients and caregivers want AI to address, and what concerns do they have?
Patients and caregivers want AI to:
 Enable earlier and more accurate diagnoses,
 Reduce unnecessary testing and delays, and
 Improve access to high-quality care.
Concerns include:
 Data privacy and security and
 Bias and fairness.

Bias in AI is a legitimate concern, but it is not unique to AI—and it is best addressed through
robust validation, diverse training data, and real-world performance monitoring. FDA already
requires evidence that AI-enabled medical devices perform safely and effectively across
diverse populations. In radiology, large-scale datasets drawn from varied geographic, racial,
and socioeconomic populations are critical to building trustworthy algorithms
These concerns are best addressed through performance transparency, robust validation, and
clinician oversight.
10. Are there specific areas of AI research HHS should prioritize?
HHS should prioritize research that:
 Evaluates real-world clinical performance and outcomes,
 Assesses longitudinal impact on quality and costs,
 Supports post-market learning and continuous improvement, and
 Preparing for increasingly autonomous AI.
Today, most AI tools support clinician decision-making. However, as evidence grows and trust
increases, some AI applications—particularly in high-volume, low-risk screening contexts—
may become partially or fully autonomous.
When AI is proven to perform as well as or better than human experts, policymakers should
be open to revisiting existing regulatory and payment frameworks to ensure they do not
become barriers to access, innovation, or efficiency.
(a) Published Findings
The literature increasingly demonstrates that AI can improve diagnostic accuracy and efficiency,
particularly in imaging-based screening and triage, when appropriately validated and deployed.
Studies have shown that general radiologists using AI technologies perform as well as or better
than specialty-trained ones without AI, which raises the standard of care particularly in
underserved communities.
(b) Costs, Benefits, and Transfers
Existing studies show that AI often shifts costs upfront but generates downstream savings
through earlier detection, reduced utilization, and improved care coordination—benefits that
current payment models do not fully capture.
