Cedar Cares, Inc. (“Cedar”) appreciates the opportunity to respond to HHS’s
Request for Information on Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial
Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care. Cedar strongly supports the Administration’s
goal of positioning the United States as a global leader in artificial intelligence
innovation while removing unnecessary regulatory barriers that impede responsible
deployment of AI across healthcare.
Cedar is a healthcare technology company dedicated to improving the healthcare
financial experience. Founded in 2016, Cedar’s platform unifies patient billing,
payments, insurance coverage, and support into a single solution —driving improved
results for healthcare providers and better financial journeys for patients. Cedar
helps patients access the coverage, aid, and payment options they need with
confidence. To date, Cedar has supported more than 50 million patients nationwide.
Cedar’s platform uses AI and machine learning to help solve the growing complexity
of patient financial engagement and deliver personalization at scale. Our experience
deploying AI-enabled tools in regulated healthcare environments informs the
comments below.
I. Regulatory and Programmatic Changes to Incentivize AI Adoption
Cedar encourages HHS to prioritize regulatory clarity, interoperability reforms, and
targeted modernization efforts that enable AI tools to access high-quality data in a
predictable and scalable manner. In many cases, barriers to AI adoption stem from

fragmented data access, artificial interoperability barriers created by incumbents,
duplicative administrative requirements, and uncertainty regarding permissible
automated access under existing rules.
HHS can accelerate responsible AI deployment by:
● Reinforcing that automated and AI-enabled access to health information via
standardized APIs is permissible when consistent with HIPAA and information
blocking safeguards, and ensuring access via those APIs is available and does
not include discriminatory pricing (i.e., without disguised information
blocking);
● Enforcing the adoption of HL7 FHIR standards across the healthcare
ecosystem to cover a broader range of healthcare data, ensuring consistent
and secure data exchange. While USCDI v3 was recently incorporated into
the standards (which we applaud), actual implementation of the FHIR
standards still remains fragmented and incomplete, especially for some types
of data, like healthcare billing;
● Continuing to modernize and streamline certification and compliance
requirements that no longer reflect current technical realities; and
● Aligning AI policy with broader interoperability initiatives, including CMS’s
patient-directed access and data exchange efforts, and HHS’s initiatives to
stop healthcare data information blocking.
Providing clear, operational guidance will reduce ambiguity for providers and
technology developers while maintaining appropriate privacy and security
protections.
II. Governance and Accountability Considerations for Non-Medical Device AI
For AI tools that are not regulated as medical devices, governance challenges
typically relate to transparency, accountability, and contractual risk allocation rather
than direct clinical safety oversight. In Cedar’s experience, these considerations are

often effectively addressed through contractual safeguards, audit, and defined
use-case limitations.
HHS can support innovation by clarifying baseline expectations around
transparency, documentation, auditability, and data stewardship, while avoiding rigid
or default liability frameworks that could discourage responsible AI development.
Clear, risk-based guidance would allow AI-enabled tools to evolve while preserving
accountability.
III. Supporting Private-Sector Certification and Testing
Cedar appreciates HHS’s interest in supporting private-sector accreditation,
certification, and testing frameworks. Industry-led benchmarking and validation
initiatives are often better positioned to evolve alongside rapidly advancing AI
technologies and adapt to emerging clinical and operational use cases.
HHS can play a constructive role by encouraging voluntary, consensus-driven
standards and facilitating collaboration across stakeholders, rather than imposing
static or prescriptive certification regimes that may quickly become outdated.
IV. Interoperability as a Catalyst for AI Innovation
Enhanced interoperability remains one of the most effective levers available to
accelerate AI adoption in clinical care. Standardized, API-based access to
longitudinal clinical and coverage data enables AI tools to support care
coordination, operational efficiency, and patient engagement.
Cedar encourages HHS to ensure that digital health companies and application
developers that directly support patients in all aspects of their medical care
(including tools that enable them to afford and pay for their care) are able to
participate meaningfully in health information exchange networks under clear,
objective, and consistently applied criteria. Interoperability frameworks should
promote patient-directed access to health information through applications of a
patient’s choice and prevent contractual, pricing, or technical practices that
functionally limit or block participation.
