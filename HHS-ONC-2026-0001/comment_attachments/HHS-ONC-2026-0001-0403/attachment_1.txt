RFI Response: HHS-ONC-2026-0001
Overview
For more than two decades, federal agencies and standards development organizations have worked
to make clinical information computable. The goal has been straightforward: when data can be read
by machines, organizations can automate processes that would otherwise require someone to open a
chart, read through documentation, and manually record what they find. This manual work, known as
chart abstraction, remains one of the largest drivers of administrative cost in American healthcare.
Quality measure reporting alone costs U.S. physician practices an estimated $15.4 billion annually,
much of it spent on exactly this kind of review.
The EHR Incentive Program, HL7 v2, C-CDA, and FHIR are examples of successive efforts to
reduce this burden by encouraging digitizing health data and establishing common data formats.
These standards have enabled significant automation, yet chart abstraction persists because clinicians
document much of what they know in narrative form. A physician's assessment, a nurse's
observations, a social worker's notes: this information often appears in free text rather than structured
fields. Furthermore there continues to be weak adoption and implementation of standardization of
data across the healthcare continuum.
AI-based clinical natural language processing offers a way to extend existing interoperability
infrastructure into narrative documentation and weakly standardized data. These methods extract
structured meaning from clinical notes, discharge summaries, and legacy records without requiring
clinicians to change how they document. The result is programmable access to information that was
previously available only through human review.
This response describes use cases where AI-enabled processing works alongside existing standards to
reduce administrative burden, support patient safety surveillance, and strengthen health information
infrastructure. The examples reflect operational deployments within health plans, health systems, and
healthcare analytics organizations. Each use case connects directly to ASTP's long standing
priorities: making clinical data computable, reducing the cost of manual abstraction, and enabling the
interoperability infrastructure that supports a learning health system.

Clinical Registry Reporting
Understanding of Need
Hospitals and physician practices voluntarily participate in clinical registries to benchmark their
performance against national standards and identify opportunities for improvement. A registry is
essentially a database that collects standardized information about patients with specific conditions or
procedures, stroke patients, surgical cases, cancer diagnoses, cardiac interventions. By comparing
their outcomes to aggregated data from hundreds of other institutions, healthcare organizations can
see where they excel and where they fall short.
The challenge is that registries require detailed clinical information that must be manually extracted
from medical records. A trained abstractor, typically a nurse or health information specialist, reads
through operative notes, progress notes, laboratory results, and discharge summaries to find specific
data elements and enter them into the registry system. This process is called chart abstraction, and it
is extraordinarily time-consuming.
A typical health system participates in dozens of registries across clinical domains: ACS NSQIP
alone collects 135 variables per case. A routine case takes an experienced abstractor about 60
minutes; complex cases with multiple complications can require 5-6 hours of chart review. A health
system performing 22,000 surgical cases annually dedicates over 11,000 labor hours, more than five
full-time employees, to this single registry.

how different clinicians and different EHR systems document the same clinical concepts. When

Clinical Quality Measurement and Reporting
At its core, healthcare quality measurement is an abstraction problem. Clinical research identifies the
markers of high-quality, evidence-based care; quality measurement then seeks to abstract those
markers from the primary medical record to evaluate performance. For decades, this abstraction was
a manual, labor-intensive process performed by human reviewers. The transition to electronic
Clinical Quality Measures (eCQMs) is designed to automate this, reducing the cost and effort of
abstraction by standardizing both the data format such as CCDA and FHIR and the measure logic
expression with the Clinical Quality Language or CQL.
The Limitations of Structural Standardization
Standardizing clinical data, moving from HL7 v2 to C-CDA and now toward FHIR has resulted in a
matrix of non-uniformity where even when organizations adopt a shared standard, local
implementation choices, custom data mappings, and varying rates of adoption across the continuum
of care leave the data "messy."
As a result, a Chief Quality Officer at an academic medical center cannot simply "run" a measure
across their enterprise and trust the result. They find that even standardized fields for common labs or
vitals remain unreliable until they undergo resource-intensive, organization-specific reconciliation. In
this environment, the administrative data still fails to speak the language of the clinician, remaining
an abstraction of a proxy rather than an abstraction of the care itself.
The Clinical Narrative and Free Text
The second, more significant challenge is that the most vital parts of the clinical story are never
"coded." Critical indicators of clinical judgment, patient nuance, and medical necessity reside in
unstructured clinical notes and free text. Because these narratives are not standardized, they remain
invisible to eCQM developers and the rigid logic of CQL.
The move toward Digital Quality Measures (dQMs) is intended to expand the data ecosystem to
include registries and devices, but it does not resolve this fundamental visibility gap. dQMs still
largely rely on Clinical Quality Language (CQL), a machine-readable expression language that,
while powerful, is restricted to structured inputs. Consequently, the industry faces a compounding
barrier: the logic standard lacks broad adoption, and the clinical narrative remains unreadable to the
machine.
Proposed Approach
AI-based natural language processing can normalize poorly standardized data and extend the reach of
electronic quality measurement by extracting measure-relevant data elements from clinical
documentation.

Scenario: The SEP-1 measure (Severe Sepsis and Septic Shock Management Bundle) is one of the most

moment when a patient first met criteria for severe sepsis, and then verifying that a bundle of treatments

Clinical Context:

Guideline Adherence Monitoring
Clinical practice guidelines exist for nearly every dimension of patient care: when to initiate
antibiotics, how to manage blood glucose, which prophylactic therapies hospitalized patients should
receive, and how to respond to early signs of clinical deterioration. These recommendations reflect
decades of accumulated evidence about what improves outcomes and prevents harm.
Medicine will always require clinical judgment and nuance. But guidelines define a baseline standard
of care - specifying the interventions, timing, and thresholds most consistently associated with better
outcomes. When care systematically deviates from that baseline, it can signal breakdowns in
processes, communication, or oversight. Detecting those deviations early offers a powerful
opportunity to identify emerging safety risks before they result in patient harm. And when avoidable
harm does occur, a central question remains: was there a moment when care diverged from best
practice?
Today, answering that question is extraordinarily labor-intensive. Determining whether care was
guideline-concordant requires a trained clinician to manually review the chart - every note, order, lab
result, and nursing assessment - reconstruct the clinical timeline, and compare it against what
evidence-based recommendations dictate. This depth of review is typically reserved for retrospective
root cause analyses and applied to only a small subset of cases. Performing the same analysis
continuously, across an entire patient population, remains effectively intractable.
Complicating matters further, guidelines include exceptions. A patient may have a legitimate
contraindication to a recommended treatment. A clinician may document sound reasoning for an
alternative approach. Distinguishing a process failure from an appropriate clinical decision requires
reading and interpreting the documentation.
Finally, clinical practice guidelines are not static. New evidence emerges continuously, yet formal
guideline updates often lag behind the science by months or years. Conversely, some
recommendations persist long after their evidentiary basis has weakened or been superseded. Once
new guidance is issued, there is rarely a systematic mechanism to evaluate how rapidly it is adopted,
how faithfully it is implemented, or whether it produces the intended outcomes in real-world settings.
This creates a feedback gap: guidelines shape care, but care data are not routinely used to inform the
refinement, retirement, or recalibration of the guidelines themselves.
Proposed Approach
AI can continuously monitor whether actual care delivery aligns with guideline expectations across
the full scope of clinical practice. For each patient, the system maps their clinical situation against
relevant guideline pathways, identifying what should happen based on their conditions and risk
factors, checking whether those care steps occurred, and reading clinical documentation to determine
whether any deviation has documented clinical justification. AI does more than just identify
guideline misses, it can reason about which process failures actually drive harm and provide targeted
intervention opportunities to the quality team.

Utilization Management
Understanding of Need
When a physician decides that a patient needs to be admitted to the hospital, stay in the ICU, or
receive a particular treatment, someone has to verify that the clinical situation justifies that level of
care. This process is called utilization management, and it exists because healthcare resources are
expensive and sometimes overused. A patient who could safely recover at home shouldn't occupy a
hospital bed.
Health plans and hospitals employ nurses and other clinical staff as case managers or utilization
review specialists. These reviewers compare patient information against standardized medical
necessity criteria, commercial products like InterQual or MCG that specify what clinical findings
justify different levels of care.
Healthcare administrative costs reached an estimated $274.5 billion in 2017, with utilization
management representing a substantial component. Case managers spend hours each day on data
extraction tasks that don't require clinical judgment, simply finding and copying information from
one system to another.
The Utilization Management Criteria Landscape
Health plans rarely rely on a single criteria set. Utilization management operates through a patchwork of
guideline products, each governing different clinical domains, layered with plan-specific customizations and
subject to a strict hierarchy of authority.

The AI Opportunity
This patchwork creates the core problem AI can solve. A single admission review may require the
case manager to determine which criteria set applies, navigate the decision hierarchy, extract clinical
data from the EHR, map it against the correct criteria, and document the determination, all within
increasingly compressed turnaround requirements.
Proposed Approach
AI can extract clinical data from electronic health records and map it against medical necessity
criteria automatically. Rather than requiring a case manager to search through the chart for each data
point, the system identifies relevant clinical information, calculates any derived scores (like
pneumonia severity indices), and presents a pre-populated review with links to source
documentation.
This approach doesn't remove the case manager from the process, it removes the tedious data
extraction that consumes most of their time.
Regulatory Alignment
Accurate level-of-care determination supports appropriate resource utilization without changing
clinical workflows. The approach reduces administrative burden while maintaining the clinical
oversight that utilization management requires.

Incidental Findings
Understanding the Need: The Abstraction of Secondary Clinical Data
Clinical documentation is primarily structured to support the immediate reason for a patient
encounter. However, diagnostic procedures frequently reveal incidental findings which are
unexpected abnormalities that are unrelated to the primary complaint but represent significant early
warning signs for chronic disease or malignancy. These findings, such as a lung nodule identified
during a cardiac scan, often contain the "second story" of a patient’s health. While these observations
are clinically relevant, they are difficult to track systematically because they fall outside the
immediate scope of the primary diagnosis and the resulting billing codes.
The Limitations of Billing-Centric Records
The central barrier to managing incidental findings is that current documentation systems are
designed for targeted, episodic care. Because an incidental finding is secondary to the chief
complaint, it is rarely assigned a discrete ICD-10 code during the initial encounter. Without a
standardized code, the finding does not trigger administrative alerts or follow-up protocols. The data
exists only within the narrative text of the clinical record. This gap creates a significant risk where
the physician notes may contain actionable findings, yet a majority do not receive the recommended
follow-up care because manual chart review is too resource-intensive to perform at scale.
The Challenges of Manual and Rules-Based Review
Traditional methods of identifying these findings rely on manual abstraction by nursing or
administrative staff, which is often cost-prohibitive. Attempts to automate this process using
rules-based logic or simple keyword searches frequently fail. These systems often produce high rates
of false positives by flagging benign variations or miss subtle clinical nuances because they cannot
interpret the context of the physician's prose.
Proposed Approach
AI-based natural language processing provides a mechanism to bridge this gap by performing
high-fidelity abstraction across the entire clinical narrative. Unlike manual review, AI models can
process vast quantities of unstructured text to identify specific, actionable findings and reconcile
them with existing patient data. This technology allows organizations to move from reactive
documentation to a proactive system of care. By automating the identification of incidental findings,
AI ensures that secondary clinical data is captured and integrated into the patient’s longitudinal
record, enabling timely intervention and more accurate risk assessment without increasing the
administrative burden on clinicians.

Elimination of Prior Authorization
Understanding of Need
Prior authorization is a requirement imposed by health insurance payers that physicians obtain
approval before delivering certain medical services. Prior authorization exists because payers lack
visibility into whether providers make appropriate clinical decisions. When a physician orders an
expensive biologic medication or an advanced imaging study, the payer has no way of knowing
whether that decision reflects sound clinical judgment or unnecessary utilization. Prior authorization
fills this information gap by requiring providers to submit documentation proving medical necessity
for each individual request ahead of time.
The time to respond to a prior authorization request can impact patient care and CMS recognized this
burden in the CMS-0057-F Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule, which requires
payers to process standard requests within 72 hours and urgent requests within 24 hours. The rule
acknowledges that authorization delays directly harm patients and impose unsustainable costs on
providers.
The burden is significant. Physicians spend an average of 14 hours per week on prior authorization
(American Medical Association, 2024). Practices complete 43 requests per physician per week
(AMA 2023 Physician Survey, 2024). Staffing costs have increased 43% since 2019 (MGMA
Regulatory Burden Report, 2024). Patients wait for treatments their physicians have already
determined they need (AMA, 2024).
This is one area where we see cautionary misuse of AI as a way to augment process with AI on both
sides of a prior authorization exchange - where two AI bots are battling each other and the winner is
likely not going to be the patient.
But the underlying question prior authorization tries to answer is straightforward: does this provider
make clinically appropriate decisions? If a payer could answer that question at the provider level
rather than the request level, individual prior authorizations would become unnecessary.
This is the concept behind "gold carding" which is granting trusted providers automatic approval for
services that would otherwise require prior authorization. Several states have enacted gold carding
legislation, and CMS has encouraged payers to reduce prior authorization burden for providers with
strong track records.

Safety Event Detection
Understanding of Need
Voluntary incident reporting, the primary mechanism hospitals use to identify patient harm, captures
only 10 to 20 percent of actual adverse events as many of the safety events are not visible to staff but
are buried in the clinical documentation.
To address this gap, the Institute for Healthcare Improvement developed the Global Trigger Tool
(Griffin & Resar, 2009). In addition to voluntary reports, trained reviewers examine patient records
for "triggers," clinical findings statistically associated with adverse events: a creatinine value that
doubles from baseline, administration of an opioid reversal agent, an unexpected ICU transfer. Each
trigger flags a case for clinical review to determine whether harm occurred. The triggers can be
automated with current reporting and technology but they only identify that an event “might” have
occurred and require manual review by a trained clinician to determine if a safety event occurred.
The methodology is effective but constrained by manual effort. IHI recommends reviewing 10
randomly selected records every two weeks. At that rate, the vast majority of discharges are never
examined, and the events within them are never counted.
Proposed Approach
AI can execute trigger tool logic against all patient records, not just a random sample. The system
scans clinical data, laboratory values, medication administrations, vital signs, and clinical
documentation, looking for the same trigger conditions that human reviewers would identify. When
triggers fire, the AI can then inspect the entire record of a patient identified by an IHI trigger to
determine if a safety event has occurred avoiding the clinician review step current IHI trigger
methodology requires.
Regulatory Alignment
This approach supports Joint Commission patient safety requirements by enabling more
comprehensive surveillance. It allows organizations to identify systemic issues without the
proportional staffing increase that manual comprehensive review would require.
IHI Trigger Tool Study
Scenario: In 2011, researchers applied the IHI Global Trigger Tool to 795 patient records at three
major academic medical centers, comparing it against voluntary incident reporting and AHRQ
Patient Safety Indicators using the same patient sample. Voluntary reporting identified 4 adverse
events. AHRQ indicators identified 35. The trigger tool identified 354. Adverse events were
present in one-third of all admissions.

Root Cause Analysis
Understanding of Need
When a serious patient safety event occurs, a wrong-site surgery, a fatal medication error, a patient
death from an unrecognized deterioration, healthcare organizations are required to conduct a
thorough investigation to understand what went wrong and prevent similar events in the future. The
Joint Commission, which accredits most American hospitals, mandates that these "sentinel events"
trigger a root cause analysis within strict timeframes: the investigation must begin within 72 hours
and be completed within 45 days.
Root cause analysis is a systematic approach to understanding not just what happened, but why it
happened. The goal is to look beyond the individual who made the final error and understand the
system failures, communication breakdowns, and process gaps that allowed the error to occur.
The challenge is that the same clinical event is documented differently by different staff members.
Reconstructing a coherent timeline from these fragmented accounts is extraordinarily labor-intensive.
Investigators must read through pages of nursing notes, physician documentation, respiratory therapy
records, pharmacy records, and safety reports. They must identify where the accounts align and
where they conflict.
In the same way that Guideline Adherence can scale to include the entire patient population, it's
valuable (completely impractical today) to run some version of an RCA on every harm event, not just
sentinel events. In conversations with safety leaders, the concept of “mini-RCAs” are emerging as a
promising approach to use AI to do an RCA of every event which massively reduces the burden on
safety managers to review and triage reported events. AI does the initial RCA and uses reasoning to
identify trends and focus review on only the highest value RCA results.

Understanding of Need
Healthcare records contain some of the most personal information imaginable, and certain categories
of health data require extra protection under the law. When someone seeks treatment for substance
use disorder, receives reproductive healthcare, or accesses mental health services, that information
carries legal protections beyond standard HIPAA privacy rules. The challenge for health information
exchanges is that this sensitive information does not always announce itself with convenient labels or

The recent (2024) introduction of TEFCA has drastically increased the volume of data exchange
across state lines and will require scaled approaches to data segmentation, security and patient
consent.
Federal and state regulations mandate that healthcare organizations segment certain types of sensitive
health information before sharing it. The 2024 rule modifying 42 CFR Part 2 aligns substance use
disorder confidentiality requirements with HIPAA, with full compliance required by February 2026.

people to seek care for stigmatized conditions without fear that their information will be
inappropriately disclosed.
Modern clinical documents like C-CDAs contain both structured coded fields and extensive narrative
sections where clinicians document the nuances of care. These documents frequently include
Base64-encoded PDF attachments containing consultation notes, imaging reports, or historical

Cautionary Framework: Responsible AI Deployment in Clinical Settings
Two failure modes pose significant risk if left unaddressed by federal policy. The first is operational:
AI systems that overwhelm the humans they serve. The second is technical: AI systems whose
reasoning cannot be trusted, explained, or reproduced.
I. Signal Overload
The IHI Global Trigger Tool demonstrated that adverse events occur in roughly one-third of hospital
admissions, ten times the rate captured by voluntary reporting. When one multi-hospital system
deployed electronic trigger tools, the volume was so overwhelming the organization disabled it
entirely. The tool worked. The humans had no framework for processing the output.
Our team experienced this directly in sepsis detection work at emergency department triage. The
model achieved strong performance (F1 0.71, published in JMIR). The modeling succeeded. The
implementation failed. We did not account for clinical workflow and saved zero lives.
The principle: detection must be paired with prioritization, and prioritization with workflow
integration. Systems should perform counterfactual analysis to surface the guideline failures most
strongly associated with harm, transforming a firehose into a ranked, actionable list. Federal
guidance should require AI safety tools demonstrate operational integration, not just detection
accuracy.
II. Hallucination and Reasoning
LLMs produce hallucinations: clinically plausible statements that are factually wrong. Research
documents hallucination rates of 1.47% and omission rates of 3.45% in clinical note generation. At
health system scale, these translate directly to patient safety events.
The deeper issue is that LLMs reason by pattern completion, not clinical logic. Consider "last known
well" in stroke care: establishing this timestamp requires synthesizing nursing notes, EMS reports,
and family interviews, resolving contradictory accounts across multiple sources. An LLM might
produce the correct answer, but there is no way to verify why, and no guarantee the same model
produces the same answer after a version update. A wrong timestamp determines whether a patient
receives tPA or is excluded from treatment.
Neurosymbolic reasoning as an alternative. Our approach, validated with the College of American
Pathologists, decomposes clinical questions into logic trees of constrained sub-problems. Each node
uses AI in a tightly scoped role. The tree composes results using explicit, inspectable logic,
producing auditability (answers trace to source evidence), reproducibility (same inputs, same outputs
regardless of model version), and targeted validation (each node tested independently).
Recommended Policy Guardrails
Require structured output over free-text generation for clinical determinations. Mandate hallucination
and omission rate disclosure tested against clinician ground truth. Require reasoning transparency so
every clinical determination has an auditable chain. Distinguish extraction from generation in federal

Conclusion
Healthcare quality improvement runs on chart abstraction and for twenty years, federal policy has
pursued a straightforward goal: make clinical data machine-readable so this manual work becomes
unnecessary.
Despite this investment, chart abstraction persists. Quality measure reporting alone costs U.S.
physician practices $15.4 billion annually. Health systems dedicate entire teams to registry
abstraction - one academic medical center employs five full-time staff just to abstract surgical cases
for a single registry. CMS leadership acknowledges that even basic structured data, such as blood
pressure readings, often remain unreliable until organizations individually correct their workflows.
The data standardization that was supposed to eliminate manual review never fully arrived.
The result is that quality improvement remains constrained by what organizations can afford to
abstract. Registries limit their data elements. Quality measures simplify their specifications. Safety
surveillance samples only a handful of records each month. Organizations discover guideline failures
only after harm occurs because comprehensive monitoring would require reading every chart.
AI-based clinical natural language processing changes this calculus. Systems that achieve
human-level accuracy while reducing abstraction time by 70–90% do not merely lower costs—they
remove the constraint that has shaped what quality improvement can attempt.
When abstraction costs drop by an order of magnitude, quality measurement works the way it was
always intended:
● Registries collect the clinical detail that drives improvement, extracted directly from
operative notes and pathology reports, without requiring large abstraction teams
● Quality measures extend into narrative documentation where relevant clinical information
actually resides, rather than contorting specifications to fit available structured fields
● Safety surveillance scales from monthly samples to continuous monitoring, surfacing adverse
events that voluntary reporting misses
● Guideline adherence shifts from retrospective discovery of failures to prospective
identification of care gaps while intervention is still possible
● Root cause analysis compresses from weeks of manual reconstruction to hours, enabling
more thorough investigations that prevent recurrence
When the constraint lifts entirely, new possibilities emerge and applications that were never feasible
when abstraction required human review:
● Quality measures too complex for manual abstraction become practical, such as the CAP
pathology measures we validated at human-level accuracy after previous automation attempts
failed
● Real-time guideline monitoring across entire patient populations, identifying the specific
protocol failures most strongly associated with harm
