GENERAL COMMENTARY
Netsmart supports a harmonized federal framework that reduces fragmentation by
preempting or aligning state rules, protects patients, and enables responsible innovation.
We also support payment and programmatic reforms that reimburse high‑value AI use
cases where evidence demonstrates improved quality, access, and efficiency across clinical
and administrative workflows.
A federal framework is necessary to reduce state‑by‑state fragmentation that imposes
significant compliance burdens on providers, and Health IT developers alike. Netsmart
supports a federal framework that distinguishes operational tools (e.g., scheduling and

summarization) from clinical tools (e.g., influencing diagnosis or treatment). Standardized
transparency requirements that build trust and enable accountability, such as AI model
cards, would help improve clinician and patient confidence.
Netsmart supports defined reimbursement pathways for AI‑enabled services that
measurably reduce burden and improve care quality, including ambient listening,
AI‑assisted clinical documentation, and automated back-office functions, among other use
cases. Payment policies should recognize multi-disciplinary benefits spanning clinicians,
administrative staff, billing teams, and care coordinators. Permanent reimbursement
pathways could be modeled on telehealth precedents, with safeguards for documentation
integrity, audit readiness, and human-in-the-loop oversight. We support aligning
reimbursement with value‑based care objectives, including the use of AI to reduce
avoidable utilization, improve care coordination, and fulfill discrete requirements under
programs such as Chronic Care Management, where tasks can be automated yet remain
verifiable and clinically supervised.
Netsmart supports targeted federal investment in AI research and development in
behavioral health and post‑acute care, where fragmentation and privacy constraints have
slowed progress. We support the use of de‑identified datasets (including registries, vital
records, and vetted public health data) for model development and validation. We oppose
policies that over‑restrict lawful secondary use of appropriately de‑identified data in ways
that unduly hinder innovation, and we support funding via grants, prospective payment
schedules or Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation (CMMI) models to reduce the
operational costs of AI-enabled tools and to advance health‑care‑specific models.
SPECIFIC QUESTIONS
WHAT ARE THE BIGGEST BARRIERS TO PRIVATE SECTOR INNOVATION IN AI FOR HEALTH CARE AND
ITS ADOPTION AND USE IN CLINICAL CARE?
The primary barriers to innovation in AI for healthcare and the adoption and use of AI in
clinical care include cost, regulatory fragmentation, limited trust and clinician acceptance,
unclear consent and authorization responsibilities, and constrained access to diverse
deidentified data. A state‑by‑state framework creates inconsistent obligations for data
masking, consent, and AI operations, which materially complicates compliance and
workflow requirements for health IT developers as well as clinicians operating across
multiple jurisdictions. There is also the potential for clinician skepticism in the absence of
transparency and human-in-the-loop oversight.

WHAT REGULATORY, PAYMENT POLICY, OR PROGRAMMATIC DESIGN CHANGES SHOULD HHS
PRIORITIZE TO INCENTIVIZE THE EFFECTIVE USE OF AI IN CLINICAL CARE AND WHY? WHAT HHS
REGULATIONS, POLICIES, OR PROGRAMS COULD BE REVISITED TO AUGMENT YOUR ABILITY TO
DEVELOP OR USE AI IN CLINICAL CARE? PLEASE PROVIDE SPECIFIC CHANGES AND APPLICABLE CODE
OF FEDERAL REGULATIONS CITATIONS.
For ambient listening and AI-assisted documentation ("ambient scribe") which has already
proven its efficacy, we support HHS funding the deployment and implementation of AI
tools via expanded reimbursement pathways within prospective payment schedules and
other billing methodologies and the creation of grant funding for one-time expenses. The
use of technology, such as ambient listening and ambient scribe in clinical care settings,
should be reimbursed to incentivize adoption. AI-enabled services support enhanced care
delivery and a reduction in administrative burden on providers.
FOR NON-MEDICAL DEVICES, WE UNDERSTAND THAT USE OF AI IN CLINICAL CARE MAY RAISE NOVEL
LEGAL AND IMPLEMENTATION ISSUES THAT CHALLENGE EXISTING GOVERNANCE AND
ACCOUNTABILITY STRUCTURES (E.G., RELATING TO LIABILITY, INDEMNIFICATION, PRIVACY, AND
SECURITY). WHAT NOVEL LEGAL AND IMPLEMENTATION ISSUES EXIST AND WHAT ROLE, IF ANY,
SHOULD HHS PLAY TO HELP ADDRESS THEM?
We recommend HHS develop guidance addressing the risks of using general-purpose AI
tools outside approved systems, clarifying responsibility and minimum security and
privacy expectations. Specifically, HHS should issue guidance that clearly distinguishes
consumer‑facing mental health chatbots from clinical AI tools used within regulated care
settings, recognizing that these technologies serve different functions, have different end
users and present materially different risk profiles.
Mental health chatbots are typically designed to provide general wellness support, and
engagement directly to consumers and operate outside formal clinical workflows of a
regulated provider organization, without clinician supervision or integration into certified
health IT systems. By contrast, clinical AI tools are deployed within covered entities,
integrated into electronic health records or enterprise platforms, and used by professionals
who are trained in their use, to support documentation, care coordination, or clinical
decision‑making under established privacy, security, and accountability frameworks.
The Clinical AI tools developed by Netsmart are used by clinicians and are not designed to
replace them. HHS guidance should clarify that mental health chatbots should not be
treated as clinical AI unless they influence diagnosis, treatment, documentation, or care
decisions, at which point they should be subject to corresponding clinical governance,
transparency, and human‑oversight expectations. Establishing functional boundaries based
on use and context would reduce provider confusion, protect patients, and support
responsible AI adoption across behavioral health and mental health settings.

FOR NON-MEDICAL DEVICES, WHAT ARE THE MOST PROMISING AI EVALUATION METHODS (PRE-
AND POST-DEPLOYMENT), METRICS, ROBUSTNESS TESTING, AND OTHER WORKFLOW AND HUMAN-
CENTERED EVALUATION METHODS FOR CLINICAL CARE? SHOULD HHS FURTHER SUPPORT THESE
PROCESSES? IF SO, WHICH MECHANISMS WOULD BE MOST IMPACTFUL (E.G., CONTRACTS, GRANTS,
COOPERATIVE AGREEMENTS, AND/OR PRIZE COMPETITIONS)?
We support establishing federal evaluation baselines for non‑device clinical AI grounded in
the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Adoption of a federal framework would also
present an opportunity to drive alignment across agencies (e.g., CMS, ASTP/ONC, SAMHSA)
on adverse-event reporting, and real-world data collection.
We support funding mechanisms, such as grants, and cooperative agreements targeted to
administrative burden reduction, documentation quality, reimbursement efficiency, and
use cases in behavioral health and post‑acute care. CMMI has an Innovations in Behavioral
Health (IBH) Demonstration Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO) currently open for
solicitation. We recommend CMMI make the AI-enabled behavioral health use cases eligible
for immediate reimbursement under the IBH model. This would drive participation in the
model and adoption of clinical AI in behavioral health. Furthermore, HHS should consider
making behavioral health, post-acute care, and specialty providers of all types eligible for
contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements to encourage adoption of clinical AI tools
across all sectors of healthcare. We are at a critical juncture of healthcare, and all types of
providers should have access to reimbursement mechanisms to enable them to provide the
highest quality, most efficient care to all patient populations in the United States.
HOW CAN HHS BEST SUPPORT PRIVATE SECTOR ACTIVITIES (E.G., ACCREDITATION,
CERTIFICATION, INDUSTRY-DRIVEN TESTING, AND CREDENTIALING) TO PROMOTE INNOVATIVE AND
EFFECTIVE AI USE IN CLINICAL CARE?
Netsmart supports a federal framework adopted consistently across HHS, and its sub-
agencies. Additionally, we support the adoption of baseline guardrails related to topics
such as safety, transparency, permissible data use, auditability, and human-in-the-loop
oversight. We recommend HHS continue to invest in research and development in all
sectors of healthcare. We welcome collaboration with HHS and its sub-agencies as you
analyze potential use cases for AI in clinical care, especially as it relates to use cases in
behavioral health, substance use, post-acute, and long-term care as well specialty practice
settings such as physical and occupational therapy.

WHERE HAVE AI TOOLS DEPLOYED IN CLINICAL CARE MET OR EXCEEDED PERFORMANCE AND COST
EXPECTATIONS AND WHERE HAVE THEY FALLEN SHORT? WHAT KINDS OF NOVEL AI TOOLS WOULD
HAVE THE GREATEST POTENTIAL TO IMPROVE HEALTH, CARE OUTCOMES, GIVE NEW INSIGHTS ON
QUALITY, AND HELP REDUCE COSTS?
Ambient listening and documentation have demonstrated robust performance value by
improving note quality, reducing administrative burden, and enhancing patient‑clinician
interaction. 22% of Netsmart behavioral health clients leverage Bells Documentation
Assistant, a generative AI solution that helps clinicians create accurate, complete notes
faster and reduce documentation time. Users completed 7.2 million notes last year, cutting
documentation time by up to 67%. Leveraging technology like this across all Behavioral
Health providers in the US could save millions of hours annually. 7% of Netsmart
behavioral health clients leverage Bells Virtual Scribe, an ambient AI solution that listens,
transcribes and drafts notes in near real time to reduce administrative burden and support
patient-focused care. More than 65,000 scribe sessions were completed last year.
We have heard directly from clients how these AI-enabled solutions have reduced
administrative burden, streamlined efficiencies, and improved organizational satisfaction.
Widespread adoption of these solutions is constrained by funding and reimbursement
uncertainty, workforce change management, and variable organizational readiness. We
support the development of federal grants and pilots to offset startup costs and to generate
publicly available evidence. High‑value innovations in AI include tools that reduce
administrative burden, strengthen revenue cycle performance, generate accurate referral
and handoff summaries, streamline intake, and improve care coordination, with public
reporting emphasizing non‑proprietary evidence.
Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care
organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What
are the primary administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
Within provider organizations, adoption decisions are commonly influenced by executive
leadership (e.g., CEOs/COOs), clinical leaders (e.g., CMIOs/CNOs), IT leadership (e.g.,
CIOs/CTOs), compliance and privacy officers, and finance leaders (e.g., CFOs) given the
importance of reimbursement and operational return on investment. The principal hurdles
related to the adoption of AI in clinical care include funding streams providers face for the
implementation of AI, CMS reimbursement uncertainty, fragmented data stewardship
obligations, and conflicting state policies. We support the adoption of a federal framework
with accepted standards, and reimbursement pathways to enhance healthcare providers’
confidence in the investment and utilization of AI in clinical care settings. To this end, the
proposed removal of standard model card data via the HTI-5 proposed rule reduces

certainty, inhibits transparency of data, and inadvertently creates greater confusion for
providers trying to understand the breadth and scope of the tools available to them.
WHERE WOULD ENHANCED INTEROPERABILITY WIDEN MARKET OPPORTUNITIES, FUEL RESEARCH,
AND ACCELERATE THE DEVELOPMENT OF AI FOR CLINICAL CARE? PLEASE CONSIDER SPECIFIC DATA
TYPES, DATA STANDARDS, AND BENCHMARKING TOOLS.
Enhanced interoperability is essential to expanding access to data for AI training,
validation, and benchmarking. The ONC certification program aims to advance secure
access, exchange, and use of EHI through standardized Application Programming Interfaces
(APIs). In addition, the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement (TEFCA)
establishes a nationwide governance and connectivity approach for network-to-network
exchange. CMS Aligned Networks are pushing interoperability forward by advancing and
expanding the standardized exchange of data. We encourage the administration to continue
to focus not only on traditional healthcare settings e.g., hospital, and primary care, but also
behavioral health/mental health, post-acute care, long-term care and specialty practice
settings and the correlating data. The aforementioned initiatives can reduce fragmentation
and help enable secure, scalable pathways for the data access needed to evaluate AI, but
only if all patient populations and care settings are included. These community-based
providers are often at the bottom of the healthcare funding food chain, and do not have the
resources a hospital may have to invest in interoperability, yet they are treating some of
the most acute patients in healthcare. Helping to fund their integration with the rest of
healthcare providers would greatly improve coordination of care.
We recommend continued focus on advancing data standards to enable AI-generated
summaries (a common use case for AI in clinical care) to allow for reliable integration into
referral, admission, and financial intake workflows. The United States Core Data for
Interoperability (USCDI) establishes a standardized set of interoperable data elements, and
HL7 Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR)/US Core provide implementable
profiles and APIs that support consistent exchange and ingestion. HHS should also expand
access to high-quality public health and population-level data, including immunization
registries, which the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) describes as
confidential population-based systems supporting both point-of-care histories and
population surveillance, enabling privacy-preserving research and evaluation across the
continuum.
ARE THERE SPECIFIC AREAS OF AI RESEARCH THAT HHS SHOULD PRIORITIZE TO ACCELERATE THE
ADOPTION OF AI AS PART OF CLINICAL CARE?
We support HHS investment in behavioral health and post-acute care use cases, de-
identification modernization, population health analytics, registry integration, and
strategies to reduce the operational costs of large language models and related

infrastructure. We support research on systematic evidence reviews that quantify the costs
and benefits associated with AI deployment in clinical workflows.
CONCLUSION
Netsmart appreciates HHS’s leadership in advancing responsible AI adoption in clinical
care. We welcome continued engagement with ASTP/ONC and other HHS sub-agencies as
the agency develops practical guardrails, evaluation approaches, and reimbursement
pathways that protect patients while enabling innovation, particularly in behavioral health,
post-acute care, long-term care, and specialty practice settings.
