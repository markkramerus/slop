Policy Priorities for Responsible AI in Healthcare
While we are optimistic about the potential for AI to enhance care quality, access, and
efficiency, we urge the Administration to establish a thoughtful policy framework that ensures
safety, equity, and innovation.
1. Transparency and Explainability: Patients and clinicians must understand when AI is
involved in the decision-making process and have access to clear explanations of AI-
generated recommendations.
2. Privacy and Data Protections: AI tools handling personal health information must be
subject to robust privacy and security safeguards, including modernized federal
privacy standards that cover digital health tools not subject to HIPAA.
3. Bias Auditing and Equity-by-Design: Developers and deployers of AI systems must test
for bias and ensure tools work equitably across diverse populations. AI has the power
to reduce structural barriers in health care and should be designed and governed
intentionally.
4. Role of Clinicians and Human Oversight: AI should augment—not override—clinical
judgment. Clinical AI tools should preserve human decision-making and avoid
automating care denials or coverage decisions.
5. Certification and Risk-Based Regulation: Federal oversight should distinguish between
high-risk, clinical AI applications and lower-risk tools such as administrative navigation.
Regulatory clarity is needed, but it must be risk-proportionate and not stifle
innovation.
6. Support for Health Care Workforce Enablement: Federal investment is needed to build
AI fluency across the health care workforce, including training for clinicians,
administrators, and digital health developers.

We also acknowledge that structural and regulatory barriers limit responsible and scalable
adoption. We offer the following recommendations to help HHS accelerate safe and effective
AI integration across its programs.
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
From Transcarent’s perspective, the lack of harmonization among state AI and privacy laws—
combined with the absence of a coherent national framework governing healthcare AI—
creates significant operational, compliance, and innovation challenges. Healthcare
organizations operating across multiple states must navigate a patchwork of evolving and, at
times, inconsistent requirements related to data use, algorithmic transparency, consumer
notice, bias audits, and automated decision-making. This fragmentation increases
compliance costs, complicates product design, and can slow the deployment of tools that
improve access, reduce administrative burden, and enhance care coordination. In the
healthcare context, where AI systems often rely on multi-state data flows, employer-
sponsored coverage spanning jurisdictions, and interoperability across provider networks, a
lack of national alignment risks creating uncertainty for providers, patients, and developers
alike. A consistent federal framework that harmonizes privacy protections, governance
expectations, and oversight standards would better support responsible innovation,
strengthen patient trust, and ensure equitable access to AI-enabled health solutions across
the country.
Another barrier is workflow integration. AI tools layered onto existing clinical or
administrative processes without thoughtful redesign risk increasing burden rather than
reducing it. Adoption is strongest when AI reduces documentation demands, synthesizes
relevant patient information prior to encounters, and simplifies navigation for patients and
caregivers.
Misaligned incentives further constrain investment. When payment systems reward volume
rather than outcomes, providers and organizations have limited financial rationale to deploy
AI tools that reduce unnecessary utilization or administrative waste. In contrast, in value-
based arrangements, AI-enabled navigation and care coordination can measurably reduce
inappropriate site-of-care utilization and avoid unnecessary procedures while improving
patient engagement.
Clinicians and patients require clarity regarding oversight, monitoring, explainability, and
responsibility when AI influences clinical workflows.

Transcarent supports the development of a risk-based, technology-neutral federal framework
for AI, that applies to healthcare, that promotes safety and accountability without
unnecessarily constraining innovation. Federal policy should clearly distinguish between
high-risk clinical decision support tools that directly inform diagnosis or treatment and lower-
risk administrative or navigation applications that streamline scheduling, benefits guidance,
documentation, or care coordination. Overly broad or undifferentiated regulation could
inadvertently slow the deployment of AI tools that reduce administrative burden and improve
access without materially increasing clinical risk. A proportionate approach—grounded in the
function and risk profile of the application rather than the underlying technology—will better
protect patients while enabling responsible innovation and scalable adoption across
healthcare settings.
HHS should also provide practical, implementation-oriented guidance under the HIPAA
Privacy Rule clarifying permissible data uses in AI development and deployment,
expectations for de-identification, safeguards against re-identification risk, and audit
requirements. Clear guidance will reduce compliance uncertainty while preserving patient
trust.
With respect to payment policy, HHS should prioritize outcome-based program designs
rather than establishing payment solely for software artifacts. Where Medicare payment
frameworks apply, policies should recognize AI-enabled services when they demonstrably
improve access, quality, equity, or affordability. Incorporating AI-enabled capabilities into
value-based care models and quality programs will more effectively align incentives than
stand-alone reimbursement for technology.
3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel
legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability
structures (e.g., relating to liability, indemnification, privacy, and security). What novel
legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help
address them?
The integration of AI into clinical environments raises important questions regarding
accountability and oversight. These include allocation of responsibility among developers,
implementers, clinicians, and organizations; governance of model updates and performance
drift; documentation of human oversight; and maintenance of auditable records.
HHS can support responsible innovation by issuing practical guidance on governance
expectations for AI used in clinical decision support and adjacent use cases. Such guidance
should emphasize transparency, monitoring, incident response protocols, and clear
communication when AI tools are used in patient-facing interactions.

Robust evaluation is essential to maintaining trust and ensuring safety. Pre-deployment
validation should include testing on representative real-world populations and workflows,
assessment of subgroup performance and calibration, robustness testing for data shift, and
human factors evaluation.
Post-deployment, continuous monitoring should assess performance drift, workflow impacts,
safety signals, administrative burden reduction, and total cost of care outcomes. The
distinction between technical model performance and real-world impact is critical; successful
integration into operational environments often determines measurable outcomes.
HHS can accelerate progress by supporting pragmatic, multi-site research initiatives that
evaluate clinical outcomes, equity impacts, and administrative burden reduction in live
operational settings. Shared benchmarks and validation infrastructure would facilitate the
scaling of responsible AI solutions across programs.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities (e.g., accreditation, certification,
industry-driven testing, and credentialing) to promote innovative and effective AI use
in clinical care?
To support private-sector testing and credentialing, HHS should harmonize expectations
across federal programs and encourage standardized reporting, auditability, and
transparency. Recognition of credible third-party evaluation frameworks would allow
scalable, consistent assurance across settings.

6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would
have the greatest potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on
quality, and help reduce costs?
At Transcarent, we believe AI holds great promise to revolutionize care delivery for the better
– to speed access to treatment, reduce administrative burdens on clinicians, and democratize
access to healthcare resources. For example, a person’s zip code no longer matters when
they want to meet with an endocrinologist. We also believe that AI should be used only for
the right reasons – it should not be used to deny access to appropriate, medically necessary
care or in a way that diminishes the quality.
In healthcare, some tasks require deep clinical expertise, while others are necessary but
administrative and labor-intensive. Generative AI is already demonstrating strong capability
in the latter, particularly in information synthesis, documentation support, and form
completion. Given that administrative activities account for a significant share of healthcare
spending, these applications can meaningfully reduce burden and clinician burnout.
Beyond administrative efficiency, the greater opportunity lies in AI-enabled triage,
navigation, and personalization. The healthcare system is complex and difficult for many
individuals to navigate. Generative AI can simplify access by guiding patients through
coverage questions, scheduling, and care options, helping them focus on their health rather
than system logistics.
AI also offers an opportunity to move beyond “one-size-fits-all” care. Personalized
educational materials tailored to a patient’s health literacy, language, and specific questions
can improve understanding and engagement. When used alongside clinicians, generative AI
can extend conversations, clarify guidance, and support informed decision-making.
Additional promising applications include personalized behavioral health support, chronic
disease risk reduction planning, synthesis of medical research for clinicians and families,
medical education, and guidance through complex treatment decisions.

8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research,
and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data
types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
A significant barrier to AI adoption in clinical and operational settings remains fragmented
and inconsistent access to high-quality clinical and administrative data. Variability in
implementation of interoperability standards, inconsistent semantic normalization across
systems, and limited access to longitudinal claims data impede model development,
validation, and real-world deployment. As we have previously emphasized in comments to
HHS and Congress, access to comprehensive claims and cost data is essential to enable
value-based arrangements, measure performance, and support longitudinal care
optimization.
Even where interoperability frameworks exist, inconsistent timeliness and completeness of
information reduce reliability. AI systems embedded in care delivery require accurate,
current, and comprehensive data to function safely and effectively.
9. What challenges within health care do patients and caregivers wish to see addressed
by the adoption and use of AI in clinical care? Equally, what concerns do patients and
caregivers have related to the adoption and use of AI in clinical care?
Patients and caregivers prioritize timely access to appropriate care, simplified navigation,
transparent pricing, and understandable information tailored to their needs. At the same
time, they express concerns regarding privacy, bias, transparency, and accountability.
Strong safeguards under HIPAA, clear notification when AI is used in care processes, and
accessible human support are essential to maintaining trust. Our experience demonstrates
that AI systems designed to augment—not replace—clinical judgment and human advocacy
are more likely to achieve sustained engagement and measurable improvements in
experience and outcomes.

In our experience, AI has met or exceeded expectations when applied to reduce
administrative burden, improve care navigation and triage, personalize educational materials,
and help individuals efficiently access appropriate in-network care. AI-enabled navigation
and virtual care integration can reduce inappropriate site-of-care utilization and improve
engagement, particularly when paired with human support.
When AI deconstructs the complexities of documentation, approvals, and benefit decision
trees to guide individuals through their healthcare journey, people can use all their benefits
more easily and efficiently, understand why and what is happening with their health at any
given time, and feel confident that they are maintaining costs while achieving the best
outcome. When we empower people with the tools to find their way to better health,
everyone benefits– Members, providers, payers, and employers.
Conversely, AI has fallen short when deployed without reliable access to complete data,
when layered onto existing workflows without redesign, or when governance and monitoring
processes are insufficient. Sustainable success requires both technological capability and
structural alignment.
Conclusion
Artificial intelligence is already improving access, reducing administrative burden, enhancing
personalization, and supporting value-based care across HHS programs, but there is
immense potential to do much more. Realizing this potential requires continued federal
leadership to advance interoperability, clarify privacy expectations, align incentives with
measurable outcomes, and promote consistent governance standards.
