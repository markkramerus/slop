Dear Secretary Kennedy,
ServiceNow appreciates the opportunity to respond to the Department’s initiative to support the
responsible adoption of Artificial Intelligence in clinical care. We share the Department’s desire
to reduce healthcare costs, eliminate administrative waste, and give time back to healthcare
workers.
As a platform company serving thousands of healthcare organizations globally, we
have observed a consistent pattern: the barrier to realizing AI’s deflationary potential is rarely
the algorithm itself. It is the fragmented workflow environment into which that algorithm must be
deployed. Today’s health systems operate dozens - sometimes hundreds - of disconnected
applications. Each new AI tool risks becoming yet another system clinicians must learn, log into,
and manually reconcile with their existing work.
This fragmentation creates three compounding problems that any federal strategy must
address:
1. The Cognitive Load Problem. Clinicians already suffer from alert fatigue and application
overload. Point-solution AI tools that operate outside the primary workflow add to this
burden rather than reducing it. Certain data and workstreams, such as automatic work
orders, incidents, and tickets, can in some cases be created and resolved before
burdening clinical or ancillary personnel -effectively resolving issues before human staff
are even aware they exist.
2. The Governance Gap. Health systems are deploying AI tools faster than they can track
them. Many organizations lack a centralized inventory of which AI models are in use,
what data they access, how they were validated, or who is accountable when they
fail. This governance vacuum creates liability uncertainty that chills adoption and
leaves patient safety to chance. Health systems need a “control tower” that provides
visibility and oversight across all AI assets.
3. The Data Silo Problem. Current interoperability standards focus almost exclusively on
clinical data - patient records, lab results, medications. But the operational
inefficiencies that drive healthcare costs live in a different data layer: staffing
schedules, bed availability, equipment status, supply chain inventory, IT service queues.
Until AI can access and act on this operational data in real time, we will not achieve
the systemic efficiency gains the Department seeks.
The solution to these challenges is not more point solutions. It is a unified workflow platform that
embeds AI directly into the operational fabric of health system delivery, connecting clinical and
administrative processes, providing centralized governance, and enabling real-time action on
enterprise-wide data, while aligning with HHS/CMS safeguards.

Responses to Specific Questions
1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its adoption
and use in clinical care? [MEDIUM PRIORITY]
Operational Data Fragmentation and the “Last Mile” Problem. The biggest barrier is not a
shortage of AI innovation - it is the inability to deliver that innovation into operational workflows
where it can create value. While clinical workflow integration is relatively well-understood (most
health systems operate on monolithic EMR platforms), the real challenge lies in operational data
fragmentation. Administrative data remains “trapped” in dozens - sometimes hundreds - of siloed
legacy systems that do not communicate with each other. Each application has its own login,
interface, and data model.
To take real cost out of the system using AI, operational workflows need to be totally
reimagined - but health systems are limited by these legacy systems. When a new AI tool is
introduced, it typically arrives as yet another standalone application. Users must leave their
primary workflow, open the AI tool, manually input or transfer data, interpret the output, return
to their original system, and take action. This “swivel chair” workflow negates the efficiency gains
AI promises. Studies consistently show that clinicians and administrators ignore alerts and
recommendations that arrive outside their primary workflow, regardless of validity.
The second major barrier is governance uncertainty. Health system executives are hesitant to
adopt AI tools when liability frameworks are unclear. Questions like “Who is responsible if the AI is
wrong?” and “How do we audit AI decisions for compliance?” remain unanswered. Without
clear governance standards, legal and compliance teams default to blocking adoption.
The path forward requires platforms that can embed AI into existing workflows rather than
adding new ones, governance frameworks that provide clear accountability without stifling
iteration, and policy that forces operational systems to openly share data.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize to
incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? [LOW PRIORITY]
Reward Efficiency Outcomes, Not Just Clinical Outcomes. Current payment models largely
reward volume and discrete clinical interventions. They do not reward the operational
efficiencies that AI is best positioned to deliver - reduced administrative time per encounter,
shorter patient wait times, optimized resource utilization, or decreased length of stay through
better care coordination.
Reimbursement models are fundamentally misaligned - they pay for procedures, not outcomes
or efficiencies. Fee-for-service doesn’t reward AI-driven prevention or automation, so tools that
could significantly reduce labor costs get shelved. Incentives favor human hours over smart
systems. This keeps AI adoption uneven: 71% of hospitals use predictive AI in EHRs, but small and
rural facilities lag below 50%, according to 2024 American Hospital Association IT Survey
(link here).
HHS should consider payment adjustments or bonus structures for health systems
that demonstrate measurable “deflationary” improvements attributable to technology
adoption. Metrics might include: administrative cost per patient encounter, time from order to
fulfillment, staff hours spent on non-clinical tasks, or patient throughput without quality
degradation. Tying reimbursement incentives (e.g., value-based adjustments) to proven

3. For non-medical devices, what novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if
any, should HHS play to help address them? [HIGH PRIORITY]
The Model Drift and Lifecycle Management Challenge. Unlike traditional software, AI models are
not static. A model validated today may degrade over weeks or months as the underlying data
patterns shift, a phenomenon called “model drift.” A scheduling optimization model trained on
pre-pandemic patient volumes, for example, may perform poorly when demand patterns
change. This creates a novel liability question: if a health system deploys an AI tool that worked
at validation but fails six months later, who bears responsibility? This may be the most
consequential barrier to broader AI adoption.
The implementation challenge is that most health systems lack the infrastructure to monitor AI
performance over time. They deploy tools and assume they continue working. There is no
equivalent of a “check engine light” for AI degradation.
Additional implementation challenges compound this problem:
interoperability remains fragmented despite FHIR progress, with 60-70% of AI failures tied to poor
quality and siloed data. Cybersecurity risks are also likely to escalate as non-device AI becomes
potential attack vectors, with breaches continuing to rise at health organizations handling PHI.
Recommendation: Require Governance Infrastructure, Not Code-Level Regulation.
Rather than attempting to regulate the code or algorithms of AI tools - which would be
impractical given the speed of iteration - HHS should require health systems
to maintain governance infrastructure capable of:
• Maintaining a centralized inventory of all AI tools in use across the organization
• Tracking version history and configuration changes for each tool
• Logging AI-generated recommendations and subsequent human actions (audit trail)
• Monitoring performance metrics and flagging drift or degradation
• Enforcing human-in-the-loop approval workflows for high-stakes decisions
This “control tower” approach to governance creates clear accountability - the health system is
responsible for maintaining oversight, and the governance infrastructure provides the evidence
needed to demonstrate due diligence. Workflow platforms that unify AI governance with
operational execution are well-suited to provide this infrastructure, as they already manage the
process layer where AI recommendations become actions.

regulatory actions:

4. For non-medical devices, what are the most promising AI evaluation methods for clinical
care? [MEDIUM PRIORITY]
Continuous Post-Deployment Monitoring Over One-Time Validation. Traditional medical device
evaluation focuses on pre-market validation: prove the device works before it ships. This model is
poorly suited to AI, which can degrade over time and whose real-world performance depends
heavily on the specific environment in which it is deployed.
The most promising evaluation approach for AI - whether clinical or operational - is continuous
monitoring of workflow-level outcomes. Rather than asking “Is the model accurate?” the
evaluation asks “Is the model delivering the intended improvement?” Metrics should include:
• Time-on-task reduction for the targeted workflow
• Error rates or rework rates before and after AI deployment
• User adoption and sustained engagement over time
• Downstream impact on efficiency metrics (throughput, cost per unit)
Recommendation: Promote Transparency Through Standardized Disclosure. HHS should
encourage development of standardized disclosure formats that require AI tool vendors to
transparently report: training data sources, intended use cases, known limitations,
validation methodology, and recommended monitoring metrics. This empowers health systems
to make informed procurement decisions and sets expectations for ongoing performance
evaluation. Such transparency standards can be promoted through guidance and best
practices without requiring formal regulatory mandates. The goal is to create market
expectations for disclosure that allow health systems to evaluate AI tools consistently.
5. How can HHS best support private sector activities to promote innovative and effective AI use
in clinical care? [MEDIUM PRIORITY]
Define “Good Governance” and Let the Market Certify It. The most effective role for HHS is to
define what good AI governance looks like without mandating specific
technical implementations. By establishing clear requirements - audit trail retention periods,
version control standards, performance monitoring minimums, human oversight protocols - HHS
can create a standard that private accreditation bodies can certify against.
This approach has several advantages: it creates market demand for governance-capable
platforms, allows for diverse technical approaches to meeting the standard, and avoids the slow
pace of direct federal certification. Health systems would seek out platforms and tools that carry
recognized governance certifications, creating a virtuous cycle that raises the bar across the
industry.

6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? [HIGH PRIORITY]
Success: Operational and Administrative Automation. AI has consistently exceeded
expectations when applied to operational workflows rather than clinical decision-making.
Examples include:
• Patient flow optimization: Predicting admission volumes, optimizing bed assignments,
and reducing emergency department boarding times
• Supply chain automation: Predicting inventory needs, automating reorder triggers, and
reducing stockouts and waste
• IT and HR service management: Automating password resets, equipment requests, and
routine HR inquiries that consume staff time
• Security and compliance: Identifying vulnerabilities, automating patch management,
and detecting anomalous access patterns
• Documentation assistance: Drafting clinical notes, summarizing patient histories, and
reducing after-hours charting burden.
These “deflationary” applications share a common characteristic: they remove friction from
existing workflows rather than adding new decision points. They free human workers to focus on
higher-value tasks. Health systems using unified workflow platforms have reported measurable
improvements including reduced time-to-resolution for IT issues, faster onboarding, significant
reductions in security vulnerabilities, and meaningful time savings for clinicians on administrative
tasks.
Shortfalls: Interruptive Clinical Decision Support and Unclear ROI. AI has fallen short when
deployed as additive alerts that interrupt clinical workflow without offering a clear path to
action. A system that flags a potential sepsis risk but requires the clinician to navigate three
screens to order the appropriate intervention creates friction, not efficiency. Alert fatigue is well-
documented: when AI generates too many recommendations, clinicians learn to ignore them
all. The lesson is clear - AI must be embedded in the workflow, not bolted on top of it.
Cost expectations have also gone unmet in many cases. While ambient scribes and
documentation tools show clear value, many clinical AI tools deployed as point solutions
lack demonstrated financial ROI - efficiency gains are often offset by new burdens from
monitoring, updating, and managing liability exposure. Fee-for-service payment models

7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations have
the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary administrative
hurdles? [HIGH PRIORITY]
The Shift from CMIO to CIO/COO. Historically, clinical informatics leaders (CMIOs, CMOs) drove
health IT adoption based on clinical validity. For AI, the decision-making center of gravity has
shifted. Chief Information Officers (CIOs) and Chief Operating Officers (COOs) now
exert significant influence because AI adoption decisions increasingly hinge on:
• Integration feasibility: Can this tool connect to our existing systems?
• Total cost of ownership: What is the implementation and maintenance burden?
• Operational ROI: Will this reduce costs or improve throughput measurably?
• Security and compliance: Does this create new risk exposure?
CFOs are also critical gatekeepers, particularly for large investments, evaluating whether AI tools
deliver genuine deflationary benefits or simply shift costs. Emerging roles such as Chief Digital
Officers and Chief AI Officers are gaining influence in some health systems, though CIO and
COO remain the primary decision-makers in most organizations today.
Primary Hurdle: The “Integration Tax.” The single biggest administrative hurdle is the cost and
time required to integrate each new AI tool with core systems, particularly the Electronic Health
Record (EHR). Every point solution requires custom interface development, security review,
workflow redesign, and staff training. This “integration tax” often exceeds the cost of the AI tool
itself and creates a strong bias toward inaction.
Health systems that have adopted unified workflow platforms report significantly lower
integration burden for new AI capabilities, because the platform provides a common
integration layer and pre-built connectors to major clinical and operational systems. HHS can
help by promoting open API standards for operational data (not just clinical data), reducing the
custom development required to connect AI tools to health system infrastructure.
8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research, and
accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? [HIGH PRIORITY]
Beyond Clinical Data: The Case for Operational Interoperability. Current interoperability
frameworks (FHIR, USCDI) focus on clinical data: patient records, medications, lab results,
diagnoses. This is essential for care coordination but insufficient for operational efficiency. The
data that drives healthcare costs, and therefore the data AI needs to achieve deflationary
impact, often lives outside the clinical record:
• Staffing levels and shift schedules
• Bed availability and patient placement status
• Equipment location and maintenance status
• Supply chain inventory and consumption rates
• IT service requests and resolution queues
• Facilities status (room turnover, environmental conditions)

The Opportunity: “Command Center” Intelligence. If AI tools could access a unified, real-time
view of both clinical and operational data, a new category of applications becomes possible.
Imagine AI that can:
• Predict patient discharge readiness and automatically trigger room turnover and
transport
• Match predicted admission volumes against staffing schedules and flag gaps before
they create bottlenecks
• Identify supply consumption anomalies and adjust orders proactively
• Route non-clinical requests (IT, facilities, HR) to resolution without human triage
This “command center” model requires data liquidity across the entire enterprise—not just the
clinical record.
Recommendation: Extend Information Blocking Rules to Operational Systems. HHS should
consider extending information blocking rules to operational data systems. Just as EHRs are now
required to share clinical data, legacy operational systems -scheduling, staffing, supply chain,
facilities - should be required to share data via open APIs. This transparency-focused approach
aligns with the Administration’s philosophy of promoting openness over prescriptive
requirements. Rather than dictating how health systems must use AI, it simply requires that the
data AI needs be accessible. Workflow platforms that connect clinical and operational systems
are well-positioned to enable this integration, providing the data fabric that AI requires to deliver
systemic efficiency.
