On behalf of the American Academy of Orthotists and Prosthetists (The Academy), we appreciate the
opportunity to respond to the Department of Health and Human Services’ Request for Information
regarding the adoption and use of artificial intelligence (AI) in clinical care.
Founded in 1970, The Academy represents certified and state-licensed orthotists, prosthetists, assistants,
technicians, fitters, and researchers dedicated to advancing evidence-based orthotic and prosthetic (O&P)
care. More than five million Americans live with limb loss or limb difference, and millions more rely on
orthotic devices to walk, work, prevent falls, correct spinal deformities, or manage neuromuscular
impairment. O&P care is not elective technology; it is essential rehabilitative infrastructure that supports
mobility, independence, workforce participation, and long-term health across the lifespan. Federal policy
decisions affecting innovation, reimbursement, and regulation in this field therefore carry direct
implications for a substantial and medically complex patient population.
Artificial intelligence technologies have meaningful potential to improve patient outcomes, enhance
clinical efficiency, and support data-informed device selection, fitting, and monitoring within O&P
practice. At the same time, our members have identified important considerations that must be addressed
to ensure responsible and evidence-based integration of AI into clinical workflows. The Academy stands
ready to assist HHS in responsibly advancing the integration of AI into clinical care as it applies to O&P
patient populations. In O&P practice, AI may be incorporated into electronic health record systems used
for documentation and reimbursement, as well as embedded within the physical components of orthoses
and prostheses provided to patients.

Regulatory Framework and Patient Safety
AI applications in O&P care may include AI-supported pressure mapping, gait analysis, predictive risk
modeling, device optimization algorithms, ambient documentation systems, and remote monitoring
technologies. These tools may enhance device fit, reduce fall risk, mitigate skin breakdown, and support
more precise clinical decision-making.
However, our members have expressed concern regarding HIPAA compliance, data governance, and the
handling of protected health information when AI systems are used in clinical environments. Some
members have suggested avoiding AI use altogether due to the uncertainty regarding privacy safeguards
and model training practices.
Additionally, our members report apprehension regarding AI “hallucinations” – the risk of confident, yet
inaccurate outputs influencing documentation, billing justification, or patient counseling.
To address these concerns, The Academy encourages HHS to:
• Provide clear, accessible guidance from the HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) regarding HIPAA
compliance for AI systems used in clinical care.
• Direct the Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy/Office of the National Coordinator for Health
Information Technology (ASTP/ONC) to promote transparent data governance standards addressing
model training, retention, and secondary use of PHI.
• Explore, in coordination with CMS, whether existing accreditation or quality frameworks can
appropriately incorporate standards for the clinical use of AI tools.
• Encourage the FDA to develop performance validation, auditability, and explainability standards for
AI-enabled clinical decision-support systems.
Regulatory clarity and enforceable safeguards will be essential to fostering clinician trust and responsible
innovation. HHS may consider whether a voluntary certification framework or recognized standards
program, including evaluation of existing supplier and quality standards, would improve trust and
compliance in the clinical use of AI tools. AI tools used for documentation should be regulated differently
than AI tools that directly influence device function or clinical decision-making.
Reimbursement and Payment Policy
Current Medicare DMEPOS payment structures are tied to HCPCS codes describing physical components
of orthoses and prostheses. AI-enabled functionality, including embedded software, sensor-driven
analytics, remote monitoring systems, and clinical decision-support tools, is not clearly recognized under
the statutory framework established in SSA §1834(h).
While AI tools can improve documentation efficiency and assist in meeting payer medical policy
requirements, current reimbursement models do not reward improvements in documentation quality,
outcome tracking, or workflow efficiency.
This misalignment creates several challenges:

• AI tools that enhance safety or efficiency may not be economically sustainable.
• Clinicians bear financial risk when investing in AI-enabled technologies without clear payment
pathways.
• Innovation may be discouraged despite potential long-term value to the Medicare program.
Where AI-enabled tools objectively improve device fit, reduce falls, mitigate skin breakdown, or prevent
secondary complications, appropriate reimbursement alignment may support long-term Medicare
savings and improved patient outcomes. The Academy encourages HHS to examine:
• Whether HCPCS coding pathways could recognize AI-enabled device components or digital
augmentations integrated into orthotic and prosthetic technologies.
• How embedded software and sensor-based analytics may be appropriately incorporated into
DMEPOS payment methodologies.
• Demonstration models through the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation to evaluate cost
savings and outcome improvements associated with AI-integrated O&P care.
• Whether certain AI-enabled documentation or monitoring tools could be considered under existing
remote monitoring or digital health benefit categories, and, where clinically appropriate and
consistent with statutory authority, clarify whether O&P practitioners may participate in such billing
pathways.
Any modernization of reimbursement frameworks should remain aligned with patient outcomes, safety,
and long-term value.
As identified in the RFI, there are “inherent flaws in legacy payment systems” that stifle innovation and
access to new and emerging technologies. The Academy supports revisiting these legacy payment systems
to ensure access to the “highest-value interventions for patients” whether those interventions
incorporate AI-enabled functionality or not. Importantly, improvements in documentation efficiency
resulting from AI-supported tools should not be interpreted as reductions in clinical labor intensity for
purposes of applying productivity adjustments within the HCPCS fee schedule.
Use of AI in Claims Adjudication and Prior Authorization
The Academy is also concerned about the use of AI-enabled systems by payers in claims processing,
utilization management, and prior authorization determinations. O&P treatment decisions are highly
individualized and clinically nuanced. If AI tools are used to automate or influence denials without
meaningful human review, patients may experience inappropriate delays or denials of medically
necessary devices, resulting in preventable functional decline, falls, skin breakdown, or loss of
independence.
AI should not function as a substitute for individualized clinical review, nor should it be used to circumvent
established coverage standards. Accordingly, HHS should ensure that payer use of AI in claims
adjudication and prior authorization is subject to robust transparency and meaningful human oversight,
including:
• Requiring review of denials by a qualified clinician with relevant expertise.

• Ensuring that meaningful peer-to-peer review processes are preserved, requiring that treating

• Requiring disclosure to patients and practitioners when AI tools are used in coverage or payment

• Monitoring for discriminatory or biased outcomes, particularly among individuals with disabilities and
medically complex conditions.
Research and Evidence Development
There is a significant lack of research specific to orthotic and prosthetic populations regarding AI use in
clinical practice. While AI tools are evolving rapidly, policies governing their integration should be
evidence-based and population-specific.
We have identified the following needs:
• Research assessing patient and clinician perspectives on AI in O&P care.
• Evaluation of efficacy in workflow integration.
• Outcome studies examining safety, functional improvements, and cost impact.
• Federal grant opportunities to support this work.
The Academy urges HHS to support targeted research initiatives, including partnerships with patient
advocacy organizations and academic centers, to ensure that AI-related policy decisions are informed by
high-quality data relevant to the O&P population. The Academy encourages coordination with NIH, AHRQ,
and PCORI to support implementation science and patient-reported outcomes research specific to O&P
populations.
Workforce and Clinical Burden
Members report that AI tools have met or exceeded expectations as they relate to clinical documentation
support, including ambient listening systems, summarization of payer policies, and drafting of encounter
notes.
Orthotists and prosthetists report that documentation burden reduces time available for direct patient
interaction. AI-supported documentation, when appropriately validated and compliant with privacy
standards, may reduce administrative workload and allow providers to devote more time to patient care.
HHS policy should recognize both the potential benefits and the need for guardrails in this domain.
Conclusion
Artificial intelligence, if thoughtfully implemented, may also improve equitable access to orthotic and
prosthetic care by supporting standardized clinical assessment, expanding remote monitoring capabilities,
