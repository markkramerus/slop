At the outset, IHE believes it is important to state clearly that the Assistant Secretary for
Technology Policy / Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology
(ASTP/ONC) has been a principal sponsor, strategic collaborator, and long-standing policy
partner of IHE for more than two decades. ASTP/ONC’s sustained engagement has helped
translate federal health IT priorities into practical, testable, real-world interoperability
across the U.S. healthcare system. This collaboration has supported national objectives
related to patient safety, data exchange, security, clinical workflow integration, and public
trust; foundational elements that are equally critical to the successful adoption of Artificial
Intelligence (“AI”) in clinical care.
IHE is a global, non-profit, multi-stakeholder initiative that convenes clinicians, vendors,
health systems, standards development organizations, and regulators to develop
implementation guidance (“IHE Profiles”) and to validate those specifications through
rigorous, public, real-world testing events (Connectathons). IHE does not develop AI
algorithms, sell products, certify vendors, or provide clinical services. Our role is to ensure
that complex health IT ecosystems, including AI-enabled systems, can reliably exchange
data, integrate into clinical workflows, and be governed safely at scale.
Interoperability as a Prerequisite for Clinical AI Adoption
Across healthcare, and particularly in data-intensive domains such as medical imaging,
IHE’s experience consistently demonstrates that the most significant barriers to AI

adoption are not algorithmic performance, but fragmentation of data, workflows, and
accountability across systems.
Effective clinical AI depends on:
● Timely access to high-quality clinical data from multiple systems
● Reliable clinical context (orders, protocols, patient characteristics)
● Predictable integration into operational workflows
● Traceability of AI outputs for audit, oversight, and continuous improvement
Absent robust interoperability, AI tools become difficult to deploy safely, expensive to
maintain, and challenging to evaluate once in use. These challenges increase operational
risk for providers, slow innovation, and ultimately undermine public trust.
IHE’s ongoing work reflects this reality. For example, IHE Radiology has developed the
AI Results (AIR) and AI Result Assessment for Imaging (AIRA) Profiles, which
define how AI-generated findings and their subsequent assessment can be encoded,
stored, queried, and retrieved using established healthcare standards such as DICOM
and HL7, while aligning with modern web-based interoperability approaches including
DICOMweb and HL7 FHIR. Rather than introducing parallel AI-specific data models,
this work demonstrates how existing standards infrastructure can be profiled and used
in combination to support interoperable, traceable AI integration within clinical systems.

In addition, IHE Radiology is advancing Manifest-Based Access to DICOM Objects
(MADO), which supports the structured exchange of a manifest identifying specific
DICOM objects—such as relevant prior studies, series, or images—associated with a
current clinical encounter. By enabling systems to reference and retrieve imaging
objects that are highly likely to be clinically relevant, rather than requiring manual search
across entire prior studies, MADO supports more efficient workflows and more reliable
data access. Such structured data access is particularly important in AI-enabled
environments where relevance, context, and traceability are essential.

Barriers to Innovation and Adoption
From an interoperability and implementation perspective, key barriers include inconsistent
use of standards for AI-related data exchange, limited mechanisms for tracing AI outputs
to source data and model versions, and the absence of scalable approaches for post-

deployment performance monitoring. These gaps frequently force providers and
innovators into bespoke, vendor-specific integrations that do not scale and inhibit
competition.
IHE’s experience in imaging environments illustrates these challenges clearly. AI
outputs must be represented in standardized formats, stored alongside imaging studies,
retrieved in predictable ways, and subject to structured review and assessment. The
AIR and AIRA Profiles were developed specifically to address these integration and
traceability concerns within diagnostic imaging workflows, enabling structured
representation of AI findings and documentation of human or system assessment of
those results.
Evaluation and Post-Deployment Monitoring
IHE strongly supports HHS’s emphasis on real-world evaluation of AI systems. Pre-
deployment validation alone cannot ensure sustained safety, equity, and effectiveness
across diverse care environments.
Interoperability-enabled evaluation approaches—including standardized representations
of AI inputs, outputs, confidence measures, and audit trails—are essential to support
ongoing monitoring, bias detection, and performance benchmarking.
The AIRA Profile, for example, defines mechanisms to document structured assessment of
AI results, enabling systems to record whether AI outputs were accepted, modified, or
rejected, and to support quality monitoring and post-market surveillance activities. This
type of standards-based assessment infrastructure can contribute meaningfully to the
governance and oversight models contemplated in this RFI.
Federal support for evaluation frameworks that rely on interoperable data exchange, rather
than proprietary reporting mechanisms, would materially strengthen the AI ecosystem.
Supporting Accreditation and Industry-Driven Testing
IHE encourages HHS to continue supporting and aligning with private-sector conformance
testing, accreditation, and certification activities that validate not only algorithmic claims,
but real-world interoperability and workflow integration.
IHE’s Connectathon testing program provides a structured, multi-vendor environment
where interoperability capabilities—including AI-related integration—can be validated.
Vendors that successfully demonstrate interoperability through multi-vendor testing may
be recognized through mechanisms such as the IHE Connectathon SEAL, which
provides a transparent, non-regulatory signal that specific interoperability capabilities
have been objectively tested.
ASTP/ONC’s long-standing collaboration with IHE demonstrates how public-private
partnerships can reduce provider burden while increasing predictability, safety, and
trust—outcomes that are directly applicable to AI-enabled clinical systems.

Offering a Practical, Testable Execution Pathway
While ASTP/ONC and many healthcare stakeholders are familiar with IHE’s role, other
federal partners involved through HHS’s interagency coordination may be less aware of
existing mechanisms that already support real-world validation and testing of complex
health IT capabilities.
IHE respectfully offers its experience and infrastructure as a practical execution pathway
that HHS and its federal partners could leverage to explore scalable, non-regulatory
approaches to AI validation and oversight.
Specifically, IHE provides:
● A neutral, multi-stakeholder forum where vendors, providers, and regulators
collaboratively define implementable requirements aligned with policy goals
● Operational implementation guidance that translates standards and policy intent
into deployable system behavior
● Public, real-world testing environments (Connectathons) where interoperability,
workflow integration, and traceability can be validated across multiple vendors and
systems
● Feedback loops that surface real-world implementation challenges early, before
they become systemic risks
Rather than creating new federal testing programs or bespoke validation frameworks, HHS
could consider how existing, industry-supported mechanisms—such as IHE Profiles and
Connectathons—might be used or extended to evaluate interoperability, auditability, and
workflow integration of AI-enabled clinical systems in a practical, repeatable manner.
IHE has recently launched a new program, IHE PRISM, designed to prioritize
interoperability in support of key market imperatives. This framework provides an ideal
structure to accelerate the adoption and responsible use of artificial intelligence in clinical
care. The IHE team looks forward to exploring with ASTP/ONC and other stakeholders how
this new approach can help advance shared goals in this area.
IHE does not regulate vendors or assess clinical efficacy. However, decades of experience
demonstrate that transparent, collaborative testing of real-world system behavior
materially improves safety, reduces implementation burden, and accelerates responsible
adoption. These same principles are directly applicable to AI-enabled clinical care.
