Response to Request for Information
HHS Health Sector AI RFI
Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care
VALID AI Collaborative
February 23rd, 2026
Introduction
VALID AI is a community of more than 300 senior healthcare executives—including CIOs, Chief
AI Officers, CMIOs, CISOs, and other health system leaders—from over 70 leading healthcare
organizations committed to advancing the responsible adoption of artificial intelligence. Our
members and partner organizations represent academic medical centers, integrated delivery
systems, payers, community health systems, and innovation leaders.
To inform this response, VALID AI worked with its membership to identify shared barriers to
adoption and convened a focused working group of member institutions and partners. The
working group included leaders from Cedars-Sinai, Sutter Health, Valir Health, CTel, and the
American Medical Association. The perspectives reflected here represent real-world deployment
experience across diverse care settings.
Our recommendations focus on three questions:
1. How can AI become deflationary rather than cost-inflating?
2. How can AI improve access to care?
3. What can HHS and its components do to accelerate responsible and secure
adoption?

I. Making AI Deflationary
AI will reduce healthcare costs only if the cost of validating, integrating, monitoring, and
reimbursing AI is lower than the value it generates. Today, much of the expense stems from
duplicative validation, unclear regulatory boundaries, fragmented monitoring, and
reimbursement misalignment—not model development itself. The federal government can
reduce this structural friction by centralizing non-differentiated trust-building functions.
Policy Actions
1. Establish a Risk-Tiered Federal Validation Framework
● FDA, ONC, and NIH should coordinate on a dynamic, risk-based validation portfolio
structured by clinical impact and AI function type.
● Rigor should scale with risk.
● Benchmarks, subgroup performance standards, and robustness protocols should be
standardized and publicly available.
2. Launch Federally Coordinated AI Validation Programs
● HHS should support structured validation programs that test models against
standardized criteria once at high quality and make results broadly accessible.
● FDA should align regulated product oversight; ONC should integrate outputs into health
IT certification pathways.
3. Develop Representative National Reference Datasets
● NIH and NLM should steward diverse, privacy-preserving datasets reflecting rural,
underserved, and heterogeneous care environments.
● ONC should align datasets with interoperability standards.
4. Create a National AI Adverse Event and Risk Signal Repository
● FDA and AHRQ should coordinate centralized signal detection for AI-related adverse
events.
● ONC should ensure interoperable reporting standards.
Centralized signal detection would reduce redundant investigations and accelerate improvement
cycles.
5. Align Reimbursement With AI-Enabled Value
● CMS should systematically evaluate which CPT, HCPCS, and value-based payment
measures are directly affected by specific AI applications.

● CMS should partner with providers and industry to identify AI categories that measurably
improve quality metrics, risk adjustment, documentation accuracy, or care management.
● The CMS Innovation Center should pilot reimbursement models recognizing AI-enabled
workflow and decision support.
Without reimbursement alignment, AI risks adding administrative burden rather than reducing
cost.
II. Improving Access to Care Through AI
In rural and underserved settings, the alternative to AI-supported care is often delayed or absent
care. This shifts the risk-benefit calculus. AI can function as a capacity amplifier—extending
expertise, reducing provider burden, and enabling earlier intervention.
Federal policy can help ensure AI expands access rather than concentrating it in well-resourced
systems.
Policy Actions
1. Prioritize Rural and Underserved Demonstration Projects
● CMMI and HRSA should fund AI-enabled pilots focused on access expansion,
time-to-intervention, provider burden reduction, and avoided downstream utilization.
● AHRQ should develop evaluation frameworks tailored to low-resource environments.
2. Recognize Context-Sensitive Risk Differentiation
● FDA and CMS guidance should acknowledge that acceptable AI deployment thresholds
may differ where specialist access is limited, while maintaining clear safety monitoring
standards.
3. Strengthen Interoperability as Access Infrastructure
● ONC should modernize API and certification requirements to reduce integration friction
for AI tools.
● Standardized AI performance reporting formats should be incorporated into health IT
certification pathways.
Access expansion depends on reducing deployment barriers in distributed and
resource-constrained settings.

III. Accelerating Responsible Adoption Across
the System
Adoption slows when organizations face regulatory ambiguity, unclear liability boundaries, and
inconsistent transparency expectations. Coordinated federal guidance can reduce uncertainty
without lowering standards.
Policy Actions
1. Provide Cross-Component Regulatory Clarity
● FDA, ONC, and CMS should issue coordinated guidance clarifying classification of
ambient AI systems, data ingestion boundaries, and responsibility allocation between
developers and providers.
2. Support AI-Enabled Transparency and Consent Mechanisms
● ONC and CMS should encourage or incentivize structured disclosure tools that inform
patients when AI influences care and how oversight is maintained.
3. Reduce Procurement and Integration Friction
● HHS should explore standardized AI performance reporting templates and safe-harbor
guidance for adoption of federally validated tools.
Clear rules and shared infrastructure procurement cycles and lower systemwdie adoption costs.
Conclusion
AI will become deflationary when the federal government reduces the system-wide cost of
creating trust. By standardizing validation expectations, supporting validation programs, building
representative datasets, centralizing signal detection, aligning reimbursement, and coordinating
regulatory clarity, HHS can eliminate redundant effort and lower structural barriers.
Paired with targeted support for rural and underserved deployment, this approach allows AI to
serve as a capacity amplifier—expanding access while maintaining accountability.
The objective is responsible acceleration: lower friction, aligned incentives, and shared
infrastructure that enables safe, scalable adoption across the healthcare system.
