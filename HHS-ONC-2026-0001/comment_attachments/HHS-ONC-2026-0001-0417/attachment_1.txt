To Secretary Robert F Kennedy, Jr., U.S. Department of Health and Human Services
(HHS), and Steven Posnack, Principal Deputy Assistant Secretary for Technology Policy
(ASTP):
The Digital Medicine Society (DiMe) is a global non-profit dedicated to advancing the
safe, effective, and equitable use of digital approaches to redefine healthcare and
improve lives. We appreciate the opportunity to respond to this Request for
Information (RFI) on accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence (AI) in
clinical care.
DiMe convenes the ecosystem directly responsible for operationalizing AI adoption:
developers, health systems, regulators, policymakers, payors, and patients. Our
response focuses on practical barriers and policy levers identified by our team and
community, emphasizing alignment across regulation, payment, governance,
interoperability, and workforce readiness.
1. Barriers to AI Innovation and Adoption
The most significant barriers to private sector innovation and adoption of AI in clinical
care are economic, operational, and governance-related.
● Payment and revenue friction : Startups lack early revenue pathways to
generate sustainable income prior to broad reimbursement. Fee-for-service
payment structures reward clinician time and service volume rather than
efficiency, automation, or measurable outcome improvement.
● Regulatory and governance uncertainty : Developers face unpredictability in
FDA review timelines and expectations amidst evolving guidance, particularly
when determining whether tools meet the definition of a medical device.
Health systems and clinicians also face challenges in determining whether AI
outputs are reliable, valid, and appropriate for their patient populations.
● Health system procurement and workflow integration : Selling into hospital
systems remains difficult due to point-solution fatigue, data and platform
fragmentation, and heterogeneous privacy, security, and legal review processes.
Integration into existing workflows, particularly alignment across IT
infrastructure and governance guardrails, is disparate and resource-intensive.

2. Priority Regulatory and Payment Changes
HHS should prioritize regulatory and payment coordination that reflects the realities
of adaptive AI systems and evolving clinical use cases.
● Support coordinated and redesigned payment models : The collaboration
between U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) TEMPO program and
Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services’ (CMS) ACCESS initiative shows how
coordinated regulatory and payment structures could support adaptive
oversight and reimbursement redesign. CMS can further use existing payment
authorities (e.g., 42 CFR § 410.26; 42 CFR § 414.40) and value-based program
mechanisms (e.g., 42 CFR Part 414, Subpart O) to incentivize adoption of AI in
high-burden, undermeasured conditions, including women’s health .
● Illustrate clearer boundaries through use cases : The ecosystem requires
greater insight into how models are trained and why certain AI products do or
do not fall under FDA oversight as medical devices. Recent examples, such as
Doctronic’s AI-supported prescription renewal, illustrate the need for
transparent, use-case-driven explanations so developers and health systems
can design and implement responsibly within defined regulatory guardrails.
● Enable adaptive oversight pathways : Where strong post-market monitoring,
documentation, and performance dashboards are in place, HHS should
consider mechanisms that allow expanded AI functionality or streamlined
review processes without requiring duplicative submissions. The recent citizen
petition requesting exemption from certain premarket notification
requirements ( FDA-2025-P-5560 ) illustrates industry interest in tying regulatory
flexibility to demonstrable lifecycle monitoring capability.
Regulatory clarity, payment redesign, and cross-agency alignment will reduce
uncertainty and accelerate responsible AI adoption.
3. Legal and Implementation Challenges
Non-device AI tools raise governance and accountability challenges that existing
frameworks do not fully address.
● Training on proportional risk : The updated clinical decision support system
(CDSS) guidance , emphasizing transparency and interpretability, introduces a
need to train clinicians to review AI outputs relative to the strength of evidence
and potential clinical impact.

● Integrating data from non-devices : The updated general wellness guidance ,
focused on physiologic parameter outputs, may result in disparate forms of
clinical measures being integrated into the EHR. When these data influence
clinical workflows, governance mechanisms must ensure the integrity,
auditability, accountability, and appropriate use of all inputs to clinical
decision-making.
● Fragmented frameworks : Scaling innovative technology is slowed by disparate
privacy, security, and legal frameworks across health systems. Establishing a
harmonized baseline would reduce duplicative review processes that delay
adoption, such as by endorsing pathways like the DiMe Seal , which was
developed to create a consistent benchmark for evidence, privacy, security, and
usability for everyone adopting digital health software products.
HHS can play a coordinating role in clarifying and aligning how these expectations
function across agencies.
4. AI Evaluation Methods for Clinical Care
Effective evaluation requires both structured pre-deployment benchmarking and
disciplined post-deployment monitoring.
● Pre-deployment benchmarking : AI tools should be evaluated against defined
performance metrics (e.g., drift and bias), with clear documentation artifacts
describing intended use, limitations, and validation methods. DiMe’s The Playbook :
Implementing AI in Healthcare includes a section detailing relevant metrics
such as defined KPIs and measures of success.
● Post-deployment monitoring : Live tools should undergo regular evaluation
against those benchmarked metrics, supported by performance dashboards
that enable human oversight and, as capabilities continue to advance, agentic
supervisory approaches to detect drift or degradation across deployments (e.g.,
"AI monitoring AI"). The Playbook includes information on a minimum monitoring
stack and governance best practices, and DiMe is committed to building upon
this foundation with our upcoming project focused on operationalizing AI
governance in healthcare .
● Ecosystem-level learning : Rather than mandating a single harmonized
evaluation process, HHS should support mechanisms that enable transparent
sharing of implementation lessons, including case study libraries and federated
sandbox environments that capture both successes (e.g., improved outcomes)
and failures (e.g., evidence of harm).

5. Private-sector Activities HHS Can Support
HHS can support private-sector activities that strengthen trust and reduce
duplicative oversight burdens for AI-enabled healthcare.
● Integration of comprehensive frameworks : Recognizing comprehensive
frameworks of standards and best practices, such as the DiMe Seal , will build
trust in the market, accelerate the adoption of digital health tools to improve
health outcomes, and invite a continuous cycle of rigorous evaluation to
elevate the practices of digital health. HHS should explore pathways to
incorporating these frameworks into federal programs (e.g., Medicare App
Library) where appropriate.
● Streamlined regulatory flexibility mechanisms : While the FDA’s precertification
pilot program did not achieve its intended outcomes, alternative approaches to
regulatory flexibility, such as mechanisms contemplated in the citizen petition
( FDA-2025-P-5560 ), should be further considered, as they may offer more
targeted pathways to streamline oversight supported by robust lifecycle
monitoring.
Together, these activities can promote innovative and effective AI use in clinical care
while reducing duplicative review and strengthening accountability.
6. Where AI Has Met Expectations or More Evidence is Needed
In certain applications that directly impact clinical operations, care providers, and
patient experience, AI tools are already meeting or exceeding expectations. Where
evidence is variable or limited, impact will depend on integration and monitoring.
● Surgical scheduling : AI to predict accurate surgical case duration has
demonstrated improved surgical suite utilization and margins.
● Automated triage : AI to expedite psychiatric triaging promises to address long
wait times for mental health care by facilitating more timely, potentially
lifesaving care via improved intake in behavioral health settings.
● Ambient documentation : Peer-reviewed literature reports reduced
documentation burden and improved clinician experience following ambient AI
scribe deployment, while realized productivity and financial impact will depend
heavily on workflow integration and performance monitoring.
Comprehensive monitoring as part of operationalized governance will further
illuminate where and how AI makes the most impact in clinical care settings.

7. Decision-makers and Administrative Barriers
Adoption of AI in clinical care is shaped by internal operational decision-making
structures, including people, processes, and technologies.
● Roles and decision-makers : IT departments, formal or informal AI governance
committees (where they exist), and individuals responsible for workforce
training and education play central roles in determining whether and how AI
tools are safely adopted.
● Administrative hurdles : Primary administrative barriers include lack of
operationalized governance processes, point-solution fatigue, workflow
integration challenges, and limited access to an ecosystem of tools that meet
harmonized privacy, security, and integration requirements and can be
deployed quickly with robust post-deployment monitoring.
● Supporting resources : Efforts including DiMe’s upcoming Operationalizing AI
Governance in Healthcare project, the governance section of The Playbook :
Implementing AI in Healthcare , and the DiMe Seal provide practical support for
organizations implementing consistent oversight, accelerating responsible
adoption across the ecosystem by building a strong foundation of trust.
Clarifying roles, reducing administrative friction, and equipping organizations with
practical evaluation frameworks and governance tools will be essential to scaling
clinical AI adoption responsibly.
8. Interoperability and Data Standards
Clarifying what interoperability is intended to achieve, and supporting it with unified
code systems and documentation artifacts, will help developers avoid unnecessary
implementation hurdles.
● Interoperability beyond EHR integration : Interoperability should foremost
enable timely and safe access to actionable information for clinical users.
Rather than requiring full archival integration into the EHR, human-centered
dashboards supported by standardized API access can provide a faster and
safer pathway to AI implementation. API availability should support third-party
integration without creating unnecessary barriers to health system adoption.
● Code system expansion : The Veterans’ Administration (VA) developed a
patient-generated health data (PGHD) code system , demonstrating how
structured integration pathways can support implementation within a defined
ecosystem. However, approaches that are not industry-wide limit broader
development, interoperability, and adoption across systems and care settings.

● Regulatory documentation artifacts : AI-enabled medical device and

been articulated in recent final guidance , creating a pathway for developers to

User-centered interoperability strategies paired with standardized code systems and
documentation expectations will widen market access while preserving safety and
regulatory integrity in AI-enabled care environments.
9. Patient and Caregiver Challenges
Patient and caregiver AI challenges stem from broad healthcare digitization and
workflow gaps, not resistance to the technology.
● Outdated workflows and misaligned expectations: Both patients and clinicians
experience friction when AI is introduced into legacy workflows. Patients facing
long wait times for appointments often turn to AI to research symptoms or
interpret results, while physicians express concerns about misinformation with
limited time to address AI outputs within existing reimbursement structures.
Introducing AI into outdated workflows without redesign can exacerbate
tension rather than improve care delivery.
● Patient education and records access: Structured education on responsible AI
use can improve clinical encounters and reduce confusion. Educational
priorities include how to securely and efficiently access medical records, how
to interpret AI-generated outputs cautiously, and how to prepare informed
questions for appointments. Modernizing patient-facing reports (e.g., imaging,
pathology, and laboratory results) to be clearer and more structured may also
reduce reliance on AI interpretation and limit avoidable prompting errors.
● Clinician training and trust-building: Clinicians require training to engage
constructively with patients who use AI tools, including how to discuss
AI-generated insights transparently and respectfully. Health systems can
support communication and trust by deploying AI applications that are both
patient- and provider-facing, ensuring shared visibility into tools informing care.
● Supporting resources : DiMe is launching the Scaling Trusted, High-Impact AI
Care Navigation project, focused on care journeys that identify patient and
caregiver friction points and opportunities for AI-enabled workflows that
demonstrate trust, utility, and impact, including return on investment (ROI).

10. Priority Areas for AI Research
Targeted research investments can accelerate adoption by clarifying economic value,
clinical impact, and the infrastructure required to sustain responsible AI deployment.
● ROI models : Research on ROI models for AI tools can clarify how value is
created and distributed across stakeholders, supporting more informed
adoption and payment decisions for both established and emerging use cases.
● Clinical impact studies : Research comparing AI-enabled outcomes with
standard human-led care processes, such as this study demonstrating
consistently favorable outcomes via AI-supported mammography screening,
provide the evidence of clinical impact needed to spur ecosystem adoption.
● Supporting resources : DiMe is committed to convening the digital healthcare
ecosystem in pursuit of answering these high-priority research areas. For
example, our upcoming project Scaling Trusted, High-Impact AI Care Navigation
will produce a trust-utility-impact framework to classify which use clinical
care use cases are ready for AI enablement, alongside evidence-informed ROI
models estimating value in terms of outcomes, cost savings, and retention.
Prioritizing research that connects clinical performance and economic impact will
support responsible scaling of AI in clinical care.
