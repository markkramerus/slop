RE: Request for Information: Accelerating the Adoption and Use of Artificial

Dear Assistant Secretary Keane:
On behalf of the American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) and our nearly 40,000
members, thank you for the opportunity to respond to the “Accelerating the Adoption and
Use of Artificial Intelligence as Part of Clinical Care” request for information (RFI).
Emergency departments serve as a critical entry point to the health care system, operate in
high-acuity and time-sensitive environments, and have already become a testing ground for
a wide range of artificial intelligence-driven (AI) tools. Accordingly, emergency medicine is
an essential lens through which to examine how AI adoption and utilization can supplement
the delivery of high-quality, lifesaving emergency care.
ACEP supports responsible acceleration of AI adoption in clinical care, provided that federal
policy ensures (1) meaningful clinician oversight, (2) clear accountability frameworks, (3)
evidence-based evaluation standards, and (4) transparency in development and deployment.
ACEP's emergency physician members, including those working in academic medical
centers, community hospitals, and safety-net settings, are encountering AI tools in their daily
practice with increasing frequency, often without having meaningfully participated in the
decisions to adopt those tools. Our response to this RFI is informed by input gathered from
emergency medicine clinicians and health system informaticists and is intended to describe
the landscape of uncertainty, concern, and cautious optimism that characterizes emergency
medicine's current relationship with clinical AI. Our comments seek to articulate the key
areas where clarity is most urgently needed, and to express ACEP's willingness to serve as a
partner to HHS as it works to develop a coherent, equitable, and evidence-based framework
for AI in clinical care.
I. Emergency Medicine as a Distinct Clinical Environment
We want to underscore for HHS that emergency medicine presents a set of conditions that
make it both a high-priority area for AI development and a setting in which the consequences
of poorly designed or poorly deployed AI are particularly acute. Emergency departments
manage an unselected patient population, operate continuously, and are frequently under
conditions of crowding, resource constraint, and time pressure. These factors affect both the
types of AI tools that are most likely to be beneficial and the ways in which AI failures are
likely to manifest.
Our members have observed that AI tools which show genuine promise in the emergency
department, including documentation assistance, radiology support for critical finding
detection, and tools to support patient triage, tend to be those that are designed with a clear
understanding of how emergency care is actually delivered.

Conversely, tools designed for elective or inpatient settings, or that generate frequent low-value alerts, tend to create
more burden than benefit. We are also attentive to a concern raised by members regarding the appropriate role of
AI in the emergency department: there is a risk that AI tools will be deployed to manage or obscure systemic
problems, such as crowding, boarding, and staffing shortages, rather than to improve the quality of care delivered
within those constraints. AI adoption in emergency medicine should be problem-driven and tied to evidence of
genuine improvement in patient outcomes or clinician experience, not used as a technological substitute for
addressing structural challenges in emergency care delivery.
II. Liability and Accountability: A Critical Area of Uncertainty
Our members have described a troubling bind: following an AI recommendation that proves incorrect may give rise
to claims of automation bias or over dependence on technology, while ignoring a recommendation that turns out to
have been correct may expose the clinician to allegations of negligence for failing to use available clinical decision
support. This tension places the entire burden of risk on the clinician, despite the fact that emergency physicians
typically have no role in selecting, procuring, validating, or configuring the AI tools they are expected to use.
Several specific areas of uncertainty warrant attention:
• There is currently no established standard of care that defines when a clinician is expected to follow, document
disagreement with, or override an AI-generated recommendation.
• The question of who bears liability when AI is involved including developers, health systems, or individual
clinicians has not been resolved, and existing malpractice frameworks were not designed to accommodate this
complexity.
• When health systems deploy AI tools that have not been validated against local patient populations, it is
unclear whether and to what degree the institution bears responsibility for downstream patient harm.
• The liability question extends well beyond FDA-regulated medical devices to encompass ambient listening
tools, natural language processing applications, clinical decision support systems, and other non-device AI
categories where governance is especially underdeveloped.
ACEP does not prescribe a particular liability model in this response, but we believe that addressing this uncertainty
is prerequisite to responsible AI adoption. We are willing to engage with HHS/ONC and relevant stakeholders to
examine what a workable framework might look like, and to share the perspectives of our frontline members who
are navigating this uncertainty daily.
III. Governance and Physician Involvement in AI Decision-Making
A recurring and frustrating theme in feedback from our members is that emergency physicians are rarely
meaningfully involved in decisions about which AI tools are adopted in their departments. It is ACEP’s position
that artificial intelligence algorithms used in health care decision-making must be transparent, accountable, and
subject to oversight by qualified health care professionals. Yet technology procurement decisions are most
commonly driven by institutional IT departments, finance leadership, and hospital administrators, with clinical input
typically added late in the process and given limited weight.
This is not simply a matter of professional preference. Emergency medicine's care environment is distinctive: it is
characterized by high patient volume, diagnostic uncertainty, time pressure, and a patient population that skews
toward complexity and acuity. AI tools that perform well in other settings frequently underperform or create new
problems when deployed in the emergency department without ED-specific validation. When the clinicians who will
actually use a tool are not involved in evaluating it, the likelihood of poor fit, and the downstream risks to patient
safety, increases substantially.
Our members have raised concerns that mirror those expressed about electronic health record adoption: systems
are selected and implemented with inadequate clinical input, and frontline physicians are left to adapt to tools that
do not reflect how emergency medicine is actually practiced. We believe HHS has an opportunity to address this
structural gap through guidance on post deployment monitoring processes.

IV. Reimbursement and Economic Alignment
Emergency physicians and health system leaders have described a structural economic misalignment in how AI
adoption is currently financed. The costs and effort associated with implementing, validating, training staff on, and
maintaining AI tools are frequently absorbed by clinical departments, while the financial benefits such as reduced
downstream costs, billing efficiencies, system-level savings accrue to health systems, payers, or vendors. This
imbalance creates a disincentive for departments to invest in thoughtful, resource-intensive adoption and contributes
to burnout risk when AI tools become additional unfunded mandates on already-stretched clinical teams.
Current reimbursement models were not designed with AI in mind, and do not adequately account for the full
lifecycle of responsible AI use: pre-deployment validation, staff training, integration support, governance overhead,
and ongoing performance monitoring. Our members have observed that where resources for these activities are not
explicitly allocated, they either do not occur or are performed inadequately thus creating patient safety risks that may
not be apparent until problems emerge in practice.
AI systems used in utilization management, prior authorization, and reimbursement determinations also materially
affect clinical decision-making and patient access to emergency care. ACEP policy supports prohibiting the use of
AI as the sole basis for denial, delay, or downcoding of care and, most importantly, supports requiring review by
qualified physicians in the relevant specialty.
V. Evaluation Standards and the Gap Between Vendor Claims and Real-World Performance
Our members have consistently described a gap between the performance metrics presented by AI vendors and the
real-world behavior of those tools once deployed in an emergency department. AI tools are typically developed and
evaluated on datasets that may not reflect the demographic makeup, data quality, workflow constraints, or clinical
complexity of the populations and settings in which they are eventually used. Emergency department data, in
particular, is often fragmented, entered under time pressure, and structured differently than the cleaner data used in
development environments.
This gap has real consequences. Tools that generate high rates of false-positive alerts, as has been reported with
some sepsis detection systems, create alert fatigue that extends to other clinical decision support tools, potentially
undermining trust in AI systems more broadly. Tools developed for use in other specialties and repurposed for the
emergency department often fail to perform as expected, and the health systems and clinicians bearing the
consequences of those failures have limited recourse.
There is currently no standard framework for how AI tools used in clinical care, particularly those that fall outside
FDA medical device regulation, should be evaluated before deployment or monitored after. Our members have
expressed interest in a more structured approach, though there are differing views about whether emergency
medicine requires a specialty-specific evaluation framework or whether existing standards for medical interventions
can be adapted. These are questions on which further dialogue would be valuable.
Areas where HHS guidance or investment could be particularly impactful include:
• Development of pre-deployment validation standards or guidelines for non-device clinical AI, with particular
attention to the importance of local validation against institution-specific patient populations.
• Support for ongoing post-deployment monitoring infrastructure, including mechanisms to identify and
respond to model drift over time.
• Investment in research capacity at community and mid-size health systems that currently lack the resources
to conduct independent AI validation.
• Promotion of transparency requirements that would allow health systems and clinicians to understand how
AI tools were developed, on what data, and what their known limitations are.
VI. ACEP's Commitment to Partnership
ACEP is committed to ensuring that AI is developed, deployed, and governed in ways that genuinely serve patients
and support the clinicians who care for them. We recognize that this is a rapidly evolving policy area and that
definitive answers to many of the questions raised in this letter are not yet available. Our goal in this response is not

to foreclose options but to ensure that the perspectives of emergency medicine are part of the conversation as HHS
develops its approach.
We are prepared to:
• Participate in technical working groups, advisory panels, or stakeholder convenings convened by HHS on AI
in clinical care.
• Provide access to emergency physician members with direct experience developing, evaluating, and using AI
tools in clinical settings.
• Collaborate on the development of evaluation frameworks, governance standards, or other guidance
documents where emergency medicine input would add value.
• Share ongoing data and feedback from our membership as AI adoption in emergency medicine continues to
evolve.
We look forward to an ongoing dialogue and thank the Department for seeking input from the clinical community.
