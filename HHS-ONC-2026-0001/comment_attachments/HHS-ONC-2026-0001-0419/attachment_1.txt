2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS
prioritize to incentivize the eective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS
regulations, policies, or programs could be revisited to augment your ability to develop
or use AI in clinical care? Please provide specific changes and applicable Code of Federal
Regulations citations.
To incentivize and eectively use AI in clinical care, HHS should prioritize transitioning from
fee-for-service models to outcomes-oriented payment frameworks and clarifying regulatory
definitions that enable healthcare to fully capture the gains of recent breakthroughs in
agentic AI . By aligning financial incentives with clinical results and modernizing standards for
autonomous systems, HHS can reduce administrative burdens and foster a more innovative
health technology ecosystem.
Priority Regulatory and Programmatic Changes
HHS has already taken important steps to advance interoperability, promote value-based
care, and modernize data exchange standards. We support these eorts and believe the
next phase of policy development should focus on ensuring that existing frameworks fully
enable responsible, enterprise-grade AI deployment in clinical care.
Based on our experience, we believe that the following structural and policy modernizations
would do the most to accelerate adoption and unlock new opportunities:
● Codifying "Autonomous Agents" in Information Blocking Rules (45 CFR § 171.102):
HHS should explicitly include automated means and autonomous AI systems acting as
2

clinician "agents" within the definitions of "access" and "use". This clarity would eliminate
the technical or contractual ambiguities that can work to block AI-enabled workflows.
Building upon the existing Information Blocking framework, clarifying this interpretation
would reduce technical or contractual ambiguities that can unintentionally impede
AI-enabled workflows, while remaining consistent with the statute’s intent to prevent
inappropriate interference with information exchange.
● Narrowing the "Infeasibility Exception"(45 CFR § 171.204): Current rules allow for
claims of "infeasibility" to deny rightful data exchange even when technical solutions
exist. Removing the "third party seeking modifications" condition would prevent
platforms from using technical complexity as a pretext. Additional clarification regarding
application of this exception would help ensure it is not interpreted in ways that
unnecessarily delay secure, technically feasible data exchange, consistent with the rule’s
interoperability objectives.
● Establishing "Agent-Ready" Bidirectional APIs (45 CFR § 170.315(g)(10)): Future
FHIR-based API requirements should shift from "read-only" access to supporting
bidirectional workflows. This enables authorized AI agents to securely "write back"
structured data, such as clinical notes or adverse events, into the system of record.
● Modernizing HIPAA De-identification for Multimodal Data (45 CFR § 164.514(b)): HHS
should provide clearer guidance and establish pilot programs for de-identifying
unstructured data types like audio, text, and ambient clinical inputs. Current frameworks
have fallen behind in providing the guidance ideally required for modern AI technologies
to refine models while maintaining rigorous privacy standards.
Payment Policy Prioritization
CMS should prioritize payment models that reward clinical insight over documentation
volume. CMS has made sustained progress in transitioning from volume-based
reimbursement toward value-based and outcomes-driven models. AI-enabled clinical
infrastructure can help accelerate and operationalize these existing policy objectives.
● Outcome-Aligned Payments (OAPs): Shift toward recurring payments for managing
qualifying conditions, with full payment tied to measurable health outcomes rather than
specific billable activities.
● Transcript-Grounded Documentation for Risk Adjustment: Incentivizing the use of
high-fidelity, auditable AI documentation can beer capture patient complexity, which is
often missed in traditional claims data. AI can identify and structure critical non-clinical

factors such as housing, food, or transportation insecurity that directly impact health
outcomes but are rarely coded in standard documentation.
● CMMI Pilots: Explore Innovation Center (CMMI) demonstration authority under Section
1115A of the Social Security Act to test AI-enabled administrative simplification models.
Carefully structured pilots could measure impacts on clinician time, documentation
integrity, risk adjustment accuracy, and patient outcomes, building on existing Innovation
Center eorts.
● AI-Driven Benchmarking and Metrics in APMs: Incorporate AI solutions to objectively
determine if Alternative Payment Models (APMs) are eectively contributing to
value-based care goals by analyzing large datasets of de-identified clinical encounter
documentation. Additionally, consider incorporating ambient AI documentation metrics
into Quality Payment Program (QPP) improvement activities.
● Payment Policy details: Clarify that AI-assisted documentation tools that improve
coding accuracy and reduce administrative burden are permissible and compliant within
Medicare Advantage risk adjustment and Medicare FFS documentation standards.

Data must be clinically useful to train robust AI, but current regulatory standards for
anonymizing that data are struggling to keep pace with modern technology. Abridge
recommends that HHS prioritize a modernized framework for de-identifying unstructured
multimodal data, such as audio, text, and ambient clinical inputs to resolve legal ambiguity
and accelerate safe innovation.
HHS should move beyond categorical removal lists toward a risk-based, standards-driven
approach that emphasizes auditability and security controls.

8. Where would enhanced interoperability widen market opportunities, fuel research,
and accelerate the development of AI for clinical care? Please consider specific data
types, data standards, and benchmarking tools.
Enhanced interoperability is the primary catalyst for shifting AI from a series of "silos" to an
integrated clinical partner. By breaking down barriers between disparate systems, the
healthcare industry can unlock significant market opportunities, fuel critical research, and
accelerate AI development. For AI adoption to transition from isolated departmental pilots to
comprehensive, enterprise-wide strategies, enhanced interoperability is essential.
Standardized data exchange would allow third-party AI "agents" to act on behalf of clinicians,
which opens markets for automated documentation, prior authorization, and real-time
clinical decision support. A centralized, platform-based model minimizes the need for
multiple direct connections, allowing hospitals to add new AI solutions without overwhelming
IT sta.
