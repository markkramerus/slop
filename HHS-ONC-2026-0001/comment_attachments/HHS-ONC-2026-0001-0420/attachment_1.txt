1. Executive Summary: Risk-tiered governance and verification can accelerate
responsible AI adoption in clinical care
Governing thought: HHS can accelerate safe and equitable clinical AI adoption by making governance
and auditability requirements legible, portable, and interoperable across products and sites.
Many adoption failures stem less from algorithmic novelty than from gaps in traceability (what ran,
when, for whom, and under which configuration), change control, and accountable human use in
workflow.
This comment recommends risk-tiered minimum governance and auditability expectations for
non-device clinical AI / decision support, tailored by runtime behavior (deterministic guideline-logic
artifacts vs probabilistic inference services), and implemented through HHS levers that do not alter FDA
jurisdiction over medical devices.
Key message to HHS
● Publish a risk-tiered “minimum governance + auditability expectations” framework for
non-device clinical AI / decision support that is tailored by runtime behavior and intended use,
and explicitly does not modify FDA device jurisdiction.
● Extend (not replace) ONC’s Decision Support Interventions (DSI) transparency and intervention
risk management concepts by emphasizing runtime auditability: version/configuration capture,
provenance, user actions, and change logs that enable retrospective reconstruction.
● Make the “AI audit artifact package” feasible as a minimum: a small set of artifacts that can be
produced, exported, and reviewed across developers, certified health IT, and deployers without
requiring proprietary disclosure of model internals.
● Use ONC Certification implementation guidance and test procedures to ensure certified health IT
can capture/export AI-relevant provenance and user actions for AI-influenced documentation
and orders using FHIR building blocks (SMART, CDS Hooks, SDC, Provenance).
● Use CMS levers (CMMI model terms, MIPS Improvement Activities / Promoting Interoperability
pathways, and hospital quality reporting guidance) to create incentives for deployers to maintain
governance artifacts and post-deployment monitoring appropriate to risk tier and runtime
behavior, with candidate CFR anchors provided for counsel validation.
● Certain internal logical properties of deterministic guideline-logic artifacts (e.g., consistency,
reachability, completeness over defined states) can be formally verified; this does not prove
clinical benefit, data quality, or appropriateness of guideline interpretation across contexts.
● Deterministic artifacts do not require statistical “model drift” monitoring, but they still require
post-deployment safety monitoring for data quality/missingness, user overrides/escalations,
disparate application, and guideline/version updates with regression test outcomes.
● OCR should operationalize Section 1557 requirements for patient care decision support tools (45
CFR § 92.210) through implementation guidance that links nondiscrimination duties to concrete
documentation, monitoring, and mitigation steps.

About the submitter / transparency
PCare+ is a health IT and standards-focused organization that participates in interoperability
implementation and governance discussions relevant to clinical decision support and AI-enabled
workflow integration.
PCare+ may have professional and/or commercial interests in approaches that favor interoperable
auditability and standards-based integration, and this comment is offered to support implementable HHS
policy and program design rather than to advocate for any single product or vendor.
Recommendations are framed as minimum expectations that can be met by multiple technical
architectures and multiple market participants.
Summary recommendation
(1) Distinguish between AI-compiled deterministic artifacts and probabilistic AI services in regulatory,
evaluation, and monitoring frameworks. These are fundamentally different technologies with different
risk profiles. Regulating compiled guideline logic under monitoring rules designed for chatbots imposes
compliance costs without safety benefit.
(2) Adopt formal verification of logical completeness (exhaustiveness, determinism, reachability) as the
evaluation standard for deterministic clinical decision logic, beyond the necessary but insufficient
baseline of audit logging.
(3) Align reimbursement incentives to verified guideline-concordant care, measured by screening
completion, provenance traceability, and patient outcomes, rather than to AI tool access or adoption
volume.
These recommendations are complementary to the existing computable guideline, eCQM, and clinical
decision support ecosystem, and are intended to improve provenance, verification of internal logic
properties where appropriate, workflow integration, and accountability rather than to imply that
computable CDS does not already exist.
Evidence base referenced in this submission: Our analysis of 227 million T-MSIS Medicaid claims rows,
2.99 million CDC BRFSS survey responses, and HRSA UDS data from 18,751 FQHC sites indicates that
preventive screening is declining nationally, even where the infrastructure and coverage mandates to
deliver it exist. Patients and caregivers need understandable disclosure and practical recourse when AI
influences clinical recommendations; a decision artifact whose correctness is provable provides a
stronger foundation than transparency-by-logging alone.

3. Understanding of HHS Context and Objectives: Adoption depends on
governance, interoperability, and accountability in real workflows
This RFI appropriately frames clinical AI adoption as a multi-component HHS problem that spans
interoperability, safety, privacy, civil rights, reimbursement incentives, and evidence generation.
We interpret HHS’s objective as enabling clinically meaningful use of AI with accountable oversight,
without creating duplicative or conflicting regulatory regimes.
We also note that a substantial computable guideline, eCQM, and clinical decision support ecosystem
already exists (e.g., eCQMs, CDS artifacts, SMART-based apps), and our recommendations are intended
to be complementary by improving auditability, provenance, verification of internal logic properties
where appropriate, and workflow integration.
Evaluator-relevant objectives reflected in this comment
● Differentiate AI technologies by runtime behavior and risk profile (e.g., deterministic decision
artifacts vs probabilistic inference services) to apply proportionate controls.
● Emphasize testability, provenance, and retrospective review for higher-risk non-device AI used in
clinical workflows.
● Reduce adoption friction through standards-based interoperability patterns (structured data capture,
app launch, event-driven CDS, and provenance-aware writeback).
● Use federal payment and program mechanisms to shift demand toward tools that are measurable,
governable, and aligned to guideline-concordant care.
● Support learning health system approaches through reusable evaluation materials (test cases,
reporting templates, and monitoring metrics) that can be applied across sites.

evaluation and monitoring expectations.

Operational risk-tier triggers (qualitative rubric)
● Does the output influence diagnosis, treatment, medication, triage, eligibility, or access to services
(vs purely administrative support)?
● Is the output patient-facing (directly consumed by patients/caregivers) or clinician-facing only?
● Is the tool autonomous (acts by default) or clinician-confirmed (requires deliberate
acceptance/attestation)?
● Does the tool generate or modify the legal medical record (documentation/writeback) or only
provide a transient suggestion?
● What is the scale of use (single clinic vs enterprise vs multi-tenant service) and the speed of
propagation of errors?
● How reversible is harm (can incorrect outputs be quickly detected and corrected before patient
impact)?
● Does the tool use protected class variables or proxies in ways that raise discrimination risk (triggering
heightened nondiscrimination governance)?
Operational risk-tier triggers (qualitative rubric)
● Tier 1 (low): administrative or informational support; clinician review; no autonomous actions;
minimal audit artifacts and change logs.
● Tier 2 (moderate): influences documentation/orders/clinical decisions but remains
clinician-confirmed; full “AI audit artifact package” plus monitoring of overrides, data quality, and
disparate application.
● Tier 3 (higher): patient-facing and/or affects triage/access and/or operates with default/autonomous
actions at scale; full audit artifact package plus enhanced evaluation, incident response, and
nondiscrimination documentation aligned to 45 CFR § 92.210.

Question 1: Adoption barriers are governance, interoperability, and operational accountability
RFI question: 1. What are the biggest barriers to private sector innovation in AI for health care and its
adoption and use in clinical care?
In practice, the most material barriers to innovation and adoption often emerge after development. The
friction typically resides in governance, interoperability, and operational accountability once AI outputs
can influence clinical decisions, documentation, or access to services.
Key barriers observed across deployments
● Lack of a regulatory distinction between deterministic and probabilistic clinical AI: Current
frameworks often treat all AI in clinical care as a single category, applying similar evaluation,
monitoring, and governance expectations whether a tool performs probabilistic runtime inference or
executes a pre-compiled, formally verified decision artifact. This can overburden deterministic tools
with requirements such as drift monitoring for logic that cannot drift, while underspecifying
oversight for non-deterministic systems whose runtime behavior is inherently less predictable.
● Accountability and auditability gaps: Many deployments cannot reliably support retrospective
reconstruction of what the tool recommended, under which version and configuration, what inputs
were used, and how the output affected documentation, orders, task routing, or clinician actions.
This limits incident investigation, documentation integrity review, and organizational trust.
● Workflow integration and interoperability burden: AI tools often fail to produce measurable clinical
benefit when they are not integrated into ordering, documentation, and task routing workflows.
Common barriers include launch context, authorization, latency, writeback, provenance capture, and
variability across certified health IT implementations. Inconsistent support for standards-based
invocation and data exchange makes implementation costly and brittle across sites.
● Unstructured guidance and inconsistent clinical data capture: Clinical guidance is often ambiguous
or not computable, and data capture practices vary substantially across organizations. This reduces
reproducibility, increases site-specific customization, and complicates cross-site evaluation.
● Change-control and update management gaps: Organizations frequently lack practical processes for
safe updates, including regression testing, version transition controls, rollback procedures, and
governance over rapid tool iterations and guideline changes. These gaps create operational risk and
slow deployment.

● Monitoring gaps not tailored to risk and runtime behavior: Many deployments lack feasible
post-deployment monitoring that is proportional to risk and appropriate to runtime behavior.
Deterministic and probabilistic tools require different monitoring approaches, but deployers often
lack clear operational guidance on what is sufficient and practical.
● Unclear minimum governance expectations for non-device AI: For non-device AI used in clinical
care, there is often no clear baseline for auditability, provenance, update control, performance
monitoring, and recourse. This uncertainty slows procurement and results in inconsistent controls
across organizations.
● Procurement, legal, and contracting friction: Health systems often repeat security, privacy, and
clinical governance reviews across vendors and products. Adoption is further slowed by unclear
allocation of responsibility between developers and deployers for tool errors, documentation
integrity, and nondiscrimination obligations.
● Equity and civil rights implementation complexity: Deployers often lack practical guidance on how
to document and operationalize reasonable efforts to identify and mitigate discrimination risks,
especially when protected characteristics may be proxied indirectly.
● Resource constraints for smaller, rural, and safety-net providers: Many providers cannot absorb the
ongoing costs of governance, integration, evaluation, and monitoring without aligned incentives,
shared infrastructure, or technical assistance. This risks widening inequities in adoption and access.
● Limited reusable evaluation and conformance tools: There is limited availability of standardized test
cases, reporting templates, and post-deployment monitoring metrics that can be reused across sites.
As a result, organizations duplicate validation and integration work.
Opportunities for Acceleration
● Standardize a portable minimum audit evidence package: HHS can define a baseline AI audit
artifact package that is reusable across products and sites, including intended use, versioning,
provenance, configuration, validation summary, monitoring plan, and recourse process. This would
reduce duplicative review burden and improve accountability.
● Build on existing ONC transparency and intervention risk foundations: HHS can extend existing ONC
transparency and intervention risk approaches toward runtime traceability that supports audits,
incident response, and continuous improvement in clinical operations.
● Strengthen existing computable clinical infrastructure: HHS can accelerate adoption by improving
current ecosystems, including computable guidelines, CDS artifacts, eCQMs, SMART apps, and
standards-based workflows, through stronger provenance, versioning, verification, and conformance
practices rather than replacing them.
● Create shared, governance-backed test harnesses for common workflows: A shared conformance
and testing infrastructure for common integration patterns, including standards-based launch and
invocation, structured data capture, and provenance-aware writeback, would reduce custom
integration burden and improve procurement readiness.

● Align payment and program incentives with governable deployment: Federal payment and
program levers can encourage adoption of tools that are auditable, monitorable, and operationally
accountable, rather than tools selected primarily on claimed functionality.
Recommendations to HHS
● Publish risk-tiered minimum governance expectations for non-device AI used in clinical care: HHS
should publish clear, risk-tiered minimum expectations for auditability and operational governance,
including versioning, provenance, input/output traceability, update control, monitoring, and
recourse, with greater requirements for higher-risk uses.
Why this helps: A predictable baseline reduces procurement uncertainty, supports consistent
controls across settings, and improves investigation when safety, equity, or documentation integrity
concerns arise.
● Distinguish deterministic AI-compiled artifacts from probabilistic AI services: HHS should publish
guidance that differentiates AI-compiled deterministic artifacts from probabilistic runtime AI services
and sets proportionate evaluation and monitoring expectations for each.
Why this helps: This avoids imposing unnecessary monitoring burdens on deterministic artifacts
while ensuring probabilistic systems receive oversight appropriate to their risk.
● Fund open conformance tools and reference implementations for common integration patterns:
HHS should support reusable conformance tools and reference implementations for standards-based
launch, invocation, structured data capture, and provenance-aware writeback.
Why this helps: Interoperability friction is a major source of cost and delay. Shared infrastructure
reduces duplicative implementation work and supports safer deployments.
● Use payment and program policy to require a basic governance package for higher-risk AI-enabled
workflows: For higher-risk AI-enabled workflows, HHS should condition federal payment support or
program participation on submission of a basic governance package, such as intended use,
monitoring plan, audit artifact package, and recourse process, rather than relying only on claimed
functionality.
Why this helps: Payment levers can shift demand toward tools that are governable and measurable
and reduce incentives to deploy poorly monitored systems.
● Provide practical implementation templates for equity and discrimination risk mitigation: HHS
should publish practical templates and examples that help deployers document reasonable efforts to
identify and mitigate discrimination risks in implementation and monitoring.
Why this helps: Many organizations support equity safeguards but lack operational clarity. Templates
can improve consistency and reduce implementation uncertainty.
● Support shared infrastructure and technical assistance for smaller and safety-net providers: HHS
should support shared governance resources, reusable evaluation tools, and technical assistance
pathways for smaller, rural, and safety-net providers.
Why this helps: This reduces structural barriers to adoption and helps ensure that benefits from
AI-enabled care are not limited to large, well-resourced systems.

Question 2: Policy changes should focus on auditability, provenance, and verification,
supported by program incentives and CFR anchors
RFI question: 2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should HHS prioritize
to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why? What HHS regulations, policies, or programs
could be revisited to augment your ability to develop or use AI in clinical care? Please provide specific
changes and applicable Code of Federal Regulations citations.
HHS should implement a jurisdiction-aware approach that focuses on minimum governance, auditability,
and interoperability expectations for non-device clinical AI / decision support, tailored by runtime
behavior and clinical risk.
This approach should be explicitly scoped so that it does not alter FDA jurisdiction over medical devices
and does not purport to “approve” model quality, but instead makes accountability and reconstruction
feasible when AI materially influences clinical care, documentation, or ordering.
Specific changes HHS should consider (with candidate CFR anchors where applicable)
1. ASTP/ONC: publish risk-tiered minimum expectations for non-device clinical AI / decision
support
a. Mechanism: ASTP/ONC guidance/playbook (non-binding) with templates.
b. Deliverable: A risk-tier trigger rubric and minimum expectations for governance artifacts,
auditability, and monitoring tailored by runtime behavior (deterministic guideline-logic
artifacts vs probabilistic inference services).
c. CFR anchor concept (ONC program context): ONC Certification Program decision support
criteria context at 45 CFR 170.315(b)(11) (DSI).
2. ONC Certification Program: make runtime auditability testable where AI materially influences
documentation/orders
a. Mechanism: Implementation guidance + test procedure updates (and, if needed, future
certification criterion refinement through rulemaking).
b. Deliverable: Testable capabilities for (a) capturing tool identity + version/configuration at
runtime, (b) recording provenance for AI-influenced notes/orders, and (c) exporting
relevant audit/provenance data for governance review.
c. CFR anchor: 45 CFR 170.315(b)(11) and related ONC certification provisions (see
Appendix E for candidate anchors).
d. Alignment note: The ONC DSI Resource Guide emphasizes transparency and risk
management and clarifies that the program does not “approve” predictive DSI quality;
our proposal extends DSI by focusing on runtime auditability that enables retrospective
reconstruction.

3. OCR: operationalize nondiscrimination expectations for patient care decision support tools
a. Mechanism: OCR implementation guidance and examples.
b. Deliverable: Practical documentation and monitoring guidance for “reasonable efforts”
to identify and mitigate discrimination risk when using patient care decision support
tools, especially where protected class variables are used or proxied.
c. CFR anchor: 45 CFR § 92.210 (patient care decision support tools) and related Part 92
requirements (see Appendix E).
4. CMS: incent governance artifacts and monitoring through existing program levers rather than
new standalone “AI billing codes”
a. Mechanism (three implementation routes):
i. CMMI model participation/reporting terms (model-specific terms and reporting
requirements)
ii. MIPS (QPP) Improvement Activities and Promoting Interoperability pathways
iii. Hospital quality reporting guidance (e.g., Hospital IQR) where AI materially
influences eligibility/ordering/documentation
b. Deliverable: Program terms and attestations that require maintenance of an AI audit
artifact package and tailored post-deployment monitoring signals when AI materially
influences care processes.
c. Candidate CFR anchors for counsel validation: 42 CFR Part 414, Subpart O (Quality
Payment Program), including 42 CFR § 414.1375 (Promoting Interoperability) and related
Improvement Activities provisions, and 42 CFR § 412.140 (Hospital IQR Program).
Reimbursement realism (standalone billing vs incentives)
The RFI correctly highlights that fee-for-service incentives can distort adoption toward volume rather
than accountability.
We recommend CMS avoid creating a standalone “AI billing code” as the default policy response and
instead tie incentives to (1) outcomes/value-based constructs, (2) validated burden reduction in
bounded workflows, and (3) auditable governance and evaluation artifacts rather than model claims.
This framing supports adoption while reducing incentives for overuse, underdocumentation of risks, or
deployment without monitoring.

intended for procurement and governance use.

Question 3: Novel issues are practical: allocation of responsibility, update governance, record
integrity, and audit access
RFI question: 3. For non-medical devices, we understand that use of AI in clinical care may raise novel
legal and implementation issues that challenge existing governance and accountability structures... What
novel legal and implementation issues exist and what role, if any, should HHS play to help address them?
For non-device AI that influences clinical decisions, documentation, or access decisions, the most
consequential legal and implementation issues are often operational: reproducibility, allocation of
responsibilities, update governance, and record integrity.
Issues that commonly create compliance and safety risk
● Privacy and security (HIPAA and beyond): AI integrations increase data flows and new access
pathways, so deployers need audit controls and documented retention/access policies appropriate
to their risk tier and workflow.
● Record integrity and provenance: when AI generates or influences documentation/orders,
organizations must be able to identify AI-influenced content, reconstruct inputs/outputs, and
preserve clinician accountability for final actions.
● Civil rights and nondiscrimination: patient care decision support tools can introduce discriminatory
effects through input selection, proxies, workflow routing, or differential error rates across pop.
● Patient trust and autonomy: patient-facing or patient-impacting AI requires clear disclosures,
meaningful recourse, and safeguards against deceptive or coercive automation.
● Liability and responsibility allocation: contracts and governance should allocate responsibility for
tool defects, configuration errors, data quality failures, and monitoring omissions.
● Information access and information blocking: interoperability obligations must be implemented
carefully within existing exceptions frameworks rather than implying blanket access rights.
● Patient rights applicability: access/amendment rights depend on whether information is maintained
in the designated record set, and disclosures should avoid overpromising rights that are
context-dependent.
Which HHS offices should be involved (and why)
● ASTP/ONC: to lead interoperability and certification-aligned auditability expectations and convene
standards implementation guidance.
● OCR: to provide implementation guidance for nondiscrimination obligations for patient care decision
support tools under 45 CFR § 92.210 and for privacy-related considerations in patient-facing tools.
● CMS/CMMI: to align incentives and reporting expectations (governance artifacts and monitoring)
through program terms and quality pathways.
● AHRQ: to fund implementation science on human factors, monitoring operations, and equitable
deployment practices, and to support evaluation infrastructure.
● OIG (as appropriate): to align compliance program guidance and oversight concepts with auditability
expectations, especially where AI affects documentation, coding, or medical management processes.

Formal verification for deterministic clinical decision logic
For clinical AI that encodes guideline logic as deterministic decision trees (as opposed to runtime
probabilistic inference), evaluation can include formal verification of logical properties using established
methods from safety-critical software engineering.
Satisfiability Modulo Theory (SMT) solvers can prove, across the full input space rather than a finite test
suite, that: (a) every possible combination of patient inputs leads to at least one recommendation
(exhaustiveness); (b) no combination triggers contradictory recommendations (determinism); and (c)
every encoded recommendation is reachable by at least one patient profile (no dead rules).
Graph-theoretic analysis can verify structural properties including acyclicity (no infinite assessment
loops), connectivity (no orphan recommendations), and minimum path length (minimum patient
burden).
These methods are established practice in aviation software and nuclear safety systems. They are
directly applicable to clinical decision logic encoded in standards-based formats such as FHIR Structured
Data Capture. HHS should fund development of formal verification tooling and reference
implementations for clinical decision artifacts, and should recognize formal verification evidence in
evaluation and procurement frameworks.
Transparency is necessary but insufficient for clinical decision logic that determines screening eligibility
and treatment pathways; the logical correctness of the encoded artifact should be provable, even
though clinical benefit and safe use in context must still be demonstrated through empirical evaluation
and post-deployment monitoring.
Recommendations to HHS
● R&D: Support development and adoption of standardized evaluation and reporting templates for
non-device clinical AI that can be completed without disclosing proprietary model details, aligned to
CONSORT-AI, SPIRIT-AI, and DECIDE-AI.
Why it helps: Standard templates reduce duplicative procurement burden and improve comparability
across sites and tools, accelerating a learning health system approach.
● R&D: Use grants, cooperative agreements, and prize competitions to fund pragmatic, multi-site
evaluations that measure workflow impact, safety outcomes, and equity, and that require version
and provenance reporting.
Why it helps: Real-world performance depends on local context; pragmatic evaluation reduces the
gap between retrospective technical performance and deployed impact.
● Regulation: Encourage risk-tiered post-deployment monitoring expectations (metrics, thresholds,
and escalation) anchored to NIST AI RMF functions (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE) and
compatible with DSI intervention risk management concepts.
Why it helps: A governance anchor helps operationalize monitoring and response without
prescribing a single technical method, enabling proportionate control across AI types and risks.

Question 6: AI value is most consistent in tight, reviewable workflows; verified
preventive-care logic remains high potential
RFI question: 6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance and cost
expectations and where have they fallen short? What kinds of novel AI tools would have the greatest
potential to improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help reduce costs?
The clearest operational wins so far have come from narrow, high-volume workflows where outputs are
reviewable and integration is tight. AI tools are most likely to meet or exceed expectations when
intended use is clear, workflow boundaries are defined, and organizations can monitor how outputs are
actually used.
AI has fallen short when inputs are poorly structured, workflow integration is weak, governance artifacts
are incomplete, or updates are pushed without clear change control. Those gaps create safety and
compliance risk and make it harder to tell whether a tool is creating real value or just shifting work and
cost elsewhere.
Below are short, de-identified vignettes that reflect common deployment patterns, with lessons tied to
governance, auditability, and interoperability:
● Ambient documentation (met expectations in bounded workflows): In multiple deployments,
ambient documentation tools have reduced clinician documentation burden and improved
perceived usability when deployed with clear intended use and workflow boundaries.
Where these deployments perform best, organizations can reconstruct tool
version/configuration and can review edits/overrides, enabling safe iteration and targeted
monitoring.
Lesson: success depends on provenance-aware writeback and user action capture more than on
headline accuracy claims, because the record integrity and clinician accountability requirements
are operationally determinative.
● Imaging worklist triage (met expectations when integrated and monitored): In settings using
imaging triage/priority tools, value is most evident when integration supports measurable
time-to-review improvements and when outputs are monitored for subgroup performance and
alerting fatigue.
Where these tools fall short, the common failure mode is not a single “bad model,” but poor
integration into clinical routing and insufficient auditing of when/why clinicians override.
Lesson: auditability and monitoring should be treated as part of the intervention, not optional
post-hoc documentation.

● Patient-facing symptom checkers (often fell short on diagnostic accuracy and consistency):
Systematic reviews continue to find low and variable diagnostic accuracy across online symptom

Common failure modes include incomplete data capture, inconsistent recommendations across

● Administrative utilization/authorization support (mixed results with transfer/payment

for why an outcome occurred, and insufficient recourse for correction.
Lesson: governance artifacts and traceability are necessary to manage transfer effects and to
support fair, appealable, and auditable processes.
Promising areas for novel tools (as observed)
● Preventive care identification and outreach grounded in computable guideline logic: A
high-potential category is preventive care identification and outreach built on structured data
capture and published guidelines, with traceable logic and auditable outputs.
One promising approach is AI-assisted compilation of guideline artifacts into constrained, reviewable
workflow logic (including deterministic decision trees where appropriate), paired with clinician
review and interoperable writeback. This can support point-of-care identification of eligible services,
more consistent outreach, and clearer documentation of why a recommendation was made.
This category remains especially promising because the core problem is often not lack of
evidence-based recommendations, but unreliable translation of those recommendations into
repeatable workflow execution.
● Care coordination and navigation tools with provenance-aware workflows: strong potential when
they reduce friction across settings while preserving provenance, role-based access control, and
reviewable workflow actions.
High-value use cases include referral completion, follow-up scheduling, preventive service
completion, and transitions across care settings. The opportunity is not only automation of tasks, but
better continuity and accountability across fragmented systems.

● Decision support that writes back reviewable, provenance-tagged artifacts: could improve quality

Evidence referenced on preventive care screening gaps
Analysis of 227 million T-MSIS Medicaid claims rows, cross-validated against 2.99 million CDC BRFSS

for USPSTF A and B grade services, affecting 150-180 million insured Americans. The gap is attributed
here to the absence of computable, verifiable clinical decision logic at the point of encounter.
AI-compiled guideline artifacts that encode USPSTF recommendations as formally verified decision trees
(deterministic, zero marginal cost per patient, FHIR-compliant) are presented as one approach to address
this supply-side bottleneck directly.

tools require careful risk-tiering, disclosures, and monitoring.
HHS can increase the utility of published findings by requiring or incentivizing minimum reporting of tool
identity/version/configuration, provenance/writeback behavior, and monitoring signals so that studies
are interpretable and comparable across sites.
10b. Literature on costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI in clinical care
RFI question: 10b. How does the literature approach the costs, benefits, and transfers of using AI as part
of clinical care?
The economic evaluation literature generally suggests that AI interventions can be cost-effective in
specific contexts, but systematic reviews identify recurring methodological gaps and inconsistent
reporting of AI-specific cost components and implementation realities.
These gaps directly affect HHS’s ability to design incentives that reward real-world value rather than
optimistic modeling assumptions.
What the recent systematic review literature emphasizes (examples)
● A 2025 systematic review of cost-effectiveness analyses of AI applications in healthcare reports
that many studies conclude AI strategies are cost-effective or cost-saving, but also notes

● A 2025 systematic review of economic evaluations for AI-assisted cancer screening/diagnosis

Transfer and incentive considerations (who pays vs who benefits)
In many workflows, costs (integration, monitoring, governance operations) accrue to providers while
financial benefits may accrue to payers or may be diffuse, creating adoption barriers under
fee-for-service.
HHS should therefore design incentives that (1) support measurable workflow burden reduction and
safety monitoring in bounded scenarios, (2) reward auditable governance practices rather than model
claims, and (3) reduce inequitable adoption skew toward highly resourced systems.
Reimbursement realism
Where payment policy is needed, CMS should prefer value-based constructs, quality pathways, and
model participation terms that require auditable governance artifacts, rather than defaulting to a
standalone AI billing code that could create utilization incentives without accountability.

5. Conclusion: Verified, computable clinical reasoning can connect evidence to
action at the point of care
HHS can accelerate responsible clinical AI adoption by making accountability feasible in real-world care
delivery: risk-tiered governance expectations, interoperable auditability (provenance, user actions, and
version/configuration traceability), and incentives for monitoring and evaluation that are proportional to
clinical impact and reversibility.
A central distinction in this submission is that deterministic guideline-logic artifacts and probabilistic
inference services have different runtime behaviors and should not be treated as interchangeable. That
distinction matters for evaluation, monitoring, and reimbursement design. At the same time, both
require core safeguards: documentation integrity, nondiscrimination protections, and post-deployment
safety monitoring appropriate to risk.
The opportunity is not only to expand AI use, but to improve how evidence is translated into action at
the point of care. Clinical guidelines exist. Interoperability standards exist. Coverage pathways often
exist. The remaining gap is frequently operational: reliable, reviewable, computable workflow execution
that can be audited, monitored, and improved across settings.
Priorities emphasized in this submission
● Adopt risk-tiered governance expectations tied to runtime behavior, clinical impact, and
reversibility.
● Strengthen interoperable auditability expectations for invocation, provenance-aware writeback,
user action capture, and runtime version/configuration traceability.
● Differentiate evaluation and monitoring expectations for deterministic guideline-logic artifacts
versus probabilistic inference services, while preserving shared baseline safeguards.
● Align incentives to demonstrated workflow and care value (including monitoring and
governance quality), not just tool deployment or unverified performance claims.
Near-term progress is achievable through existing HHS levers, including guidance, ONC certification
implementation and testing pathways, OCR implementation guidance for 45 CFR § 92.210, and CMS
program terms and quality pathways that reward governable, monitorable deployment.
The infrastructure for safer, scalable clinical AI is already largely in place. The near-term policy task is to
align governance, interoperability, and reimbursement so the market rewards systems that can be
reviewed, reconstructed, and trusted in practice.

A.2 Definitions
The working definitions below are included as provided in the source submission.
Term Definition
Clinical AI / decision Software-enabled methods that generate recommendations, predictions, summaries, or workflow actions
support used in clinical care, including both deterministic and probabilistic approaches.
Deterministic A deterministic, version-controlled artifact that applies defined guideline/policy logic to inputs to produce
guideline-logic artifact repeatable outputs for the same inputs and configuration.
Probabilistic inference A service that produces outputs based on statistical inference, often sensitive to data distributions and
service subject to performance variation over time and across populations.
Formal verification Verification of internal logical properties of a deterministic artifact over defined states (e.g., consistency,
(bounded) reachability, completeness), which does not establish clinical benefit or appropriateness across contexts.
AI audit artifact A minimum set of artifacts (identity, version/config, provenance/writeback behavior, user action capture,
package limitations, monitoring plan, change logs) sufficient to support retrospective reconstruction, governance
review, and incident response.
Provenance Machine-readable representation of who/what generated information and how it was derived, used to
identify AI-influenced content and enable traceability.
Post-deployment Ongoing monitoring for safety signals proportional to risk, including data quality/missingness,
safety monitoring overrides/escalations, disparate application, incidents, and update impacts (and drift monitoring for
probabilistic tools where appropriate).
Risk tier A qualitative classification driven by clinical impact, autonomy, patient-facing use, record writeback, scale,
reversibility, and discrimination risk triggers.
