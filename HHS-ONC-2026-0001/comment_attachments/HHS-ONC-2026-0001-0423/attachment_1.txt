Comment on HHS Health Sector AI RFI
Submitted by: Basys.ai (Company)
Basys.ai appreciates the opportunity to respond to the Department of Health and Human Services' Request for Information
regarding accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence in clinical care.
Basys.ai is an AI-powered healthcare technology company focused on automating prior authorization, utilization management,
and medical review workflows for health plans and government contractors. Through our work with health plans, CMS-aligned
programs, and healthcare administrators, we have observed firsthand the operational barriers, regulatory friction, and workflow
inefficiencies that slow responsible AI adoption in clinical environments.
Our responses below are grounded in real-world implementation experience, including reducing administrative burden in
CMS-related review programs and participation in federal innovation initiatives. We have also included some of our publications
and announcements in the references at the end of this document, including a reference for the Healthcare Moonshot
publication that principally guides our response to the RFI.
1. Biggest Barriers to AI Innovation and Adoption in Clinical Care
a. Workflow Fragmentation and Legacy Infrastructure
Clinical decision-making remains embedded in siloed EHR systems, PDF documents, manual review queues, and
disconnected policy repositories. AI cannot scale without structured, interoperable inputs.
b. Regulatory Ambiguity for Non-Device AI
For administrative-clinical AI (e.g., medical necessity review, coverage validation), governance expectations are unclear.
Organizations are uncertain about liability, documentation, and audit standards.
c. Misaligned Incentives in Fee-for-Service Models
AI that reduces unnecessary utilization or administrative waste may not align with legacy reimbursement incentives.
d. Trust and Transparency Gaps
Health plans and providers require explainability, audit trails, and clinician override pathways. AI tools lacking structured
transparency slow adoption.
Upshot: In our experience, we have seen the biggest barriers to the adoption of AI are overconfidence and a lack of
transparency in AI implementation. We have addressed this challenge here and also discussed a resolution framework.
2. Regulatory and Payment Policy Changes HHS Should Prioritize From Our Wider Payer
Learning/Perspective
Regulatory
a. Establish Governance Framework for Non-Device Clinical AI
Clarify expectations for AI used in clinical-adjacent decision support (e.g., prior authorization, utilization review) distinct from
FDA-regulated devices.
b. Standardized Documentation and Audit Requirements
Provide guidance under HIPAA (45 CFR Parts 160 & 164) and related CMS program integrity frameworks to define acceptable
AI audit logs, traceability standards, and human-in-the-loop requirements.
c. AI Transparency Safe Harbor
Create regulatory safe harbors for AI systems that meet defined transparency, documentation, and clinician oversight
thresholds.
Payment Policy
a. Incentivize Administrative Efficiency in Value-Based Models
Align CMS payment programs so that AI-driven reductions in administrative burden and inappropriate utilization are financially
rewarded.

b. Recognize AI-Enabled Care Pathway Optimization
Allow AI-supported clinical triage and alternative care pathway recommendations to be integrated into reimbursement
modernization efforts.
3. Novel Legal and Implementation Issues (Non-Medical Devices)
a. Liability Allocation
Clear delineation is needed between AI tool vendors, health plans, and clinical users when AI informs but does not
autonomously decide.
b. Auditability and Appeals Governance
Administrative AI must produce defensible rationales for coverage decisions, especially in Medicare and Medicaid programs.
c. Data Provenance and Model Drift
Standards are needed for documenting training data categories, monitoring model drift, and ensuring policy alignment.
Recommended HHS Role:
HHS should publish model governance guidance for non-device clinical AI, including recommended oversight committees,
documentation standards, and periodic validation expectations.

5. Supporting Private Sector Accreditation and Certification
HHS can accelerate adoption by:

● Encouraging industry-driven AI accreditation frameworks aligned with CMS program integrity standards
● Supporting independent third-party validation programs
● Developing standardized reporting templates for AI audit logs and documentation
Clear accreditation pathways would reduce procurement uncertainty for health systems and payers.
6. Where AI Has Met Expectations and Where It Has Fallen Short
Met or Exceeded Expectations
● Administrative burden reduction in utilization management
● Faster turnaround times in structured medical review
● Improved consistency in policy application
In CMS-aligned review environments, AI-supported workflows have demonstrated measurable reductions in manual review time
while maintaining clinical oversight.
Fallen Short
● Overreliance on unstructured LLM outputs without guardrails
● Insufficient integration into legacy workflows
● Lack of interoperability with policy databases and EHR systems
High-Potential AI Areas
● Policy-to-chart matching
● Longitudinal care gap detection
● Early risk stratification in chronic disease management
● Automated fraud, waste, and abuse pattern identification
Upshot and Learnings: While there are gaps with commercial insurers, there are models of AI adoption that we can
learn from - basys.ai can help fill that gap.

Desired Improvements

● Reduced paperwork

Common Concerns

● Bias in AI systems

10. AI Research Priorities for HHS
Priority Areas
● AI for administrative burden reduction
● Bias detection and mitigation frameworks
● Explainability methods tailored to clinical-administrative decisions
● Longitudinal population health modeling
a. Published Findings
Emerging literature suggests AI improves documentation efficiency and triage accuracy when embedded within structured
governance frameworks. Administrative AI is less studied and represents an important research opportunity.
b. Costs, Benefits, and Transfers
Literature often focuses on clinical accuracy but underestimates:
● Administrative cost savings
● Reduced clinician burnout
● Improved patient throughput
● System-wide cost avoidance
HHS can catalyze research measuring system-level efficiency gains, not just predictive performance.
Upshot: We have delineated our learnings and a framework here.
