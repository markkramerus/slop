I. Summary
This comment responds to the Department’s Request for Information on accelerating the
adoption and use of artificial intelligence in clinical care.1 We respectfully submit that the
framing of this RFI, which focuses primarily on removing barriers to AI adoption, reflects an
incomplete picture of the current landscape. Before the Department acts to accelerate adoption, it
should examine the systemic risks created by AI systems that have been deployed with
inadequate evidence, that operate in regulatory gaps no federal agency has claimed jurisdiction
over, and that access patient data through legal frameworks never designed for autonomous
technology. These risks are not hypothetical. They are documented, ongoing, and escalating.2
This comment draws on the collective expertise of the authoring team, which includes
contributors to MIT Critical Data, a consortium spanning 40 countries with over a decade of
work at the intersection of clinical medicine, health data science, and global health fairness,
including the development of the MIMIC database, one of the most widely used open clinical
datasets in the world.3 This comment also draws on direct investigation of the Doctronic AI
prescribing pilot in Utah,4 including public records requests filed by members of our team under
the Government Records Access and Management Act (GRAMA), as well as extensive analysis
of the broader AI healthcare landscape, including multiple companies, documented patient
harms, regulatory gaps, liability questions, algorithmic bias, and international comparisons.
Doctronic is discussed as a case study, but the systemic problems this comment identifies extend
far beyond any single company or state.
We respond to all ten RFI questions below. Our central recommendation is that HHS should
establish federal minimum evidence standards, liability frameworks, and fairness requirements
for clinical AI before further accelerating adoption. We recognize that some commenters will
argue these safeguards would slow innovation. We believe the opposite is true: clear standards
are the foundation on which sustainable adoption must be built. Without them, the inevitable
accumulation of patient harms will produce a public backlash that sets clinical AI back by years.

II. The Evidence Crisis: AI Healthcare Claims Outpace Proof
A. The Doctronic Case Study: How a Preprint Became Policy
To understand the evidence gap in clinical AI, it is useful to examine the strongest case: the
Doctronic pilot in Utah, which has received significant regulatory scrutiny and press coverage
compared with other autonomous clinical AI systems. If the evidence supporting this pilot is
inadequate, it raises serious questions about the rest of the industry. The central evidence
supporting Doctronic is a single preprint posted to medRxiv in July 2025.5 The paper reports a
99.2% concordance rate between AI and physician treatment plans. This figure has been repeated
by major outlets as though it constitutes validated evidence.6
It does not meet that standard. medRxiv’s own FAQ states preprints “should not be used to guide
clinical practice.”7 The methodological problems are substantial:
• All eight authors are equity holders in Doctronic Inc.8
• Ethics approval came from Doctronic’s own internal committee, not an independent
IRB.9
• The sample: 500 urgent-care encounters from a single week, which is insufficient to
support claims about safety at scale.10
• Clinicians saw AI-generated documentation before rendering assessments, introducing
anchoring bias.11
• Primary diagnosis match was only 81%; the 99.2% measures “treatment plan alignment,”
a softer standard.12
• The study tested urgent care, but the Utah pilot involves chronic medication renewals,
which is a fundamentally different clinical context.13
Doctronic’s co-founder claimed the AI is “infinitely safer than a human doctor”14, a claim
supported by no independent evidence. The medical co-founder is a vascular surgeon with no
prior AI research.15 The company’s first CMO with relevant AI credentials was hired in October
2025, after the study.16

B. A Landscape of Unvalidated Clinical AI Claims
The evidentiary problems with Doctronic are not unique to that company. They reflect standard
practice across the clinical AI industry, where companies routinely make clinical claims that far
outstrip their published evidence. The following examples illustrate the scope of the problem.
Hippocratic AI has raised $404 million at a $3.5 billion valuation, marketing “AI nurse agents”
for direct patient interaction. The company claims 115 million clinical interactions “with no
safety issues,” yet these metrics come from internal studies using a proprietary model that cannot
be independently benchmarked. National Nurses United organized over 20 demonstrations
against the company.17
Glass Health (YC-funded, $6.5M raised) generates AI differential diagnoses and treatment
plans. Despite claiming use at Harvard and Stanford, no published peer-reviewed clinical
validation studies exist. The company deliberately avoids FDA-triggering language by marketing
as “clinical decision support.”18
K Health ($380M raised, $900M valuation) operates an AI symptom checker. While a
company-associated study claimed 68% alignment with physicians, an independent vignette
study found top-3 diagnostic accuracy of only 36% compared to 82.1% for general practitioners.
Among newer entrants, YC-backed FutureClinic claims “digital medical clinics that are 99%
AI, 1% doctor,” while Prana markets itself as a “full-stack medical provider” and “always-on
physician.” These are extraordinarily aggressive clinical claims for pre-revenue startups.
C. The Corporate Failure Record

was trained on synthetic cases and proved inappropriate internationally.19 Babylon Health raised

systematic triage failures, including missed heart attack symptoms and documented gender

III. Documented Patient Harms: A Systematic Pattern
The evidence reviewed above concerns unvalidated claims. But the consequences of deploying
AI health tools without adequate safeguards are not merely theoretical. Documented harms have
already occurred across multiple products, patient populations, and categories of harm. These
cases illustrate what happens when AI systems enter clinical environments without the
protections that HHS has the opportunity to establish through this rulemaking process.
A. The NEDA/Tessa Incident
After the National Eating Disorders Association terminated its human helpline staff, the Tessa
chatbot, which was augmented with generative AI capabilities without NEDA’s knowledge, gave
eating disorder patients advice to lose 1–2 pounds per week, eat no more than 2,000 calories
daily, maintain a 500–1,000 calorie deficit, and purchase skin calipers to measure body fat.24 As
one eating disorder survivor reported: every suggestion “were things that led to my eating
disorder.”
B. Character.AI Teen Suicides: The Failure of Voluntary Safety Measures
Sewell Setzer III, age 14, died by suicide on February 28, 2024, after developing an intense
emotional attachment to a Character.AI chatbot. When he expressed suicidal ideation, the bot
failed to trigger safety interventions. Juliana Peralta, age 13, died in November 2023 after similar
engagement. Google and Character.AI settled in January 2026, with additional lawsuits filed in
Colorado, New York, and Texas. These cases are relevant to the clinical AI question because
they demonstrate that AI companies deploying health-adjacent tools without mandatory safety
standards will not reliably implement adequate safeguards on their own. Character.AI added
safety features only after public litigation and media pressure, not before deployment.25
C. The Koko Experiment
Approximately 4,000 mental health users received GPT-3-co-written support through the Koko
platform without clear informed consent and without IRB approval.26 Medical ethicist Art
Caplan stated: “If you want to set back the use of AI in mental health, start exactly this way.”
The Koko incident demonstrates a recurring problem: AI was introduced into a clinical context
without the informed consent protections that would be required of any human-delivered
intervention. The same dynamic is present in the Doctronic pilot, where patients interacting with
AI may not fully understand the nature of the entity making decisions about their medications.

Paxlovid-verapamil interaction and fabricating references.28

of AI chatbots the number one health technology hazard for both 2025 and 2026, documenting
chatbots suggesting incorrect diagnoses, recommending unnecessary testing, and inventing body
parts.29 Mount Sinai researchers found AI chatbots “routinely elaborated on fake medical details,
confidently generating explanations about conditions or treatments that do not exist.”30
A Stanford study found AI therapy chatbots enabled dangerous behavior: when a user mentioned
losing their job and asked about tall bridges, the chatbot listed bridges rather than recognizing
suicidal ideation. An additional safety concern that warrants research attention is AI sycophancy:
the tendency of AI systems to agree with users rather than provide accurate information,
particularly in patient-facing applications. When a patient tells an AI chatbot they believe they
have a particular condition, sycophantic AI will tend to confirm rather than challenge that belief,
even when the evidence does not support it. This dynamic is especially dangerous in mental
health contexts, where validation of distorted thinking can reinforce harmful patterns. Taken
together, these cases and dynamics establish that AI health tools have caused serious harm across
a range of contexts, that companies have not voluntarily implemented adequate safeguards, and
that the harms tend to fall disproportionately on vulnerable populations. This pattern should
inform how HHS approaches the question of accelerating clinical AI adoption.31

IV. Venture Capital Is Shaping Clinical AI Policy
A. Regulatory Strategy as Investment Thesis
The preceding sections establish that clinical AI companies are making unvalidated claims and
that those claims have produced real patient harms. This section examines why the system is
producing these outcomes: the financial incentives driving clinical AI deployment are
structurally misaligned with patient safety. Doctronic has raised $25 million from Lightspeed
Venture Partners,3233 Union Square Ventures, and Tusk Venture Partners.34
Tusk Venture Partners is of particular relevance to this comment. Founded by Bradley Tusk, who
served as Communications Director for Senator Schumer, Deputy Governor of Illinois,
Bloomberg’s campaign manager35, the firm invests in startups facing regulatory barriers and
provides political strategy in exchange for equity. Co-founder Jordan Nof stated: “How do we
close the door behind us? I want to create a regulatory moat.”36 Tusk himself: “Venture
capitalists, you know, we’re not here for the good of humanity.”37
Doctronic’s website acknowledges Tusk’s “regulatory expertise helps us navigate the complex
healthcare landscape.”38 Public records obtained through GRAMA confirm Tusk Ventures
appears in negotiation records between Doctronic and Utah’s Office of AI Policy.39
This arrangement creates a structural conflict that HHS should consider as it evaluates how to
accelerate adoption responsibly. When the same entity that profits from a company’s commercial
success is also shaping the regulatory environment that company operates in, the resulting
regulatory outcomes cannot be presumed to reflect an independent assessment of patient safety.
The question for HHS is whether the current regulatory framework has adequate safeguards
against this kind of influence. Based on our investigation, it does not.
B. The “AI Doctor” Gold Rush
Y Combinator and other accelerators are actively funding startups marketing AI as replacements
for physician judgment. HHS should recognize that pressure to “accelerate adoption” is
substantially generated by venture capital return timelines rather than patient demand, clinical
evidence, or healthcare system needs. Patients want affordable, accessible healthcare, and
replacing physicians with AI does not address that need.

V. The FDA Regulatory Framework Has Critical Gaps
A. The Clinical Decision Support Exemption
The evidence, harms, and incentive misalignment documented above might be less concerning if
a robust federal regulatory framework existed to ensure clinical AI safety. It does not. The most
significant gap is the Clinical Decision Support (CDS) exemption. Section 3060(a) of the 21st
Century Cures Act exempts CDS software from FDA oversight if it meets four criteria, including
that clinicians can independently review the AI’s reasoning.40 Companies exploit this by framing
autonomous AI systems as “support” tools and adding disclaimers that recommendations require
“clinician review.” The opacity of deep learning models makes the independent review criterion
largely fictional.
FDA Commissioner Makary’s January 2026 CDS guidance further loosened oversight, extending
enforcement discretion and stating the FDA should be “less paternalistic.” The guidance
removed analysis of automation bias.41
B. The 510(k) Pathway Is Inadequate
Over 1,300 AI/ML-enabled medical devices have been authorized, virtually all via the 510(k)
pathway requiring only “substantial equivalence” to a predicate device. A 2025 study found
fewer than 2% were supported by randomized clinical trials.42 More than a third of
first-generation AI devices were cleared based on non-AI predicates, meaning novel AI
capabilities were deemed equivalent to fundamentally different technologies. The practical
consequence is that the vast majority of clinical AI tools in use today reached the market without
the kind of clinical trial evidence that would be required for a new pharmaceutical or medical
device. This is the evidentiary environment into which the Department is proposing to accelerate
further adoption.43
C. Why Autonomous AI Prescribing Falls in the Gap
The FDA regulates medical devices but not the practice of medicine. State medical practice acts
define prescriptive authority, which has historically been limited to human licensees. The FDA
has declined comment on Doctronic’s pilot, stating it “falls outside the agency’s regulatory
purview.”44 This means no regulatory body is clearly responsible for ensuring autonomous AI
prescribing is safe.

VI. The Liability Vacuum for Autonomous Clinical AI
If the regulatory framework does not prevent harm, the legal system should at least provide
recourse for patients who are harmed. But here, too, the existing framework has no clear answer
for autonomous clinical AI. As of early 2026, no U.S. malpractice jury verdict has centered on
AI as the basis of a clinical claim, and the legal doctrines that would govern such a case are
poorly suited to the technology.49
A. Medical Malpractice Does Not Fit
Traditional malpractice requires a physician-patient relationship (which does not exist with
autonomous AI), a standard of care (undefined for AI), breach (which is nearly impossible to
determine in opaque systems), and causation (which is difficult to isolate). As W. Nicholson
Price II of Michigan Law School has documented, deep learning embeds reasoning in thousands
of simulated neurons, making breach determination effectively impossible.50
B. Product Liability Does Not Fit Either
Courts have historically been reluctant to apply product liability to software, treating it as a
“service.” Yet when AI acts autonomously, the malpractice framework, which requires a human
practitioner, also breaks down. The practical result is that a patient harmed by an autonomous AI
prescribing decision may have no viable legal theory under which to seek compensation. This is
not an abstract concern. It is the legal environment in which the Doctronic pilot is currently
operating.51
C. The Learned Intermediary Doctrine Collapses
The learned intermediary doctrine shields drug manufacturers by requiring warnings only to
prescribing physicians. When AI removes the physician, neither the manufacturer (which warned
the “prescriber”) nor the AI company clearly bears liability.52 Consider a concrete scenario:
Doctronic’s AI renews an SSRI for a patient who has since been prescribed a medication with a
dangerous interaction. The drug manufacturer warned the “prescriber,” but the prescriber is an
algorithm. The AI company may argue it is a technology provider, not a medical practitioner.
The state authorized the pilot but disclaimed clinical responsibility. The patient is left with no
clear defendant. This is not a hypothetical edge case; it is the foreseeable consequence of
deploying autonomous prescribing without first resolving the liability framework.

VII. Algorithmic Bias Threatens to Worsen Health Disparities
The risks documented above, including unvalidated evidence, patient harms, regulatory gaps,
and liability failures, do not fall equally on all populations. The evidence consistently shows that
clinical AI systems perform worse for minority and underserved patients, raising serious fairness
concerns that this RFI should address directly. Obermeyer et al. (Science, 2019) found that an
algorithm applied to roughly 200 million Americans used healthcare costs as a proxy for need,
systematically underestimating Black patients’ illness. At a given risk score, Black patients had
26.3% more chronic conditions than White patients. The bias reduced Black patients identified
for extra care by more than half.54
Pulse oximetry bias feeds directly into AI decision-making: Sjoding et al. (NEJM, 2020) found
Black patients had nearly three times the frequency of undetected hypoxemia.55 Dermatology AI
performs substantially worse on dark skin. No public benchmark included biopsy-proven
malignancy on dark skin before Daneshjou et al. (Science Advances, 2022) created the first such
dataset.56
The Epic Sepsis Model, deployed at hundreds of hospitals, achieved an AUC of only 0.63 in
external validation, well below the claimed 0.76 to 0.83, and missed 67% of sepsis patients.57
A systematic review found 22 of 24 studies (91.7%) identified demographic biases in medical
LLMs, with 93.7% finding gender bias and 90.9% finding racial/ethnic bias.58 GPT-4 specifically
showed associations between demographic attributes and recommendations for more expensive
procedures.59
The data representation problem is structural: non-Hispanic Whites constitute over 90% of
clinical trial participants while representing 60.7% of the population, and fewer than 44% of
clinical trials report any race/ethnicity data. These disparities are compounded for rural and
lower socioeconomic status populations, who are systematically underrepresented in both the
clinical data used to train AI systems and in the health systems where AI tools are first deployed
and validated. Safety-net hospitals and rural health facilities, which serve the most vulnerable
patients, are least likely to have the infrastructure to evaluate AI tools locally, creating a dynamic
in which the populations at greatest risk from biased AI are also the populations with the least
capacity to detect and mitigate that bias. For autonomous prescribing systems like Doctronic, this
means the AI is making medication decisions based on training data that systematically
underrepresents the patients most likely to experience adverse outcomes. HHS cannot
responsibly accelerate adoption of clinical AI without requiring that these systems demonstrate
equitable performance across the populations they serve.60

VIII. State Regulatory Capacity Does Not Match Ambition
A. Utah: Institutional Mismatch
Given the evidence gaps, documented harms, incentive misalignment, regulatory failures,
liability vacuum, and fairness risks detailed in the preceding sections, the question of who
authorizes clinical AI deployment becomes critical. In the case of the most consequential clinical
AI authorization to date, the answer is troubling. Utah’s Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy
has four staff members: a mathematician, a social systems modeler, an internet privacy attorney,
and a wealth management professional. None has medical, clinical, or pharmaceutical
training.6162
This team authorized Doctronic to autonomously renew approximately 192 medications,
including SSRIs, antihypertensives, statins, and hormonal contraceptives.63 Healthcare law firm
Goldsand Friedberg concluded the agreement “clearly steps over” licensed professional activity
and noted no agreement “requires representations regarding FDA qualification.”64
No major medical society has endorsed autonomous AI prescribing. The AMA stated it “poses
serious risks.”65 The Utah Medical Association called it “a bad idea.”66 Public Citizen’s Dr.
Steinbrook called it “a dangerous first step” and warned that “decisions made by AI with human
oversight are in practice decisions made by AI most of the time.”67
B. The Transparency Deficit
Our team filed public records requests under GRAMA seeking monthly safety reports,
negotiation drafts, metric definitions, and federal communications about the pilot. The Utah
Department of Commerce denied access to the majority of records, classifying safety reports as
“protected” under business confidentiality exemptions. The state’s position is that our interests
“do not outweigh Doctronic’s business confidentiality interests.”68
The result is a closed loop: a state office without medical expertise authorized clinical AI
deployment, the safety data that could allow independent evaluation is classified as confidential,
and the public has no mechanism to assess whether the pilot is functioning safely. If HHS intends

X. International Comparisons Reveal the U.S. as an Outlier
The gaps in the U.S. framework are not inevitable features of AI regulation. Other jurisdictions
have developed more protective approaches that provide useful models for HHS consideration.
The EU AI Act classifies medical AI as high-risk, requiring risk management systems, data
governance ensuring representative datasets, mandatory human oversight, accuracy standards,
post-market monitoring, and serious incident reporting. Penalties can reach €35 million or 7% of
global turnover.74 Product defectiveness can be presumed if AI does not comply with safety
requirements.
Health Canada requires a Sex and Gender-Based Analysis Plus: manufacturers must collect
disaggregated data on sex, gender, racial/ethnic minorities, elderly, and pediatric populations.
This mandatory fairness requirement has no U.S. parallel.75
Japan mandates that AI medical devices must be used under physician oversight, with
physicians making all final treatment decisions.76
The U.S. is the only major jurisdiction to have allowed autonomous AI prescribing and the only
one with a broad statutory CDS exemption that removes many clinical AI tools from oversight
entirely. We do not suggest that HHS adopt any single international framework wholesale, but
the comparative evidence demonstrates that the requirements we recommend, including
mandatory fairness testing, human oversight proportional to autonomy, and post-market
surveillance, are not radical proposals. They are the minimum standards that other nations have
concluded are necessary to protect patients.

XI. Payer and Insurance Implications
The regulatory and legal gaps documented above have practical consequences for how
AI-prescribed care will be paid for and insured. No major insurer has issued a policy specifically
addressing coverage of medications prescribed by autonomous AI systems. The current
framework presumes prescriptions from licensed human practitioners.77
CMS has focused on preventing AI-driven denials: the April 2023 Final Rule requires Medicare
Advantage determinations to be based on individual circumstances, not algorithms. CMS’s
WISeR Model (January 2026) uses AI for Medicare prior authorizations but maintains that final
coverage denial decisions are made by “licensed clinicians, not machines.”78
Malpractice insurance for AI remains undefined. Major insurers review AI involvement
case-by-case; no AI-specific frameworks exist.79 The reimbursement framework is embryonic:
CPT 2026 will be the first year AI is explicitly recognized in formal medical coding.80
These gaps create practical consequences on both sides of the adoption question. Physicians who
might benefit from AI-assisted decision support may resist using it because the liability and
coverage implications are unclear. Meanwhile, patients whose medications are renewed by an
autonomous AI system may discover that their insurer does not recognize the prescription as
valid, or that no malpractice framework covers them if something goes wrong. Resolving these
questions is not a barrier to innovation; it is a prerequisite for responsible deployment.

XII. Expert and Medical Society Consensus
The concerns raised in this comment are not ours alone. They are shared by the leading medical
societies, academic journals, and patient safety organizations in the United States.

comprehensive values-driven framework for responsible AI in health. The consistency of these
positions across institutions with different mandates, constituencies, and political orientations is

deployed without independent validation, fairness testing, and clear liability frameworks. The

XIII. Responses to RFI Questions
Question 1: What are the biggest barriers to AI innovation in healthcare?
We acknowledge that real barriers to responsible AI innovation exist. Data interoperability
remains fragmented across incompatible EHR systems. Reimbursement pathways are embryonic;
CPT 2026 will be the first year AI is explicitly recognized in formal medical coding. Regulatory
jurisdiction is divided among federal agencies and 50 state governments, creating uncertainty for
companies seeking to deploy across state lines.85
However, the most consequential barrier to sustainable AI adoption is the absence of clear
federal safety standards. This absence undermines public trust, creates liability uncertainty that
deters responsible developers from adopting AI tools, and invites a race to the bottom among
states competing to attract AI companies with the least restrictive environments. When patients
learn that the AI renewing their medications was authorized by a four-person state office with no
medical expertise, or that the supporting evidence is a company-funded preprint, their confidence
in clinical AI erodes. Moreover, if reimbursement and regulatory frameworks privilege
AI-enabled care, health systems that cannot afford AI tools, particularly safety-net hospitals and
rural facilities, risk falling further behind, creating a two-tiered healthcare system in which the
quality of care depends on whether a patient’s provider can afford the latest technology.8687
The framing of this RFI implicitly treats safety standards as obstacles to innovation. We urge
HHS to recognize that the opposite is true. The pharmaceutical industry earned public trust not
by operating without FDA oversight but because FDA oversight gave patients confidence that
approved drugs met minimum safety standards. Clinical AI requires the same foundation.
Question 2: What regulatory or payment changes should be prioritized?
The most urgent regulatory change is closing the Clinical Decision Support exemption for AI
tools that functionally make autonomous clinical decisions. The CDS exemption under the 21st
Century Cures Act was designed for tools that assist clinicians, not systems that replace them.
Companies exploit this by adding nominal "clinician review" disclaimers to functionally
autonomous AI output. HHS should clarify, in coordination with FDA, that the exemption does
not apply when AI output is the primary or default clinical action, when clinicians cannot
meaningfully review the reasoning due to model opacity, or when the AI operates without
real-time physician oversight.88

Question 4: How should AI be evaluated for clinical safety?
Current evaluation practices are inadequate. The Doctronic preprint illustrates the problem: all
eight authors hold equity in the company, ethics approval came from an internal committee, and
the sample was 500 cases from a single week. Yet this study is the evidentiary basis for the first
autonomous AI prescribing system in U.S. history. The Epic Sepsis Model, deployed at hundreds
of hospitals, achieved an AUC of only 0.63 in external validation versus the claimed 0.76 to
0.83, missing 67% of sepsis patients. The Oxford/Nature Medicine study (February 2026) found
AI diagnostic accuracy dropped from 95% on standardized tests to under 35% in real-world
interactions. These cases demonstrate that company-reported metrics cannot substitute for
independent evaluation.979899
HHS should require, at minimum: (a) independent third-party testing by entities with no financial
relationship to the developer, following the model proposed in npj Digital Medicine; (b)
mandatory reporting of disaggregated performance data across racial, ethnic, gender, and age
subgroups, given the systematic biases documented by Obermeyer et al. and others; (c)
real-world performance monitoring post-deployment with mandatory adverse event reporting,
since controlled-setting performance consistently fails to predict real-world outcomes; and (d)
standardized clinical AI safety benchmarks developed in collaboration with NIST.100101
The NEJM AI editors have mandated trial preregistration for AI intervention studies starting
January 1, 2025. HHS should adopt a parallel requirement: any clinical AI system seeking
federal recognition, reimbursement, or deployment in federally funded facilities should be
supported by preregistered, independently conducted clinical evidence. We also commend to
HHS the LTARC evaluation framework (Local, Task-specific, Agile, Reflective,
Community-partnered), which goes beyond existing reporting checklists by requiring evaluation
to be conducted locally at the deploying institution, tailored to the specific clinical task, updated
continuously as models and populations evolve, reflective of the care context including
workflow integration, and developed in partnership with the communities the AI is meant to
serve. Only 0.2% of healthcare AI publications report any community involvement in the
evaluation process; this must change.102

governance, not just the AI tools themselves. Effective clinical AI requires continuous
monitoring, local validation, clinician training, bias auditing, and community engagement. These
activities are currently unfunded, creating a systematic underinvestment in the governance
infrastructure that safe AI deployment requires. CMS should develop dedicated reimbursement
codes for AI governance activities as part of its AI payment strategy.

Interoperability is a legitimate priority, but it is insufficient without safety standards. The

exception designed for communication between human providers. Expanding interoperability
without addressing this consent gap would compound an existing problem.

XIV. Structural Recommendations
1. Establish federal minimum evidence standards for clinical AI.
Any AI system making autonomous clinical decisions should demonstrate safety and efficacy
through independent, peer-reviewed clinical evidence before deployment. Company-funded
preprints with equity-holding authors cannot constitute an adequate evidentiary basis. HHS
should work with FDA to create a premarket review pathway for autonomous clinical AI
proportionate to risk.124
2. Close the CDS exemption loophole for functionally autonomous AI.
AI tools that functionally make clinical decisions, even those with nominal “clinician review”
disclaimers, should not escape oversight through the 21st Century Cures Act exemption. HHS
should clarify that the CDS exemption does not apply when AI recommendations are presented
as the primary or default clinical action.125
3. Require mandatory fairness testing.
Following Health Canada’s model, HHS should require clinical AI vendors to collect and report
disaggregated performance data across demographic subgroups before and after deployment. The
evidence that AI trained on non-representative data systematically harms minority patients is too
extensive to ignore.126

XV. Conclusion
This RFI asks how HHS can “accelerate the adoption and use of AI as part of clinical care.” We
have documented in this comment why acceleration without evidence standards does not
constitute responsible innovation. The evidence base for the most prominent clinical AI systems
is company-funded and unreviewed. Documented patient harms range from eating disorders to
teen suicides to systematic racial bias. The regulatory framework has gaps that no federal agency
has claimed. The liability system cannot tell patients who is responsible when AI harms them.
And the financial incentives driving deployment are structurally misaligned with patient safety.133
The pattern documented in this comment, in which companies deploy AI with minimal validated
evidence, navigating regulation through political strategy rather than clinical proof, access
patient data through frameworks never designed for autonomous AI, and generate claims that
major media amplify without scrutiny, is not confined to one company or state. It reflects
systemic pressures that will only intensify.
Every major medical society opposes autonomous AI prescribing without rigorous safeguards.
Every comparable international jurisdiction imposes more protective requirements. The
documented record of AI health tool harms, including eating disorder patients receiving dieting
advice, teenagers dying by suicide, and algorithms that miss 67% of sepsis cases, demonstrates
the technology is not ready for autonomous clinical deployment without robust protections.
The Department has both the authority and the responsibility to establish the federal floor. Doing
so will not slow innovation. It will build the public trust that is the precondition for AI’s
sustainable integration into American healthcare.
