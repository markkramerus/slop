1.0 Executive Summary
Artificial intelligence holds significant promise for transforming clinical care delivery, improving
outcomes, reducing administrative burden, and advancing health equity across the United States.
Yet despite the rapid proliferation of AI tools in healthcare settings, adoption remains constrained
— not by the absence of capable algorithms, but by the absence of the governance, lifecycle
management, and integration infrastructure required to deploy those tools safely, sustainably, and
accountably.
This response is submitted by Parish Lemons, President of Total Quality Solutions, LLC, a
Missouri-based IT consulting firm with over 20 years of experience managing enterprise software
development lifecycles in regulated public-sector environments. The perspectives offered here
are grounded in hands-on practitioner experience implementing and governing software systems
through the full development lifecycle — including requirements analysis, design, development,
testing, deployment, and post-deployment performance management — applied specifically to
government and healthcare program contexts.
This submission asserts that the primary barriers to clinical AI adoption are structural and lifecycle-
based in nature. Organizations possess increasingly capable AI tools but lack the governance
frameworks, evaluation infrastructure, and interoperability standards required to deploy them
responsibly at scale. This challenge is especially acute at the state Medicaid program level, where
agencies such as Missouri's MO HealthNet division are simultaneously managing legacy system
modernization, new federal compliance requirements, and the pressure to integrate AI-enabled
solutions — often without the SDLC governance discipline needed to do so safely.
Our recommendations emphasize: (1) establishing SDLC-based governance standards for non-
medical device AI; (2) investing in post-deployment monitoring infrastructure; (3) creating
reimbursement pathways that incentivize governed AI deployment; and (4) funding cooperative
agreements with state Medicaid agencies and mid-market IT consultants to develop and pilot
implementation frameworks that the broader health sector can adopt.

3.0 Barriers to AI Adoption in Clinical Care
Despite meaningful advances in AI capability, adoption within clinical care environments remains
constrained by a set of interrelated, structural barriers. These challenges are not primarily
technical in origin — they reflect gaps in governance, lifecycle management, interoperability, and
organizational accountability that cut across institutions, vendors, and care settings. From a
practitioner SDLC perspective, each barrier maps directly to a phase of the software development
and deployment lifecycle that has not been adequately addressed in current AI adoption
frameworks.
3.1 Absence of SDLC Governance Frameworks for AI Systems
The most fundamental barrier to responsible AI adoption in clinical care is the absence of
established software development lifecycle governance frameworks applied specifically to AI tool
deployment and maintenance. In enterprise IT environments, mature SDLC frameworks —
including CMMI, ISO/IEC 12207, and Agile-based governance models — provide the structural
discipline required to manage software through design, testing, deployment, change
management, and retirement. These frameworks define roles, oversight mechanisms,

documentation standards, and escalation procedures that enable organizations to deploy
complex software responsibly in regulated environments.
AI tools deployed in clinical settings are software systems. Yet they are routinely introduced
outside of SDLC governance structures — evaluated under controlled conditions, deployed
without change management protocols, and maintained without version control or performance
monitoring plans. The result is a pattern in which clinically promising AI tools are deployed at
scale without the institutional discipline required to manage their behavior over time. This gap is
not unique to healthcare; it reflects the broader challenge of AI governance in regulated
environments, and it is one that HHS is uniquely positioned to address through policy guidance.
3.2 Lack of Post-Deployment Lifecycle Monitoring
Enterprise software development practice has long recognized that the most consequential phase
of a system's lifecycle occurs after deployment, when the system encounters the full complexity
of real-world operating environments. Post-deployment monitoring, performance trending, drift
detection, and structured change management are foundational disciplines in mature software
development organizations — and they are largely absent from current clinical AI deployment
practice.
AI systems are particularly sensitive to environmental change. Shifts in clinical documentation
practices, patient population demographics, care delivery models, or upstream data pipeline
configurations can materially alter AI performance over time. Without continuous monitoring and
structured performance review cycles, degradation in AI output quality may go undetected until it
has already influenced clinical decision-making. This is precisely the category of risk that SDLC
governance frameworks are designed to mitigate — and it is a gap that HHS policy can directly
address by establishing post-deployment monitoring requirements as a condition of AI tool
adoption in federally funded care settings.
3.3 Unclear Accountability Across Multi-Stakeholder Environments
Clinical AI deployments involve a complex web of stakeholders — model developers, data
providers, system integrators, health system administrators, clinical end-users, and regulatory
bodies — among whom accountability for performance, safety, and remediation is frequently
undefined. From a program management perspective, this ambiguity is among the most
consequential barriers to responsible adoption. Organizations operating in regulated
environments with established risk management obligations are rationally hesitant to deploy AI
tools when the governance structure does not clearly define where accountability resides when
tools underperform or produce unintended clinical consequences.

This is a governance design problem, not a technology problem. SDLC governance frameworks
in enterprise IT environments address this challenge through defined RACI matrices, documented
escalation procedures, and contractual performance obligations. HHS can support the extension
of these disciplines into clinical AI deployment by issuing guidance that defines accountability
expectations for AI tool developers, system integrators, and deploying institutions — particularly
in Medicaid and Medicare program contexts where federal funding creates a direct policy lever.
3.4 Workflow Integration and Clinician Trust
AI tools that are not designed for seamless integration into existing clinical workflows impose
cognitive and operational burden on clinicians — even when the tools themselves demonstrate
strong technical performance under controlled conditions. From an SDLC perspective, this
reflects a failure of requirements analysis and user-centered design discipline. Effective enterprise
software development begins with deep stakeholder engagement, workflow mapping, and
iterative validation with end-users before deployment. These disciplines are inconsistently applied
in clinical AI development, resulting in tools that clinicians resist or underutilize.
This challenge is compounded when AI outputs exist outside of the EHR environment, requiring
clinicians to leave established workflows to access or interpret results. Integration discipline —
ensuring that AI outputs are surfaced within existing clinical workflows and documented within the
patient record in a manner consistent with clinical governance requirements — is a solvable
engineering and design challenge when treated as a first-class requirement of the SDLC process.
3.5 Data Integrity and Interoperability Gaps
Clinical AI is frequently developed and evaluated using retrospective EHR data that reflects local
documentation practices rather than standardized, longitudinal clinical context. Data
completeness, consistency, and provenance — disciplines that are foundational to enterprise data
governance frameworks — are inconsistently applied in clinical AI development environments.
The result is AI tools whose performance is difficult to reproduce across institutions, populations,
or care settings.
Interoperability standards such as FHIR have significantly advanced data access and exchange;
however, interoperability for AI governance must extend beyond data exchange to encompass
metadata, data provenance, evaluation artifacts, and governance policy documentation. Without
these additional layers, organizations cannot assess AI performance with the rigor that regulated
care environments require. HHS investment in shared data governance infrastructure — including
provenance standards, evaluation metadata frameworks, and interoperability requirements
specific to AI system inputs and outputs — would materially reduce this barrier.

The barriers described above are particularly acute at the state Medicaid program level, where
agencies are simultaneously managing the demands of legacy system modernization, new
federal compliance requirements, and pressure to integrate AI-enabled solutions — often with
constrained resources and limited access to the specialized expertise required to govern AI
deployments responsibly.
Missouri's MO HealthNet division exemplifies this challenge. MO HealthNet is currently navigating
a multi-year Medicaid Management Information System (MMIS) modernization effort, new federal
work requirement verification obligations under the recently enacted Medicaid work requirements
framework, and a $216 million rural health transformation initiative funded by CMS. Each of these
programs involves significant IT modernization work — and each creates a potential entry point
for AI-enabled tools to improve program administration, eligibility verification, care coordination,
and reporting.
Yet Missouri's current technology infrastructure and governance capability are not yet positioned
to responsibly deploy AI tools at scale across these programs without the foundational
governance, interoperability, and monitoring infrastructure that HHS policy can help establish.
The MO HealthNet context is offered here not as an isolated case but as a representative example
of the implementation reality facing state Medicaid agencies nationwide — a constituency that
HHS policy should explicitly address.
4.0 The Need for Lifecycle-Based Evaluation and Continuous
Monitoring
Effective AI adoption in clinical care requires a fundamental shift in evaluation philosophy — from
episodic, pre-deployment validation to lifecycle-based assessment that persists through the full
operational life of the AI system. This is not a novel concept in software engineering; it is standard
practice in mature software development organizations and is directly applicable to clinical AI
governance.
4.1 Pre-Deployment Versus Post-Deployment Evaluation
Current practice in clinical AI evaluation is heavily weighted toward pre-deployment testing
conducted under controlled conditions. While necessary, pre-deployment evaluation provides
limited insight into how AI tools perform when integrated into the full complexity of real-world
clinical workflows, diverse patient populations, and variable data quality environments. SDLC
governance practice addresses this through structured user acceptance testing, pilot deployment
phases with defined success criteria, and post-deployment performance review cycles —
disciplines that should be incorporated into clinical AI governance requirements.

4.2 Continuous Performance Monitoring and Drift Detection
AI systems are sensitive to environmental change in ways that traditional software is not. Model
drift — the degradation of AI output quality as the operating environment evolves — is a well-
documented phenomenon that requires active monitoring to detect and manage. Continuous
performance monitoring, including automated drift detection, anomaly flagging, and periodic
revalidation cycles, should be established as a standard requirement for AI tools operating in
federally funded clinical environments.
HHS can support this requirement by establishing minimum monitoring standards as a condition
of Medicaid and Medicare program participation for AI tool vendors, and by investing in shared
monitoring infrastructure that state agencies and smaller health systems can leverage without
building duplicative internal capability.
4.3 Interoperability Beyond Data Exchange
Interoperability requirements for clinical AI must extend beyond the exchange of clinical data to
encompass the full information environment in which AI systems operate. This includes metadata
standards for AI input and output documentation, provenance requirements for training and
validation data, evaluation artifact standards that enable cross-institutional performance
comparison, and governance policy interoperability that allows oversight mechanisms to function
across organizational boundaries.
Without these additional interoperability layers, clinical AI deployments will remain fragmented,
difficult to evaluate at scale, and resistant to the kind of comparative effectiveness assessment
that informed policy and reimbursement decisions require.
5.0 Regulatory and Policy Considerations
The regulatory landscape for clinical AI is characterized by a mismatch between existing
frameworks — designed for discrete medical devices and static health IT systems — and the
operational reality of non-medical device AI that evolves continuously, operates across
organizational boundaries, and influences care indirectly through workflows and information
presentation. Addressing this mismatch does not require comprehensive new regulation; it
requires targeted policy guidance that brings AI governance into alignment with established
software engineering disciplines.
5.1 Adopt SDLC Maturity Frameworks as AI Governance Standards
HHS should consider formally adopting SDLC maturity frameworks — specifically the Capability
Maturity Model Integration (CMMI) or equivalent standards — as the governance baseline for

non-medical device AI operating in federally funded clinical environments. These frameworks are
well-established, widely understood by the IT vendor community, and directly applicable to the
lifecycle management challenges that currently constrain responsible AI adoption. Requiring AI
tool vendors to demonstrate SDLC maturity at a defined level as a condition of program
participation would provide a practical, verifiable governance standard that does not require
development of novel regulatory infrastructure.
5.2 Clarify Accountability and Governance Responsibilities
HHS should issue guidance that explicitly defines accountability expectations for AI tool
developers, system integrators, and deploying health institutions — including documentation
requirements, escalation procedures, and performance remediation obligations. This guidance
should be structured to apply to the full stakeholder ecosystem of a clinical AI deployment, not
only to the AI tool developer, and should be designed to integrate with existing compliance
frameworks including HIPAA, the CMS Conditions of Participation, and state Medicaid program
requirements.
5.3 Establish AI-Specific Requirements Within Medicaid IT Standards
CMS should establish AI-specific requirements within the existing Medicaid IT standards
framework — specifically the Medicaid Information Technology Architecture (MITA) framework
and the standards and conditions governing MMIS design, development, and implementation.
Integrating AI governance requirements into MITA would provide a direct mechanism for
extending SDLC-based governance standards to the state Medicaid program context, ensuring
that AI tools deployed within Medicaid infrastructure meet minimum governance, monitoring, and
accountability requirements as a condition of the enhanced federal matching funds available for
Medicaid IT investments.
6.0 Reimbursement Pathways for AI-Assisted Care
Reimbursement policy is among the most powerful instruments available to HHS for shaping AI
adoption behavior in clinical care environments. Current reimbursement frameworks create
limited incentive for health systems to invest in the governance infrastructure required to deploy
AI tools responsibly — because governance investment is not reimbursable, and because
reimbursement for AI-assisted care is inconsistent and poorly defined.
6.1 Create Pilot Reimbursement Pathways for Governed AI Deployments
CMS should establish pilot reimbursement pathways — through the Physician Fee Schedule,
Medicaid managed care authorities, or CMS Innovation Center models — specifically for AI-
assisted care coordination and clinical decision support tools that meet defined governance

standards. Tying reimbursement eligibility to governance requirements creates a direct financial
incentive for health systems to invest in SDLC-based AI governance — and provides CMS with a
practical mechanism for evaluating the effectiveness of those governance requirements in real-
world deployment contexts.
6.2 Align Medicaid Enhanced Match Incentives with AI Governance Standards
The 90% enhanced federal matching rate available for Medicaid IT system design, development,
and implementation should be explicitly extended to cover the SDLC governance infrastructure
required for responsible AI deployment — including monitoring systems, evaluation frameworks,
and interoperability capabilities. States are currently incentivized to invest in IT infrastructure
generally, but lack specific guidance on how AI governance investments qualify for enhanced
matching. Clarifying this eligibility would materially reduce the financial barrier to responsible AI
adoption at the state Medicaid program level — a direct benefit to programs such as Missouri's
MO HealthNet that are simultaneously managing modernization and AI integration demands.
7.0 Role of R&D and Public-Private Partnerships
Research and development investment by HHS should be directed toward the foundational
infrastructure gaps that constrain responsible AI adoption — not toward additional AI model
development, which the private sector is pursuing aggressively and at scale. The comparative
advantage of HHS R&D investment lies in creating shared public goods: evaluation frameworks,
governance standards, interoperability infrastructure, and implementation playbooks that private-
sector entities cannot produce individually.
7.1 Fund SDLC Governance Playbooks for Clinical AI
HHS should fund the development of implementation playbooks that translate SDLC governance
disciplines into practical, actionable guidance for clinical AI deployment — specifically designed
for use by state Medicaid agencies, community health systems, and mid-market health IT
organizations that lack the internal resources to develop governance frameworks independently.
These playbooks should address the full AI deployment lifecycle from requirements definition
through post-deployment monitoring, and should be developed through cooperative agreements
that include practitioner-level IT consultants with direct experience in regulated government and
healthcare environments.
7.2 Establish State Medicaid AI Governance Pilots
HHS should fund cooperative agreements with a cohort of state Medicaid agencies to pilot SDLC-
based AI governance frameworks within active program modernization contexts. Missouri's MO
HealthNet is a strong candidate for such a pilot, given the convergence of active MMIS

modernization, new work requirement verification obligations, and CMS-funded rural health
transformation investment that collectively create a rich testing environment for AI governance

7.3 Prioritize Mid-Market and Practitioner Voices in R&D Investment
HHS R&D investment in clinical AI has historically been concentrated in large academic medical
centers and major defense and technology contractors. The implementation challenges that
constrain AI adoption in community health systems, state Medicaid agencies, and smaller health
organizations are distinct from those facing large institutions — and the solutions developed in
large institutional contexts are frequently not transferable. HHS should explicitly prioritize mid-
market IT consultants, regional health systems, and state agency partners in cooperative
agreements and applied research investments, ensuring that AI governance solutions are
developed and validated in the contexts where adoption challenges are greatest.
