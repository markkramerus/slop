About SimplePractice
SimplePractice is a leading practice management and electronic health record (EHR) platform
built for independent behavioral health, mental health, and wellness practitioners. We serve over
250,000 mental health practitioners across all 50 states, the majority of whom are solo or
small-group practitioners, including licensed psychologists, social workers, counselors, marriage
and family therapists, nurse practitioners, and psychiatrists.
We occupy a unique position in the AI conversation: we are both building AI-powered tools for
clinical care and supporting the independent practitioners who adopt them. That gives us direct,
ground-level insight into what AI adoption looks like in behavioral health settings—the wins, the
hesitations, and the real-world gaps that federal policy can help close.
This submission is grounded in responses from our Clinical Advisory Board and Customer
Advisory Board, many of whom use our platform daily and agreed to share their perspectives for
this submission.
What We Know: AI Is Working—And Mental Health Practitioners Have Real Concerns
Where It's Working
AI-assisted documentation is already changing practice for the better. As one SimplePractice
Customer Advisory Board member put it:
"Documentation currently has improved efficiency in my practice and allowed more eye
contact and interactions with the patient [versus the] computer."
- Zachary Moraity, MD
One of SimplePractice’s Clinical Advisors, who also uses SimplePractice’s AI powered note
taker with his patients, stated:
“AI assisted note taking has been a game changer, allowing me to focus on being in the
moment rather than writing things down. It’s actually changed the way I speak to
patients. I now summarize my thoughts at the end of the session, telling patients clearly

what my impression is and plan going forward. I started doing this to make the ambient
AI tool work better and I found that it really helps the patient understand how I’m thinking
and forces me to explain/answer questions about my formulation.”
- Ish Bhalla, MD
This reflects what we see across our platform: when AI tools are well-designed and
well-implemented, they reduce the administrative burden that pulls practitioners away from their
patients. That is a win worth protecting—and building on.
“One of the most exciting prospects is using AI to improve clinical care. I love the idea of
AI helping develop a case conceptualization and offering treatment suggestions. I
strongly believe that it would still be up to the therapist to use their background, training,
and clinical intuition to modify, adapt, and employ the AI suggestions. However, having
AI as a tool to assist with some of the heavy lifting would take a major administrative
load off of therapists and allow them to do their jobs more effectively.”
- Max Maisel, PhD
Where Mental Health Practitioners Are Hesitating
Adoption is not universal. Mental health practitioners are navigating genuine uncertainty, and
that uncertainty is suppressing uptake in ways that policy can directly address. Our team and
our advisors identified five core concerns:
1. Unclear liability:
When an AI-assisted note contributes to a clinical decision or a payer dispute, or a breach, who
is responsible? The practitioner? The platform? The AI vendor? This ambiguity is one of the top
concerns our advisors raised.
“The biggest barriers I anticipate are liability ambiguity and uncertainty around HIPAA
compliance. It's hard to feel confident adopting a new tool if it is unclear who is
accountable if something goes wrong.”
- Moe Ari Brown, LMFT
Similarly, as we see increases in the number of AI vendors in the field, clinicians are not always
clear on the standards that have been adhered to within the product.
“There’s still uncertainty around how data is processed across platforms, and whether
information is retained or shared beyond its intended use. I’m cautious about how patient
information is handled, stored, and protected. Until HIPPA safeguards are crystal clear,
caution isn’t resistance; it’s my ethical responsibility.”
- Dr. Dwayne Buckingham
2. The learning curve:
Mental health practitioners will adopt tools that save time, but only if those tools are intuitive and
come with accessible training.
“Cost is a real barrier that I have encountered in regards to adopting AI tools in my
practice. Quality AI tools aren’t cheap, and implementing them responsibly requires time,

training, and ongoing oversight. While I understand the benefits of AI, I am slightly
concerned about the cost of adopting it into my practice.”
- Dwayne Buckingham, PhD, LCSW-C
3. Reimbursement risk:
Practitioners see very real potential value of AI in supporting reimbursement. Dr. Dwayne
Buckingham states:
“I believe AI could significantly reduce my administrative burden…eligibility checks,
claims follow-ups, and reimbursement processes often require additional staff many
private practices can’t afford.”
However, they are also beginning to worry that AI-assisted documentation could work against
them, if their notes look "too clean" or insufficiently individualized, or if patient symptoms are not
accurately reflected with AI usage that payers will deny claims. Dr. Ish Bhalla adds:
“I don’t want third parties to have access to transcripts or recordings of sessions with
patients for the purpose of malpractice claims or payment restrictions. I don’t want clients
to feel concerned about their words and actions being used against them in any way
either.”
4. Clinical integrity:
Several advisors worried about the risk of going on "autopilot", and becoming so reliant on AI
that they miss a critical clinical signal. This isn't an argument against AI. It's an argument for
implementation standards that keep clinical judgment front and center. One of our customer
advisors, Dr. Max Maisel, notes that:
“One concern would be if therapists over-rely on AI and don't do their due diligence.
However, with proper training and education about how AI works and how it doesn't
work, I believe this can be largely avoided.”
5. Patient-facing AI:
Mental health practitioners want AI help with patient communication, but they're cautious. The
concern: "patient frustration or AI providing inaccurate interpretation/recommendations without
safeguards." Patient-facing AI features deserve a higher bar of oversight, and guidance should
reflect that distinction.
Our Recommendations
The RFI asks about regulation, reimbursement, and research and development. Our
recommendations are organized around those three themes, with a focus on what matters most
for behavioral health and independent practice.
1. Regulation: Clarify Accountability (Responds to RFI Q1, Q2, Q3)
The problem: Practitioners don't know how existing rules apply to AI. Does using AI
documentation create a HIPAA risk? What attestation is required? Who is liable if something

goes wrong? This uncertainty is a real adoption barrier, and a clear, plain-language framework
from HHS could help to clarify these issues.
Specific Recommendations:
● Publish clear guidance clarifying how HIPAA, professional licensure standards, and
existing documentation requirements apply when mental health practitioners use
AI-assisted tools.
● Clarify the liability allocation when AI tools contribute to a clinical note, a care
recommendation, or a documentation error.
● Create a certification or safe harbor pathway for EHR and practice management
platforms that implement AI responsibly so practitioners can trust the tools they use.
● Establish a minimum standard for clinical attestation: AI may assist, but the practitioner's
review and signature must remain the standard of record.
2. Regulation: Risk-Based Frameworks Over Bans (Responds to RFI Q3, Q5)
The problem: The emergence of consumer-facing AI chatbots that purport to provide mental
health support has raised legitimate safety concerns. Some of these tools operate outside any
clinical relationship, without human oversight, and without accountability for adverse outcomes,
leading some policymakers to propose outright bans on "therapy chatbots." However,
SimplePractice urges HHS to advocate for a risk-based framework rather than categorical
prohibitions. The distinction that matters is not whether AI is involved, but whether a licensed
clinician remains accountable for the patient's care and what safeguards have been put into
place. The current draft notes concerns about patient-facing AI providing "inaccurate
interpretation/recommendations without safeguards." This concern is well-founded, but the
solution is appropriate safeguards, not prohibition of all AI in patient communication.
Specific Recommendations:
● Adopt a risk-tiered regulatory posture that distinguishes between AI tools that support
practitioner judgment (lower risk) and AI tools that substitute for it (higher risk).
○ Low Risk: AI-assisted documentation, scheduling, billing automation
○ Medium Risk: AI-generated treatment suggestions, clinical decision support
○ High Risk: Patient-facing AI providing clinical guidance or interaction with
practitioner involvement
○ Critical Risk: Autonomous AI making treatment decisions without clinical
oversight
● Safeguards should align with the risk-tiered levels. While low risk tools may simply
require practitioner attestation or platform certifications, tools with increased risk may
involve a combination of safeguards appropriate for the risk level such as:
human-in-the-loop reviews, patient transparency, robust safety and effectiveness
validations, requirements for disclosure of limitations, scope limitations, and/or crisis
protocols with mandatory clinician escalation pathways

● Avoid regulatory frameworks that treat all "AI in mental health" as a single category.
AI-assisted documentation and autonomous therapy chatbots present fundamentally
different risk profiles and should be regulated differently.
3. Reimbursement: Don't Penalize Efficiency (Responds to RFI Q2, Q9)
The problem: The payer relationship is a significant source of anxiety for mental health
practitioners considering AI tools. If using AI to write notes efficiently is interpreted as cutting
corners, rather than working smarter, adoption will stall.
Specific Recommendations:
● Issue guidance clarifying that AI-assisted documentation does not inherently constitute
fraud, waste, or abuse under Medicare/Medicaid billing rules, provided clinical review is
documented.
● Work with CMS to confirm that AI-assisted clinical encounters remain fully reimbursable
when human judgment is clearly applied and documented.
● Explore reduced administrative burden (e.g., prior authorization simplification) for
practices using certified AI tools that demonstrably improve documentation quality and
access to care.
● Consider a demonstration program that tests reimbursement incentives for small and
solo behavioral health practices adopting AI tools.
4. Reimbursement: Opportunity to Accelerate Adoption (Responds to RFI Q2)
The problem: The current draft appropriately raises concerns about AI-assisted documentation
being penalized by payers. But reimbursement policy is not only a risk to mitigate; it is a lever to
accelerate adoption. No CPT codes exist specifically for AI-assisted clinical activities, leaving
practitioners uncertain how to document and bill for encounters where AI played a supporting
role. Medicare and Medicaid documentation requirements were designed for a pre-AI world. AI
could enable more efficient documentation that meets clinical needs, but current payer
expectations create friction.
Specific Recommendations:
● Work with CMS and the AMA CPT Editorial Panel to evaluate whether new codes or
modifiers are appropriate for AI-assisted behavioral health services, particularly for:
○ AI-supported outcome measurement (e.g., automated symptom tracking and
measurement)
○ AI-assisted care coordination (e.g., summarizing patient history for referrals,
matching)
○ AI-enhanced documentation (e.g., ambient listening, documentation generation)
○ AI-assisted client facing tools monitored by the clinician
● Ensure that any AI-related reimbursement policies apply equally to independent
practitioners and health systems. Small practices should not face higher barriers to
payment for using the same tools available to large organizations.

5. Research and Development: Prioritize Behavioral Health (Responds to RFI Q6, Q9,
Q10)
The problem: Behavioral health is chronically underrepresented in health AI research, despite
having one of the highest documentation burdens and one of the most significant workforce
shortages in the sector. This is a gap HHS can help fill.
Specific Recommendations:
● Fund research specifically on AI use in behavioral health and mental health settings,
including solo and small-group practices, not just large health systems.
● Prioritize research on patient experience and safety in AI-assisted behavioral health
care, including patient-facing communication tools.
● Invest in implementation science: what training, workflows, and design patterns lead to
safe, sustainable AI adoption in independent practice?
● Support public-private partnerships that bring together EHR vendors, mental health
practitioners, and payers to develop behavioral-health-specific AI use standards.
Innovation in the application of AI in mental health can bring very tangible clinical value to
providers, but to do so effectively, research in the application of tools in independent solo and
small group practice settings is essential.
I hope AI can help me see blind spots in my clinical decision-making. It would be great to
get recommendations on tests, medications, and psychotherapy interventions that I
might have missed because of unconscious bias or new research that I might not know
about.
- Ish Bhalla, MD
6. State Regulatory Fragmentation: A Hidden Barrier to AI Adoption (Responds to RFI Q1,
Q2, Q3)
The problem: SimplePractice serves practitioners licensed in all 50 states. The inconsistent
patchwork of state-level rules governing AI use is a significant barrier to use. Consider the
practitioner who is licensed and providing treatment across multiple state lines. Each state may
have different requirements for AI use across patient consent or disclosure, documentation
standards, and/or strict use limitations. Some states have enacted restrictions on AI in health
care that may sweep too broadly. While we share concerns, blanket prohibitions on AI-assisted
tools risk cutting practitioners off from tools that support their practice.
This fragmentation creates compliance uncertainty that disproportionately burdens small
practices. Large health systems have compliance departments to navigate multi-state
requirements; solo practitioners do not.

Specific Recommendations:
● Issue federal guidance affirming that AI-assisted documentation tools used by licensed
practitioners within the scope of their clinical judgment do not require additional
state-level authorization beyond existing telehealth and practice act frameworks.
● Work with state licensing boards through organizations like the Federation of State
Medical Boards (FSMB), the American Association of State Counseling Boards, and the
Association of State and Provincial Psychology Boards (ASPPB) to develop model
guidelines for AI use in behavioral health that states can adopt, reducing fragmentation.
A Note on Behavioral Health
Behavioral health is often missing from health AI conversations, but it shouldn't be. The
workforce shortage is still severe in spite of mental health parity laws, the documentation burden
is among the highest in any specialty, and the therapeutic relationship itself is a clinical
instrument that AI can either support or undermine, depending on how it's deployed.
One dimension of this challenge is worth naming explicitly. As consumer AI tools have become
widely accessible, more people are using them as a first—and sometimes only—point of contact
for mental health concerns. For individuals experiencing suicidal ideation, acute paranoia, or
other conditions requiring clinical judgment, this gap carries real risk: a well-designed AI can be
responsive and even helpful, but it cannot assess imminent safety, coordinate care, or bear
professional accountability for what happens next. The question for HHS is not whether to stop
this, as it is already occurring at scale, but whether federal policy will create the conditions
under which a human mental health practitioner remains meaningfully in the loop. Supporting
responsible AI adoption within clinical relationships is one of the most direct levers available.
SimplePractice is committed to getting this right. We believe AI can expand access to behavioral
health care, reduce practitioner and clinician burnout, and improve outcomes if the policy
environment gives practitioners and platform builders the clarity and confidence to move
forward.
We look forward to working with HHS to make this a reality.

successful, I'm excited about that support. However, I urge clinicians to use the
technology as operational support that helps them deepen therapy work.”
