Doctronic has emerged as a leader in AI-native clinical care, demonstrating that artificial
intelligence can safely expand access while preserving physician oversight and
accountability. In 2025, Doctronic’s platform supported more than 20 million patient
visits, reflecting scaled deployment of AI-powered triage, structured diagnostic
reasoning, medication safety screening, and automated documentation workflows.
A defining milestone was Doctronic’s work in Utah, which marked the first deployment of
an autonomous AI system to process prescription refills within defined clinical
guardrails. The system safely evaluated refill eligibility, screened for contraindications
and drug interactions, and escalated exceptions to licensed physicians when risk
thresholds were met. This initiative demonstrated that carefully bounded autonomous AI
can manage high-volume, protocol-driven tasks while maintaining safety and
compliance.
Importantly, Doctronic operates under a human-in-the-loop model for broader clinical
care. The organization conducts approximately 15,000 human doctor–led encounters
per month, with physicians reviewing AI-generated workups, validating care plans, and
managing complex or high-risk cases.
The combination of scaled automation and physician oversight has enabled measurable
expansion of access, reduced administrative burden, and improved operational
efficiency—positioning Doctronic at the forefront of responsible, large-scale AI
deployment in clinical care.

• Fee-for-service (FFS) rewards volume, not automation or prevention.

b. Regulatory Uncertainty
• Ambiguity between FDA-regulated medical devices and CDS exempt
under 21st Century Cures Act § 520(o)(1)(E).

c. Liability Ambiguity
• Unclear standards of care when AI assists in diagnostic reasoning.
d. Interoperability Gaps

f. Trust & Governance
• Clinician skepticism without transparent evaluation standards.
• Lack of standardized AI assurance frameworks.
2. What regulatory, payment policy, or programmatic design changes should
HHS prioritize to incentivize the effective use of AI in clinical care and why?
HHS should prioritize reforms that align incentives with prevention, automation,
and measurable quality improvement—rather than visit volume.
• Modernize Payment Policy to Reward AI-Enabled Care
A. Recognize AI-Assisted Clinical Work Under the Physician Fee Schedule
Under 42 CFR Part 414, CMS should:
• Create reimbursement pathways for AI-assisted documentation, triage,
and longitudinal monitoring.
• Clarify that time saved through automation does not reduce eligibility
for E/M coding when clinical complexity remains.
• Recognize asynchronous, AI-augmented encounters as reimbursable
services.
Why: AI increases efficiency and expands capacity, but current FFS rules
penalize reductions in time or visit volume.
B. Integrate AI Performance into Value-Based Payment Models

Within:
• 42 CFR Part 425 (MSSP)
• ACO REACH (42 CFR Part 512)
CMS should:
• Explicitly recognize validated AI tools as quality and cost-reduction

• Allow shared savings credit for AI-driven reductions in ED visits,
readmissions, and avoidable utilization.
• Encourage AI-enabled care redesign within primary care
transformation models.
Why: AI’s strongest ROI appears in risk-bearing environments. Value-based

6. Where have AI tools deployed in clinical care met or exceeded performance
and cost expectations and where have they fallen short?
a. What kinds of novel AI tools would have the greatest potential to
improve health care outcomes, give new insights on quality, and help
reduce costs?
The AI tools with the greatest potential to improve outcomes, generate new
quality insights, and reduce costs are those embedded directly into care
delivery and population health workflows. AI-native primary care platforms
that provide structured triage, diagnostic support, and automated
documentation under clinician supervision can expand access, reduce
emergency department utilization, and alleviate clinician burnout. Longitudinal
risk detection models that continuously analyze clinical, claims, and social

determinants data can identify rising-risk patients earlier and trigger
preventive interventions that reduce hospitalizations and total cost of care.
Medication optimization engines can decrease adverse drug events and
polypharmacy risks. AI-driven post-discharge and chronic disease monitoring
tools can reduce readmissions and improve control of high-cost conditions.
Finally, quality intelligence systems that synthesize claims and clinical data to
identify practice variation and care gaps can provide actionable insights for
value-based performance improvement. The highest-impact AI tools are
those that measurably improve prevention, efficiency, and risk management
rather than automate isolated administrative tasks.

organizations have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical

AI adoption in clinical care is driven by enterprise leadership focused on cost,
quality, risk, and workforce impact—not solely by IT.

o Executive Leadership (CEO & Board): Sets strategic transformation
priorities.
o Clinical Leadership (CMO & Quality Committees): Validates safety,
standard-of-care integration, and physician adoption.
o Finance Leadership (CFO & Finance Committee): Evaluates ROI
under FFS vs. value-based contracts.
o Board / Risk Committees: Oversee liability, compliance, and patient
safety.
