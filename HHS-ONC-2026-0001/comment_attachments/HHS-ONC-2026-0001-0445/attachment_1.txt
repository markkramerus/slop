The Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) is an international medical and professional
association representing approximately 3,100 physicians, doctoral scientists, and medical
laboratory scientists (technologists) who perform or are involved with laboratory testing based
on knowledge derived from molecular biology, genetics, and genomics. Membership includes
professionals from academic medicine, hospital-based and private clinical laboratories, the
government, and the in vitro diagnostics industry. AMP members are highly involved in the
development, validation, and interpretation of molecular diagnostic tests, including biomarker
tests for the diagnosis and management of inherited disorders, cancers, and rare and infectious
diseases.
AMP supports the responsible adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) or machine learning tools to
improve clinical molecular diagnostics across clinical laboratory workflows spanning analytics,
reporting, and interoperability, provided that is consistent with the practice of laboratory
medicine including implementation with human-in-the-loop professional oversight, deliberate
validation, transparent performance monitoring, and appropriate guardrails to ensure accuracy,
equity, patient safety, and regulatory compliance. To ensure these AMP requests that HHS
prioritize four actions:

1. Update the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) to revise the definition
of “laboratory” to reflect contemporary molecular laboratory practices and add
“laboratory analytics” to cover the use of AI tools in clinical molecular diagnostic testing.
2. Provide implementation guidance and program support to adopt structured, standards-
based genomic and patient data for interoperability across Electronic Health
Record/Laboratory Information System and payers.
3. Address challenges with Prior Authorization (PA) by requiring payers to provide specific,
structured denial rationales including coding and policy references and support
electronic prior authorization (ePA) to better align processes across Medicare.
4. Promote validated, human-in-the-loop AI by encouraging validation on representative
populations, transparent documentation of training and evaluation data and post-
deployment monitoring with final clinical responsibility retained by the qualified laboratory
professionals and physicians.
AI holds tremendous promise for medicine including in the delivery of molecular laboratory
testing. We provide greater details below in response to the specific questions in the RFI.

1. Update CLIA to reflect current laboratory practices
Clinical laboratories play an extremely important role in the U.S. healthcare system, as an
astounding 14 billion laboratory tests are conducted annually with 70% of all medical decisions
relying on the results of laboratory tests to guide diagnosis or treatments.1
In order to assist laboratory professionals, a wide range of Artificial Intelligence/Machine
Learning (AI/ML) tools are currently used in clinical laboratories. Some technologies, such as
algorithms, work in tandem as part of laboratory developed testing procedures while other more
complex AI tools are used to obtain patient results to guide treatment options.
Electronic Health Records (EHRs) that are integrated into healthcare systems and utilized by
laboratories throughout the country are tools that help streamline certain laboratory operational
processes and reduce administrative burdens. Laboratory information systems (LIS) and other
laboratory instrument software are critical to laboratory operations because they have many
other functions in addition to those in EHRs. The LIS is the core digital infrastructure that
manages, tracks, and integrates all operational, analytical, and reporting activities within a
clinical laboratory. In molecular diagnostics—including genomic medicine and infectious disease

2. Provide implementation guidance and program support to adopt structured,
standards-based genomic and patient data for interoperability across Electronic
Health Record/Laboratory Information System and payers.
AMP has acknowledged the critical role of informatics in advancing genomic medicine. The
incorporation of genomics into clinical practice is progressing rapidly, with many laboratories
using advanced technology for next-generation sequencing (NGS). However, fully utilizing
genomic data within the EHR remains challenging because of limitations in interoperability
standards and technology. Organizations like AMP are working to develop guidelines to
standardize the interoperability of genomic data in EHR systems. Despite the expansion of
genomics in medicine, information systems struggle to effectively support genomic workflows
from laboratory and patient-facing provider perspectives. The complexity of genomic data, lack
of data standards, and insufficient genomic fluency and functionality have resulted in significant
gaps between the generation, interoperability, and utilization of genomic data. These gaps pose
challenges for laboratory and pathology professionals, clinicians, and patients in generating,
communicating, interpreting, and utilizing genomic test results. The AMP EHR Working Group
was formed to assess these challenges and propose solutions for the display and use of
genomic data in EHRs11. Genomic data can be difficult to locate in EHRs and more difficult to
compare across tests and panels over time. Additionally, the AMP recognizes that all
components of the software and systems used to generate genomic test results are important.
In 2016, Congress passed the 21st Century Cures Act. While the Act mandated that laboratories
and providers facilitate patient access to electronic health information (EHI), it did not include
specific, dedicated financial assistance to cover the costs of these technical implementations
and could increase financial and administrative burdens on clinical laboratories seeking to

maintain compliance with regulatory requirements.5 To achieve data interoperability,
laboratories will require funding to ensure that they have the ability to broadly implement and/or
replace systems to support interoperability data standards in compliance with regulatory
requirements.
Although the use of genomics in medicine is expanding rapidly, information systems are lagging
in their ability to support genomic workflows from both the laboratory and patient-facing provider
perspectives. The complexity of genomic data, lack of needed data standards, lack of genomic
fluency and functionality, and several other factors have contributed to the gaps between
genomic data generation, interoperability, and utilization. These gaps pose significant
challenges to laboratory and pathology professionals, clinicians, and patients in terms of
generating, communicating, consuming, and using genomic test results.
Concerns exist regarding the ability of EHRs to capture complete and accurate patient
information, including genomic testing information (Carter et al., 2022, Background Table X, and
additional Background information), which may be amplified when utilizing AI systems. For
example, because the AI systems used for populating forms for prior authorization pull this
information from EHRs that may contain incomplete and/or inaccurate patient and/or genomic
testing information, they may erroneously delay or deny appropriate testing. Gaps in data
interoperability exacerbate these challenges. Additionally, AI systems must be maintained and
updated in real time to ensure that the latest payer policies and practice guidelines are
appropriately applied, along with other challenges mentioned in Table X. If AI systems utilize
flawed data, rendered downstream decisions may be inappropriate.

AI is being applied to laboratory and/or treating provider-submitted test prior authorizations to
automate, accelerate, and optimize the review, approval, and billing-readiness processes. Key
cases, how they work, benefits, and risks are summarized in the background information below.
Specifically, regarding laboratory testing in molecular medicine and genomics, AI prior
authorization faces additional challenges, including complex clinical justification, evolving
guidelines, inadequate coding, and payer knowledge gaps (see Background).
AI-driven prior authorization has significant potential to reduce administrative friction and
improve test utilization; realizing these benefits requires addressing data quality, interoperability,
and regulatory challenges, particularly in specialized areas like genomic medicine. Key
considerations to achieve include, but are not limited to, the following:
• Regulatory Compliance: AI‑generated content must meet HIPAA, 21st Century Cures
Act, and payer‑specific requirements.
• Human‑In‑The‑Loop: All AI-drafted narratives should be reviewed and signed by a
qualified provider before submission.
• Data quality: Accurate CPT/HCPCS coding, up-to-date payer policies, and complete
patient demographics are essential for reliable AI performance.
• Improved EHR Integration: Better structured data capture in EHRs will enhance the
ability of AI to extract relevant clinical context.
• Explainable AI: Development of more transparent AI models that can explain denial or
appropriateness decisions to clinicians.
• Real-Time Policy Sync: Enables more dynamic integration with payer policies to reduce
lag in rule updates.
• Outcome Tracking: AI systems that learn from approved tests and their clinical outcomes
to refine appropriateness models over time.

4. Promote validated, human-in-the-loop AI
AI is being applied across multiple stages of molecular laboratory workflows, including sample
processing and data analysis, result interpretation, and clinical reporting (Background Table X).
8 https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/prior-auth-reforms-issue-brief.pdf

These applications aim to improve the accuracy, efficiency, turnaround time, and clinical utility
of molecular test results.
Artificial intelligence (AI) has moved from experimental use to routine deployment in several
areas of molecular diagnostics, particularly in genomic variant calling, infectious disease
sequencing, and laboratory quality monitoring. In specific analytic tasks with well-defined
performance metrics and large training datasets, AI has met or exceeded expectations for
accuracy and operational efficiency. However, AI has underperformed in areas involving
complex biological interpretation (e.g., variant pathogenicity classification, structural variant
interpretation, broad antimicrobial resistance prediction, and variant interpretation in the correct
clinical context). Multistakeholder engagement is now required to:
1. Promote responsible clinical validation and regulatory clarity.
2. Incentivize high-value AI applications with demonstrable patient impact.
3. Avoid overinvestment in tools that lack generalizability or cost-effectiveness.
4. Ensure interoperability, transparency, and equity in deployment.
AI in molecular diagnostics can deliver when it replaces manual analytic steps, reduces repeat
testing, shortens the time to targeted therapy, and/or increases the diagnostic, predictive or
therapeutic yield in actionable conditions. Several safeguards are necessary for safe and
effective integration into routine clinical practice (see Background). AI underdelivers when
biological knowledge gaps limit model reliability, implementation costs exceed operational
savings, and/or generalizability across populations is poor. AI in molecular laboratory medicine
is not uniformly transformative as its value may be task-dependent (see Background).
Safely and effectively integrating AI into molecular laboratory testing:
• Robust validation: Prospective clinical validation in diverse populations and
comprehensive variant sets.
• Transparent methodology: Clear documentation of model training data, performance
metrics, and limitations.
• Human-in-the-loop: Pathologists retain final decision-making authority; AI serves as a
decision support tool and not a replacement.
• Continuous monitoring: Ongoing performance tracking, error reporting, and model
recalibration.
• Ethical oversight: Institutional review, informed consent, and equity assessments for AI
deployment.
• Explainable AI (XAI): Development of interpretable models that provide transparent
reasoning for variant classifications.
• Standardized data exchange: Implementation of fit-for-purpose genomic data standards
to enable seamless AI integration across systems.
• Outcome tracking: Linking AI-driven diagnoses to patient outcomes to validate and refine
model performance.

The adoption of AI in molecular diagnostics is rarely driven by a single individual. It typically
requires alignment across clinical, laboratory, financial, regulatory, and IT leadership (see
Background).Influence varies by organizational structure (academic medical center, community
hospital, or integrated delivery network). Strategic investment, regulatory clarity, and outcome-
focused validation will determine whether AI in molecular diagnostics reduces costs and
improves patient outcomes — or merely adds technological complexity without measurable
benefit.

Prior Authorization Background
AI in PA use cases:
• Eligibility & coverage determination: Match patient, payer, and policy rules to determine
if a test is covered.
• Order appropriateness checking: Compare requested tests with clinical indications,
guidelines, and previous results to flag unnecessary or missing tests.
• Prior authorization (PA) form automation: Auto-population of PA forms from EHR and lab
systems.
• Clinical justification generation: Drafting evidence-based rationales citing guidelines,
prior results, and diagnosis codes.
• Workflow triage and routing: Prioritize urgent requests and route complex cases to
specialists or medical directors.
• Predictive denials and cost estimation: Predict the likelihood of denial and estimate
patient financial responsibility.
• Decision support for formulary/test selection: Recommend alternative tests or panels
with a higher likelihood of approval.
• Audit, reporting, and analytics: Identify denial patterns, provider behavior, and process
bottlenecks.
Models and technologies currently used:
• Natural language processing (NLP; named entity recognition and relation extraction) to
extract clinical indications, prior results, and text justifications.

• Classification models for predicting approval/denial and required documentation.
• Transformer LLMs for drafting justifications and summarizing records (with guardrails).
• Rules-based engines for explicit payer policies and hard constraints.
• Robotic process automation (RPA) to interact with payer portals where APIs are
unavailable.
• Knowledge bases linking guidelines (e.g., clinical society guidance), coverage policies,
and prior authorization rules.

Fit-for-purpose Data Interoperability Background

The use of genomics in clinical medicine is expanding rapidly, and many laboratories are
implementing highly complex technologies to perform next-generation sequencing (NGS).
However, the full utilization of complex genomic data in the electronic health record (EHR) is
challenging in the current state of interoperability standards and technology. AMP and other
professional organizations are actively working to develop guidance to standardize the
interoperability of genomic data in EHR systems.
Genomic data can also be difficult to locate in electronic health records (EHRs) and can be
even more difficult to compare across different tests and panels over time. Additionally, AMP is
cognizant that all components of the software and systems used to generate genomic test
results for patients are important. AMP has long recognized the importance of informatics to the
success of genomic medicine.
With the increasing use of molecular pathology and genomic testing, complaints regarding the
significant lack of functionality that necessitates the use of paper records and/or manual transfer
of data to and from instrument software, laboratory information systems (LISs), and electronic
health records (EHRs) have increased. LISs and other laboratory instrument software are
critical to laboratory operations because they have many other functions in addition to those in
EHRs. The LIS is the core digital infrastructure that manages, tracks, and integrates all
operational, analytical, and reporting activities within a clinical laboratory. In molecular
diagnostics—including genomic medicine and infectious disease testing—the LIS serves as the
backbone connecting specimen flow, analytical instruments, interpretation workflows,
compliance systems, and EHRs. Reporting requirements for different testing methods
(molecular pathology, genomic and germline and somatic variant) in various types of
laboratories were examined with regard to challenges in EHRs, including the following
laboratories: molecular and genetic; cytogenetics; those performing fluorescence in
situ hybridization (FISH), tissue typing, histocompatibility, immunogenetics (human leukocyte
antigen (HLA) testing), and infectious organisms.

Laboratory directors and clinical teams working with medical team senior leadership that have
identified ways for AI to help reduce the administrative burden and/or improve patient care. Most
influential decision-makers:
1. Laboratory Medical Director (analytic authority)
2. Chief Medical Officer or CMO (clinical governance)
3. Chief Financial Officer or CFO (financial gatekeeper)
4. Chief Information Officer/Chief Data Officer or CIO/CDO (technical feasibility)
5. Stewardship or genetics committees (domain endorsement)
6. External regulators (structural influence)
In most large academic centers, the Laboratory Medical Director + CFO + CIO + CMO
alignment is the critical adoption bottleneck. If the laboratory director is convinced of analytic
validity and the CFO sees cost justification with CMO endorsement required for standard-of-
care change, adoption is likely. Without alignment among these four, deployment rarely
progresses beyond limited pilots. Primary administrative hurdles include, but are not limited to,
implementation, data storage, and maintenance costs in both direct costs and FTE; ongoing
personnel and direct costs to support validation and use of AI in a CLIA environment; and
additional education to understand the complexity of the test systems and inputs needed to
build, launch, and operate AI in a manner that reduces the risk of patient harms and medical
errors. Other hurdles include regulatory ambiguity, reimbursement gaps, integration complexity,
validation burden, workforce trust, infrastructure cost, and fragmented governance.

There are several potential benefits of AI in clinical molecular laboratories
• Improved Diagnostic Accuracy: AI enhances variant detection and interpretation, thereby
reducing missed diagnoses and false positives.
• Faster Turnaround Times: Automation and intelligent prioritization reduce the time from
sample to report.
• Increased Throughput: Robotic and automated workflows allow laboratories to process
more samples efficiently.
• Improved Workflows: Automation decreases manual curation and review work.
• More Standardized Reporting: AI-generated reports ensure consistency and compliance
with professional guidelines.

• Knowledge Integration: NLP and machine learning synthesize vast amounts of genetic
and clinical literature automatically.
• Proactive Variant Monitoring: AI tracks evolving evidence and alerts clinicians to
reclassifications or new findings.

For AI to be safely and effectively integrated into molecular laboratory testing:

• Robust validation: Prospective clinical validation on diverse populations and
comprehensive variant sets.
• Transparent methodology: Clear documentation of model training data, performance
metrics, and limitations.
• Human-in-the-loop: Pathologists retain final decision-making authority; AI serves as
decision support, not replacement.
• Continuous monitoring: Ongoing performance tracking, error reporting, and model
recalibration.
• Ethical oversight: Institutional review, informed consent, and equity assessments for AI
deployment.
• Explainable AI (XAI): Development of interpretable models that provide transparent
reasoning for variant classifications.
• Standardized data exchange: Implementation of fit for purpose genomic data standards
to enable seamless AI integration across systems.
• Outcome tracking: Linking AI-driven diagnoses to patient outcomes to validate and refine
model performance.
AI holds tremendous promise for molecular laboratory testing, but realizing its benefits requires
addressing data standardization, validation rigor, and data interoperability challenges—
particularly for rare variants and underrepresented populations.
7. Which role(s), decision maker(s), or governing bodies within health care organizations
have the most influence on the adoption of AI for clinical care? What are the primary
administrative hurdles to the adoption of AI in clinical care?
Adoption of AI in molecular diagnostics is rarely driven by a single individual. It typically requires
alignment across clinical, laboratory, financial, regulatory, and IT leadership. However, influence
varies by organizational structure (academic medical center vs. community hospital vs.
integrated delivery network).
Laboratory directors and clinical teams working with medical team senior leadership that have
identified ways for AI to help reduce administrative burden and/or improve patient care. Most
influential decision makers:
7. Laboratory Medical Director (analytic authority)
8. Chief Medical Officer (CMO; clinical governance)
9. Chief Financial Officer (CFO; financial gatekeeper)
10.Chief Information Officer/Chief Data Officer (CIO/CDO; technical feasibility)
11.Stewardship or genetics committees (domain endorsement)
12.External regulators (structural influence)
In most large academic centers, the Laboratory Medical Director + CFO + CIO + CMO
alignment is the critical adoption bottleneck. If the laboratory director is convinced of analytic
validity and the CFO sees cost justification with CMO endorsement required for standard-of-
care change, adoption is likely. Without alignment among these four, deployment rarely

progresses beyond limited pilots. Primary administrative hurdles include but are not limited to
implementation, data storage, and maintenance costs in both direct costs and personnel full
time equivalents (FTE); ongoing personnel and direct costs to support validation and use of AI
in a CLIA environment; additional education to understand the complexity of what systems and
inputs will be needed to build, launch, and operate AI in a manner that reduces the risk of
patient harms and medical errors. Other hurdles include regulatory ambiguity, reimbursement
gaps, integration complexity, validation burden, workforce trust, infrastructure cost, and
fragmented governance.
