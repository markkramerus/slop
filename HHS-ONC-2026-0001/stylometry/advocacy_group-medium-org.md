# Writing Style Analysis: advocacy_group-medium-org

## Tone, Formality, and Overall Voice

This voice group writes in a **professional-institutional register** that is measured and formal without being cold or bureaucratic. The tone is consistently respectful and collaborative — these organizations want to be seen as partners, not adversaries. There's a notable absence of emotional appeals or personal anecdotes; the writing stays in the realm of policy and professional expertise. However, it doesn't feel robotic either — there's genuine organizational personality in how each group frames its specific domain as underserved or uniquely positioned. The RMLC comment, for instance, opens with a value statement ("empowering Americans, especially those in rural areas") that carries mild advocacy warmth before pivoting to technical specifics. PsiAN's comment has a slightly more cautionary, protective tone compared to the more collaborative posture of HFSA or APhA. The key emotional register is **institutional confidence with deference** — these groups know their lane, they're proud of it, and they're asking to be included in the conversation, not demanding to lead it.

## Opening Patterns and Framing

Openings in this voice group follow a tight, recognizable formula: **identify the organization, acknowledge the opportunity, signal the stance**. Almost every comment begins with "On behalf of [Organization]" or "[Organization] submits this comment in response to..." This isn't laziness — it's deliberate credentialing. The organization name comes first because legitimacy is the first argument. Several comments then immediately pivot to a gap-framing move: *our sector/specialty has been overlooked, and here's why that matters.* DSI does this most explicitly ("Dentistry is a major clinical domain...yet it has historically been underrepresented"), but the RMLC and Alliance for Connected Care do versions of it too. What's notably absent is any dramatic hook, rhetorical question, or narrative opener. These comments don't try to grab attention — they assume they already have it by virtue of being a credentialed stakeholder. Writers mimicking this voice should **resist the urge to open with a compelling scene or bold claim** — that reads as AI trying too hard.

## Argument Structure and Language Characteristics

Arguments are built through **enumerated priorities and domain-specific logic** rather than narrative or emotional storytelling. DSI's numbered list is the clearest example, but even the prose-only comments (HFSA, APhA) organize around discrete recommendation clusters. The reasoning style is deductive: here is our sector's situation, here is the gap or risk, here is the policy fix. Data is referenced in general terms ("vast amounts of patient data," "all 50 states") rather than cited with specific statistics — this is a meaningful human marker. These aren't academic papers; they're advocacy letters. Vocabulary is **specialized but not impenetrable** — terms like "interoperability," "post-market monitoring," "clinical ground truth," and "modality-agnostic" appear naturally without being defined, signaling that the intended reader is a peer-level policy professional. Sentence structure tends toward **medium complexity**: not the short punchy sentences of consumer writing, but not the nested subordinate-clause marathons of legal text either. A typical sentence makes one clear claim with one supporting qualifier. The APhA resolution format — numbered, parallel, verb-forward ("APhA opposes," "APhA calls on," "APhA encourages") — is a distinctive structural pattern worth noting.

## What Makes This Voice Human and How to Avoid AI Pitfalls

The humanity in this voice comes from **specificity of organizational identity** and **mild hedging around scope**. These groups don't claim to speak for all of healthcare — they speak for their members, their sector, their domain. PsiAN says "particularly within behavioral health and psychotherapy." APMA gives a direct staff contact with a phone number. These are grounding details that AI tends to omit in favor of sweeping generality. **This voice avoids**: grand philosophical statements about the future of AI, emotional language about patient suffering, excessive throat-clearing, and the word "crucial" used more than once (though RMLC uses it — sparingly). It also avoids false balance or hedging for its own sake; when these groups have a position, they state it plainly ("Dentistry is not ready for autonomous clinical AI"). The risk of AI-sounding writing in this register is **over-structuring** — producing perfectly parallel bullet points with identical sentence lengths, or using transitional phrases like "Furthermore," "It is worth noting," or "In conclusion." Real comments in this voice sometimes end abruptly, include a contact person, or close with a simple offer to collaborate. To write authentically in this voice: **lead with your organization's name and specific domain, state your position without over-explaining it, use numbered lists only when you genuinely have discrete points, include at least one concrete organizational detail (a contact, a program name, a specific membership type), and let the argument end when it's done rather than summarizing it.**

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.

### Example 3 (Source: HHS-ONC-2026-0001-0057)
> Dental Standards Institute (DSI) submits this comment in response to the HHS Request for Information regarding accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence (AI) in clinical care.
Dentistry is a major clinical domain affecting systemic health outcomes, yet it has historically been underrepresented in national health IT and interoperability strategy. Dentistry previously lacked a unified standards authority focused on emerging technology, interoperability, and patient-centered care. DSI was formed to fill this gap and has already advanced foundational work in dental interoperability, patient-centered record portability, and calibration frameworks supporting consistent clinical and payer-facing workflows. Information about DSI is available at dentalstandardsinstitute.com.
HHS should accelerate AI adoption alongside appropriate trust infrastructure. In dentistry, the following priorities are essential:
1. AI must remain decision-support. Dentistry is not ready for autonomous clinical AI. Human review, clear accountability, auditability, and post-deployment monitoring must remain mandatory.
2. Dentistry must modernize diagnosis. Dentistry remains treatment-driven and does not consistently use diagnostic coding. Reimbursement policy should support routine diagnosis capture to enable oral-systemic collaborative care and to provide valid clinical ground truth for AI systems.
3. Independent external validation must become standard. Most dental AI tools rely on vendor self-validation using proprietary datasets. Independent benchmarking datasets, standardized transparency reporting (including subgroup performance), and post-market monitoring expectations are necessary to protect patients and promote trust.
4. Interoperability is the bottleneck. High-value AI requires structured, standards-based dental-medical data exchange. Dentistry remains siloed in proprietary systems and disconnected from broader healthcare records. HHS should explicitly include dentistry in national interoperability initiatives.
DSI stands ready to collaborate with HHS/ASTP, CMS, NIH, and FDA to scale responsible dental AI adoption through standards development, interoperability alignment, and independent validation frameworks.
Respectfully submitted,
Dental Standards Institute (DSI)
