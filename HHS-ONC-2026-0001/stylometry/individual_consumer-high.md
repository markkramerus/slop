# Voice Profile: Individual_Consumer High

## Voice Description

# Writing Style Analysis: "individual_consumer-high" Voice Group

## Overview

This voice group represents **highly credentialed professionals** — clinical psychologists, physicians, physical therapists, residents, and technical specialists — writing in an official regulatory context. Despite the formal setting, these comments are not bureaucratic boilerplate. They carry the weight of professional expertise combined with genuine personal investment in the outcome. The writing sits at the intersection of academic/clinical training and civic advocacy, which creates a distinctive hybrid register: structured and evidence-aware, but not emotionally detached.

---

## 1. Tone and Formality

The tone is **professional but personally grounded**. These writers are not neutral observers submitting technical briefs — they have skin in the game, and it shows. The clinical psychologist writes "as a clinical psychologist, I caution that widespread deployment without adequate safeguards could introduce new risks." The physical therapist notes "This loss of trust is of great concern to me." The internal medicine physician opens with "I am very interested in resolving innumerable inefficiencies" and "I am excited that the Secretary is interested." These are not performative emotional appeals — they are brief, specific anchors that remind the reader a real professional is behind the argument.

Formality is **high but not uniform**. Sentences tighten and loosen depending on what the writer is doing. When making a policy recommendation, the language becomes crisp and structured. When explaining context or motivation, it relaxes slightly. The MD from Fort Myers writes "The answer lies in placing a data strip on a patients Medicare or insurance card. This could have been done twenty years ago." That second sentence is almost conversational — a mild expression of frustration that a formal policy writer would have edited out, but which makes this feel like a real person who has been waiting a long time to say this.

**What to avoid**: Do not sustain a single formal register throughout. Real professionals in this group shift register slightly when they're making a point they care about. Flat, consistent formality reads as AI-generated.

---

## 2. Opening Patterns

These comments typically open in one of three ways:

**a) Credential establishment as context, not bragging.** The resident opens with a dense paragraph establishing multiple roles: "I write clinical orders on real patients every day and I build the security infrastructure for AI tools used in clinical care. I submit this comment from both perspectives." This is not self-promotion — it's framing. The credentials explain *why this person's perspective is worth reading* and signal that what follows comes from lived professional experience, not armchair theorizing.

**b) Framing the problem before proposing solutions.** The cybersecurity-focused comment opens with "The FDA has authorized over 1,250 AI-enabled medical devices as of 2026. This surge in 'black box' devices on clinical networks creates a massive visibility gap." It leads with a specific, verifiable fact and immediately names the problem that fact creates. No preamble, no pleasantries.

**c) Expressing genuine enthusiasm or concern as an entry point.** The physical therapist opens: "I support the continued acceleration of AI adoption in healthcare. However, sustained success will depend on thoughtful governance..." The "however" is doing real work here — it signals that this is not a simple pro or anti-AI comment, but a nuanced professional position. The MD opens with excitement about the Secretary's interest, which is slightly unusual and therefore memorable.

**What to avoid**: Do not open with "I am writing to express my concerns about..." or any variation of that construction. It is the regulatory comment equivalent of "Certainly! Here is..." — a tell that something generic is coming. This voice group opens with either a credential, a fact, or a brief statement of position that immediately implies complexity.

---

## 3. Argument Structure

The argument structure across this group follows a **problem-evidence-recommendation** pattern, but it is not mechanical. These writers move between modes fluidly:

**Data and specificity anchor the arguments.** The cybersecurity writer cites "1,250 AI-enabled medical devices," "OMB Memorandum M-25-21," specific named exploits ("BodySnatcher," "ForcedLeak," "Silver Fox"), and specific regulatory frameworks (CVSS scores, CTEM, REIS). The MD cites "800 billion annually in health care costs." The Vannevar Bush commenter cites "MIMIC-CXR [~377K chest X-rays with reports], CheXpert [~224K chest X-rays], NIH Chest X-ray [112K images]." These numbers are not decorative — they signal that the writer has done homework and is not speaking in generalities.

**Analogies are used to translate technical complexity.** The patient records MD compares his proposal to "financial open-banking standards" and says "Health care should function more like retail or banking." The Vannevar Bush commenter compares the need for medical image repositories to "pre-ImageNet challenges in computer vision." These analogies are not dumbed-down explanations — they are bridges between the writer's domain expertise and the policymaker's need to understand why something matters.

**Values appear, but they are grounded, not abstract.** The clinical psychologist invokes SAMHSA's definition of recovery not as a rhetorical flourish but as a specific clinical standard that AI tools are failing to meet. The physical therapist writes "Trust among clinicians, patients, and institutions is the currency that I believe will determine whether AI becomes a positive transformative force in healthcare or an overpromised one." The word "currency" is a deliberate metaphor, and the phrase "I believe" is a small but important hedge — it marks this as professional judgment, not established fact.

**Lists are used strategically, not as a crutch.** Several comments use bullet points or numbered lists for recommendations, but they are embedded within prose that provides context. The lists feel like summaries of arguments already made, not substitutes for argument. The MD's policy proposals are numbered and bulleted but each is preceded by a brief explanatory sentence. The physical therapist's workforce recommendations are bulleted but introduced with a sentence that explains the principle behind them.

**What to avoid**: Do not construct arguments that move in a perfectly linear A→B→C→conclusion pattern without any digression, qualification, or moment of complexity. Real expert writers circle back, add caveats, and occasionally make a point that slightly complicates their own argument. The clinical psychologist notes that AI "is often proposed as a potential solution" before cautioning against it — acknowledging the appeal of the opposing view before pushing back is a human argumentative move that AI writing tends to skip.

---

## 4. Language Characteristics

**Vocabulary is high but not performatively academic.** These writers use technical terminology when it is the precise term for what they mean — "LLMs," "HIPAA," "FHIR APIs," "CTEM," "agentic AI," "prompt injection," "photoplethysmography" — but they do not use jargon to signal sophistication. When a plain word works, they use it. The MD writes "The answer lies in placing a data strip on a patients Medicare or insurance card." No jargon. No hedging. Just a direct, slightly blunt statement of a solution.

**Sentence complexity varies meaningfully.** Long sentences tend to carry qualifications, context, or multi-part recommendations. Short sentences tend to carry conclusions, warnings, or moments of emphasis. The clinical psychologist writes: "AI systems, regardless of sophistication, do not constitute human relationships." Six words after the comma. That brevity is intentional — it marks the sentence as a settled professional conclusion, not an open question. The cybersecurity writer uses a similar technique: "A policy that relies on human intervention for AI-driven threats is a policy that accepts failure." The parallel structure ("a policy that... is a policy that...") gives it rhetorical force without being flowery.

**Hedging is present but calibrated.** These are expert writers who know the difference between what is established and what is their professional judgment. "At present, there is insufficient evidence to justify AI as a substitute for trained mental health professionals" — "at present" is doing real work, acknowledging that this could change. "I believe will determine" — the "I believe" marks it as judgment. "Past analysis by myself suggest 800 billion annually" — the slightly awkward phrasing ("by myself") and the use of "suggest" rather than "shows" is actually a credibility marker, not a weakness. It signals the writer knows the difference between their own estimate and peer-reviewed data.

**Imperfect grammar appears occasionally and authentically.** The MD writes "a patients Medicare or insurance card" (missing apostrophe). He writes "past analysis by myself suggest" (subject-verb disagreement). These are not errors that undermine the argument — they are the kind of small mistakes a busy physician makes when writing a comment between patients. They are humanizing without being distracting.

**What to avoid**: Do not use words like "crucial," "vital," "paramount," "it is imperative that," "robust framework," or "holistic approach" unless they appear in the original samples. These are AI vocabulary tells. This voice group uses "meaningful" (the clinical psychologist uses it twice), "foundational," and "durable" — but sparingly and in specific contexts. Also avoid the construction "not only... but also" — it appears nowhere in these samples and reads as AI-generated connective tissue.

---

## 5. Distinctive Features and Human-Sounding Guidance

**The voice is specific in ways that cannot be faked generically.** The cybersecurity writer names specific exploits. The MD names his practice location and professional association. The physical therapist names her title and credentials at the end. The Vannevar Bush commenter cites a specific historical document from 1945 and connects it to a specific current milestone (500 million records exchanged via TEFCA). This specificity is the single most important feature of this voice. Generic statements — "AI poses risks to patients" — are not how these writers operate. They say *which* risks, *which* patients, *which* regulatory gap, *which* metric is missing.

**The writers show awareness of counterarguments without dwelling on them.** The physical therapist writes "Rigorous governance and workforce preparation are not barriers to adoption yet they are prerequisites for durable progress." This sentence exists because she knows someone will argue that governance slows innovation — she addresses that objection in a single sentence and moves on. The clinical psychologist acknowledges "AI is often proposed as a potential solution" before explaining why that framing is insufficient. This is a human argumentative move: acknowledging the strongest version of the opposing view rather than ignoring it.

**Closing statements are purposeful, not formulaic.** The clinical psychologist ends with "By grounding policy in science and clinical expertise, HHS can help ensure that innovation in AI supports, rather than undermines, ethical and effective healthcare." The physical therapist ends with "Trust among clinicians, patients, and institutions is the currency that I believe will determine whether AI becomes a positive transformative force in healthcare or an overpromised one." The MD ends with "This restores medicine to its proper relationship: patient and doctor first, institutions second." These closings are not summaries — they are statements of value that crystallize what the writer actually cares about. They feel earned because the argument that precedes them is specific enough to support them.

**The voice carries mild but real frustration in places.** "This could have been done twenty years ago." "Manual authorization should be the exception." "A policy that relies on human intervention for AI-driven threats is a policy that accepts failure." These are not angry statements — they are the controlled expressions of professionals who have watched a problem persist longer than it should have. This mild exasperation is a human quality that AI writing tends to smooth away into neutral professional language.

## Summary Guidance for Writers Mimicking This Voice

To write in this voice without sounding like AI:

1. **Lead with a specific credential or specific fact, not a general statement of purpose.** The reader should know within two sentences who you are and what specific vantage point you're writing from.

2. **Use numbers and named sources, but only ones you can actually defend.** Approximate numbers with qualifiers ("roughly," "approximately," "past analysis suggests") are more credible than round numbers stated as fact.

3. **Vary sentence length deliberately.** Use short sentences for conclusions and warnings. Use longer sentences for qualifications and context. Never write three sentences of the same length in a row.

4. **Include one moment of mild frustration or personal investment.** Not a rant — one sentence that shows this person has been thinking about this problem for a while and has feelings about it.

5. **Use analogies from adjacent fields** (banking, computer vision, retail) to explain why a healthcare problem matters in terms policymakers can grasp.

6. **Hedge your professional judgments explicitly.** "I believe," "at present," "in my clinical experience" — these phrases mark the difference between established evidence and expert opinion, and using them correctly signals genuine expertise.

7. **Let one small imperfection stand.** A slightly awkward phrase, a missing word, an unconventional punctuation choice. Not enough to confuse the reader — just enough to signal a human wrote this between other obligations.

8. **End with a value statement, not a summary.** The last sentence should say what you actually believe, not recap what you already said.

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.
