# Writing Style Analysis: "Individual Consumer — Medium" Voice

## 1. Tone and Formality

This group occupies a genuinely mixed register — neither fully professional nor casually personal. The tone is **engaged and purposeful**, often carrying a sense of personal investment without tipping into emotional pleading. Several writers identify their credentials or personal circumstances early ("As a licensed clinical psychologist," "I am a high-functioning American on SSI"), which grounds their authority while keeping the voice human and situated. The formality fluctuates noticeably *within* individual comments: a writer might open with careful bureaucratic framing ("I respectfully submit this response") and then shift into more direct, even blunt language ("AI governance that prioritizes transparency without enforceable contestability will fail in practice"). This tonal inconsistency is not a flaw — it's a signature of real people who have strong opinions but are also trying to be taken seriously by a federal agency. There's occasional frustration leaking through, particularly around repeated policy failures ("LET'S NOT REPEAT THAT MISTAKE THIS TIME"), but it's generally controlled rather than venting.

## 2. Opening Patterns

These comments tend to open in one of three ways: **credential-first** ("As a licensed clinical psychologist, I am writing to you..."), **procedural acknowledgment** ("I submit this response to the Request for Information..."), or **direct assertion of a problem or recommendation** ("AI in clinical care will support Observation Grouping..."). Notably, very few of these comments open with a broad philosophical statement about AI in general — they tend to locate themselves quickly in a specific role, concern, or domain. This is a meaningful human marker: real commenters know what they came to say and get to it relatively fast, even if their framing is sometimes a bit clunky or over-formal. Some comments skip pleasantries entirely and launch straight into bullet points or technical references, which reads as someone who has thought about this a lot and is more focused on getting the content down than on crafting a polished introduction.

## 3. Argument Structure

The dominant argumentative mode is **logical reasoning layered over personal or professional experience**, rather than pure data citation or pure storytelling. These writers build cases through a combination of: identifying a structural problem ("Large provider entities control access to diagnostic data..."), asserting a principle ("Governance must reflect that by requiring durable decision provenance"), and then offering specific, often bulleted recommendations. The bullet-point structure appears frequently and functions as a kind of intellectual shorthand — it signals someone who thinks in systems and categories, not someone crafting a literary essay. Anecdote appears occasionally but briefly: the SSI commenter's personal situation is stated in two sentences and immediately pivoted into a policy ask. There's limited use of citations or peer-reviewed evidence in most comments; instead, authority comes from professional identity, domain knowledge, and specificity of recommendation. Arguments are generally **incomplete in interesting ways** — they don't always anticipate counterarguments or hedge extensively, which is realistic for individual commenters who are making a case rather than writing a policy brief.

## 4. Language Characteristics

Vocabulary ranges from **moderately technical to highly specialized**, depending on the writer's domain. Healthcare and policy jargon appears frequently and without apology — NEMSIS, NFIRS, NIBRS, FHIR, HITECH, interoperability, contestability, provenance — but it's used functionally rather than decoratively. These writers are not trying to sound smart; they already are, and they're writing for an audience they assume shares their vocabulary. Sentence structure is notably **uneven**: some sentences are long and clause-heavy ("Because this is an RFI rather than a rulemaking action, its principal value is agenda-setting: it defines which governance dimensions will be treated as central as HHS explores policy, program, and operational levers"), while others are short and declarative ("My primary recommendation is simple"). This variation is organic, not stylistically managed. Plain language and technical language coexist in the same comment without apparent tension. Grammar is generally correct but occasionally informal or slightly rushed — a missing article here, an abrupt transition there — which adds authenticity.

## 5. Distinctive Features and Human-Sounding Guidance

**What makes this voice distinctive:** These comments feel like they were written by people who have been thinking about their specific issue for a long time and finally have a formal venue to say it. There's a quality of **compressed expertise** — a lot of knowledge packed into relatively short comments, sometimes without full explanation because the writer assumes the reader can follow. The parenthetical aside is a recurring feature: "(I would like to see AI eliminated for this use but that's not the real world)" — this is a deeply human move, the writer checking their own idealism mid-sentence. Similarly, the all-caps outburst in the behavioral health comment ("LET'S NOT REPEAT THAT MISTAKE THIS TIME") is the kind of thing a real person does when they've been frustrated by the same pattern for years.

**How to write in this voice without sounding like AI:**

- **Use uneven sentence length deliberately.** Short declarative sentences should interrupt longer analytical ones. Don't smooth this out.
- **Let credentials do quiet work.** Mention your role or situation once, early, and then move on. Don't keep reminding the reader who you are.
- **Include one moment of hedging or self-correction.** Real people in this group occasionally acknowledge a limitation ("that's not the real world"), express a preference they know is impractical, or note that their view is partial. AI tends to project confidence uniformly — this voice doesn't.
- **Be specific about the domain, vague about the solution.** These writers know their problem in granular detail but often leave the "how exactly" somewhat open, deferring to the agency. Avoid the AI tendency to provide perfectly complete, symmetrical recommendations.
- **Don't over-explain transitions.** These comments often jump from one point to the next without signposting ("First... Second... Finally..."). The logic is implied, not scaffolded.
- **Avoid balanced "on one hand / on the other hand" framing.** This voice has a point of view and argues it. Artificial balance reads as AI hedging.
- **Let bullet points be imperfect.** Real bulleted lists in this group are sometimes inconsistently formatted, mix noun phrases with full sentences, or include one item that's slightly off-topic. Perfect parallel structure is an AI tell.
- **Ground abstraction in a specific consequence.** Instead of "AI poses risks to patient safety," write "when AI influences eligibility decisions, patients can't appeal what they can't see." The move from abstract principle to concrete mechanism is characteristic of this group and hard for AI to fake naturally.

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.

### Example 2 (Source: HHS-ONC-2026-0001-0013)
> Department of Health and Human Services
Office of the Deputy Secretary; ASTP/ONC
I submit this response to the Request for Information on accelerating the adoption and use of artificial intelligence as part of clinical care.
Because this is an RFI rather than a rulemaking action, its principal value is agenda-setting: it defines which governance dimensions will be treated as central as HHS explores policy, program, and operational levers.
My primary recommendation is simple: AI governance that prioritizes transparency without enforceable contestability will fail in practice. Explanations, model cards, and “human-in-the-loop” language do not produce accountability unless affected parties (patients and clinicians) can compel review, correction, and repair within defined time bounds.
Treat AI as a source of exposure, not a neutral tool. When AI influences clinical action, eligibility, documentation, or resource allocation, it becomes part of the causal chain of harm. Governance must reflect that by requiring durable decision provenance and enforceable reversal pathways.
Minimum governance requirements HHS should advance:
• Decision provenance as a default output. AI-influenced actions should generate a durable record: inputs, model/version, confidence/uncertainty, downstream action taken, and accountable authority.
• Contestability with binding timelines. Create standard pathways for clinicians and patients to contest AI-driven outcomes, with deadlines and escalation.
• Override and rollback as real control surfaces. “Human oversight” should be defined as a functional override and rollback capability—not merely a nominal review step.
• Liability clarity and anti-evasion design. Avoid governance frameworks that allow responsibility to be laundered through vendor disclaimers or performative “human review” while the system design prevents meaningful intervention.
Accelerating adoption without enforceable remedy externalizes risk onto clinicians and patients and undermines trust. The question is not whether an AI system can explain itself; it is whether harm can be forced to matter through correction, repair, and rollback.
Independent researcher and systems designer (Chicago, IL)
