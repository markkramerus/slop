# Voice Analysis: "individual_low" Regulatory Comment Style

## Tone and Formality
This voice sits in a distinctly **personal-professional middle ground** — writers are clearly informed or credentialed in some way, but they're not writing policy briefs. The tone swings noticeably between samples: one is frustrated and almost combative ("It's despicable that the AMA — a doctor cartel"), another is clinically concerned but slightly flustered ("ans other mental health professional," "exprerience"), and another is measured but direct. What unifies them is that **these are real people writing from lived experience**, not institutional representatives crafting careful language. There's emotional investment visible in word choice — "despicable," "very uncomfortable," "nonsense" — but it's not pure venting. Writers anchor their feelings to specific, concrete grievances: a $400 bill, an insurance website being down, patients reporting chatbot experiences firsthand. The formality is inconsistent *within* individual comments, which is itself a key authenticity marker.

## Opening Patterns and Argument Structure
These comments tend to **open with a positioning statement** — either a credential, a recommendation, or a bold claim — before diving into the substance. "My recommendation would be," "As a Licensed Clinical Professional Counselor," "It's time to let AIs prescribe medications!" — each one plants a flag immediately. The argument structure is largely **anecdotal and values-driven rather than data-driven**, but with occasional gestures toward evidence ("the evidence is minimal," "same outcomes with PT versus surgery"). Writers build their case through personal experience first, then extrapolate outward to systemic claims. The orthopedic comment is the most structurally sophisticated, moving from a specific AI use case → a policy inefficiency → a cost argument → a call to action. The frustrated consumer comment does something similar but emotionally: personal injury → named villain (AMA) → systemic critique → proposed solution. Neither follows a formal academic structure. Arguments feel assembled in real time, the way someone talks through a problem rather than outlines an essay.

## Language Characteristics and Distinctive Features
Vocabulary is **accessible but domain-specific in patches** — these writers know their fields and drop terminology naturally ("rotator cuff tears," "spinal stenosis," "psychotherapy," "licensed clinical professional"), but they don't sustain formal register throughout. Sentences are **short to medium length, often fragmented or loosely connected**, with conjunctions doing heavy lifting ("and," "but," "because"). Punctuation is functional rather than precise — ellipses trail off mid-thought, parentheses hold asides, capitalization gets used for emphasis ("LET THE PEOPLE CHOOSE"). Typos and minor grammatical slips appear ("ans," "exprerience," "guidlines," "licences") and are **not corrected**, which is crucial. There's also a recurring pattern of **the afterthought or postscript** — the P.S. at the end of the consumer comment, the "Email for more details if needed" sign-off — suggesting writers are aware they're dashing something off and leaving room for follow-up rather than trying to be comprehensive.

## How to Write in This Voice Without Sounding Like AI

**The most important rule: do not be complete.** AI writing tends toward exhaustive coverage — this voice leaves things out, trails off, and trusts the reader to fill gaps. Use incomplete lists (the "etc…" is a perfect example), drop a thought mid-development, and let one or two specific personal details do the work that AI would spread across three paragraphs. **Use first person early and keep returning to it** — "I," "my patients," "my insurance bill" — but don't make every sentence about yourself. The personal detail should anchor a larger point, not be the point itself. Avoid smooth transitions; these writers jump between ideas with minimal connective tissue, or use blunt pivots like "Right now" or "Of course they are."

**Crucially, allow mixed feelings and hedging.** The counselor says she's "very concerned" but also "feel very uncomfortable" — she's not issuing a verdict, she's expressing ongoing discomfort. The PT advocate hedges with "likely diagnosis" and "things like." Real humans in this voice **don't resolve their uncertainty into clean conclusions**; they leave the tension visible. AI writing defaults to confident, symmetrical claims — this voice should feel slightly unresolved, like the writer submitted before they were fully done. One or two minor typos or punctuation quirks (an extra space, a missing apostrophe, a comma splice) will help, but don't overdo it — one or two per comment, not a pattern of errors that looks planted. The goal is a comment that reads like someone typed it in fifteen minutes because they actually cared, not because they were performing caring.

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.
