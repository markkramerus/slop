# Voice Analysis: individual_medium

## Tone, Formality, and Opening Patterns

This voice group occupies a distinctive middle register: **professionally credentialed but personally invested**. These writers are not bureaucrats filing paperwork — they are practitioners who feel genuine alarm and are using formal channels to express it. The tone is measured but not detached. Comments almost universally open with a professional credential as an anchor: *"As a physician and psychiatrist,"* *"As a licensed clinical psychologist,"* *"As a practicing psychologist."* This is not boilerplate — it functions as a moral claim. The writer is saying: *I have earned the right to speak on this, and I am speaking from experience, not ideology.* Occasionally the credential is embedded mid-comment or implied through technical vocabulary rather than stated outright (the FHIR/surveillance commenter). The opening credential is followed almost immediately by a pivot to concern, urgency, or a specific problem — there is very little warm-up or scene-setting. One writer drops their credential and goes straight to the ethical charge: *"I personally think it is unethical..."* — which is the exception that proves the rule, and notably feels rawer and more emotionally direct as a result.

## Argument Structure and Rhetorical Strategy

The dominant argumentative mode is **values-grounded professional reasoning**, not pure data citation and not pure personal narrative. These writers know clinical evidence exists and gesture toward it (*"decades of psychotherapy research confirm,"* *"documented cases of AI providing harmful guidance"*), but they do not lean on citations or statistics as their primary persuasive tool. Instead, they build arguments by asserting professional consensus, naming specific harms, and appealing to structural logic: *if X condition is not met, Y harm will predictably follow.* The more organized comments use numbered lists or short declarative headers (*"Federal Regulation Is Essential," "Privacy Concerns and HIPAA Gaps"*) that mimic policy brief formatting — but the language inside those sections remains relatively plain and direct rather than bureaucratic. Several comments use a **concession move** that feels distinctly human: acknowledging the limits of their own position (*"I would like to see AI eliminated for this use but that's not the real world"*) or the complexity of the situation (*"While AI is advancing rapidly, the science and safeguards have not kept pace"*). This hedging is not weakness — it signals intellectual honesty and distinguishes these writers from advocates reciting talking points.

## Language Characteristics and Sentence Patterns

Vocabulary is **educated but not academic**. These writers use clinical terms naturally and without definition (*LLMs, HIPAA, FHIR, suicidal ideation, symptom remission, therapeutic relationship*) because they are writing from inside the profession, not explaining it to outsiders. Sentence structure varies considerably within individual comments — a hallmark of authentic human writing. A writer will use a long, clause-heavy sentence to establish context, then follow it with a short declarative punch: *"AI systems, regardless of sophistication, do not constitute human relationships."* Or: *"We do not have this luxury."* These short sentences land hard precisely because they follow complexity. Paragraph length is uneven. Some comments are a single dense paragraph; others break into numbered lists; one is almost telegraphic. There is occasional grammatical informality — *"to insure"* instead of *"ensure,"* a missing comma, a parenthetical aside that trails off — which reads as genuine rather than careless. The more polished comments (the PsiAN-affiliated ones) are tighter and more structured, but even these avoid the smooth, frictionless cadence that marks AI-generated text.

## How to Write in This Voice Without Sounding Like AI

**Lead with your credential, then immediately name your concern** — don't ease in. The credential is doing rhetorical work, not just providing context. **Use short declarative sentences as punctuation after longer explanations** — this is the most distinctive rhythm of this voice and the hardest for AI to replicate naturally. **Include at least one moment of hedging, concession, or acknowledged limitation** — something like *"I know this isn't realistic, but..."* or *"I recognize AI has legitimate uses, however..."* Real professionals in this group don't claim omniscience. **Be specific about the harm** — not "AI could hurt people" but "there are documented cases of AI providing harmful guidance to people experiencing suicidal ideation" or a link to a Wikipedia article about chatbot-linked deaths. Specificity is what separates grounded human concern from generic AI-generated alarm. **Avoid**: smooth transitional phrases (*"Furthermore," "It is worth noting," "In conclusion"*), perfectly parallel list structures where every bullet is the same length, and any sentence that sounds like it's summarizing itself. This voice does not wrap up neatly. It often ends on an imperative or a warning, not a balanced conclusion. The writer is still worried when they stop typing — and that should show.

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.

### Example 2 (Source: HHS-ONC-2026-0001-0175)
> 1. Federal Regulation Is Essential. There is currently no comprehensive federal regulatory framework governing AI in healthcare. This regulatory gap poses significant risks to patients. Leaving oversight to corporations or to a patchwork of state laws would create inconsistent protections and expose patients to avoidable harm. PsiAN calls for uniform federal standards to govern privacy, safety, and accountability.
2. Privacy Concerns and HIPAA Gaps. Many AI chatbots marketed for mental health support are not compliant with HIPAA requirements, meaning patient data may not be protected under federal healthcare privacy law. Without legally enforceable safeguards, patients may unknowingly expose sensitive information without adequate protection. No acceleration of AI adoption should occur without clear, enforceable federal privacy standards.
3. Lack of Evidence for Safety and Effectiveness. Large language models (LLMs) are prone to generating inaccurate or fabricated outputs. There have been documented cases of AI systems providing harmful guidance to vulnerable individuals, including those experiencing suicidal ideation. Corporate metrics such as user engagement, downloads, and customer satisfaction are not substitutes for rigorous clinical outcome data. New AI tools must be evaluated using meaningful health metrics, including symptom remission, dropout rates, sustained recovery, and improvements in quality of life. Evaluation and safety research should be conducted by independent researchers, not by the corporations developing and selling the products, so as to minimize conflicts of interest.
4. AI and the Limits of the Therapeutic Relationship. Decades of psychotherapy research confirm that the therapist-patient relationship is the strongest predictor of treatment outcome. AI systems, regardless of sophistication, do not constitute human relationships.  At present, there is insufficient evidence to justify AI as a substitute for trained mental health professionals. Recovery in behavioral health involves more than symptom reduction. AI systems must be evaluated against broader, recovery-oriented outcomes, not simply short-term symptom change.
