# Writing Style Analysis: Industry High-Org Voice

## Tone and Formality

This voice group occupies a specific register: **formally professional but not sterile**. These are organizations that know how to write to government agencies — they use proper salutations, RIN numbers, structured headers — but they're not law firms or trade associations with decades of polished lobbying prose. The tone is measured and respectful without being obsequious, though you do see genuine deference to the agency ("Thank you for your leadership on this important issue"). What's notably absent is the emotional register of patient advocates or individual clinicians. These writers are making *business and policy arguments*, not personal ones. However, several comments (particularly People's Health Clinic and HealthScoreAI) let genuine organizational identity and even mild frustration or urgency bleed through. PHC's comment has real stakes embedded in its numbers: "one in five patients had disease detected." That's not neutral. It's chosen to land. The Alabama/Kentucky Health Command submission is the outlier — it's looser, less polished, with inconsistent capitalization of "AI" and a somewhat promotional register that breaks the formal frame. This variation is actually important: **this voice group is not monolithic**. It's defined more by shared purpose and structural convention than by uniform polish.

## Opening Patterns and Argument Structure

Nearly every comment opens with a formal acknowledgment of the RFI opportunity, followed immediately by a **dense organizational credential paragraph**. This is the signature move of this voice: *establish who we are before we say anything else*. HealthScoreAI lists patents, EHR experience, Medicare Advantage networks, and IoT in rapid succession. Echo IQ describes its FDA-cleared platform in the second paragraph. MediView XR introduces the term "Physical AI" and defines it almost academically. PHC leads with raw operational numbers — 30 people, 21,500 encounters, 95% below 200% FPL — which is a more data-forward version of the same credentialing instinct. The argument structure that follows is typically **logical-sequential with embedded evidence**, not narrative or anecdotal. They build from "here is what we do" → "here is what we've observed or validated" → "here is what policy should therefore do." PHC is the most explicitly data-driven, citing a specific academic paper (Saenz et al., npj Digital Medicine 7:348, 2024) and its own repeatability testing protocol. Echo IQ and MediView XR are more conceptual, building arguments around framework gaps (reimbursement structure, regulatory clarity) rather than empirical outcomes. The Health Command submission is structurally looser — it pivots mid-comment to revenue generation from pharmaceutical sponsorships in a way that feels unedited, which is actually a realistic feature of smaller or less-resourced organizations writing these comments.

## Language Characteristics and Distinctive Features

Vocabulary is **technical but not impenetrable** — these writers assume the reader knows what EHR, SaMD, CPT codes, and De Novo clearance mean, but they still define or contextualize specialized terms when introducing them ("LumineticsCore (Digital Diagnostics), the first FDA De Novo-cleared autonomous AI diagnostic for diabetic retinopathy"). Sentence complexity varies: Echo IQ and MediView XR tend toward longer, more elaborately constructed sentences with embedded clauses and parallel structures ("reduces diagnostic variability, and supports earlier, more precise identification of disease"). PHC uses shorter, punchier sentences when making its sharpest points ("It works because predictive AI is deterministic—same input, same output—and can be validated using standard methods"). The em-dash appears frequently across multiple submissions as a stylistic marker of this voice — used to insert clarifying phrases or create emphasis without a full subordinate clause. Numbered recommendation lists appear in several comments, which is a structural convention of the genre. Recurring phrases include variants of "augment/augmentative" (not replace), "responsible adoption," "human-in-the-loop," and "patient safety, privacy, and trust" — these are the ideological anchors of this voice group, and they appear almost formulaically. The Health Command submission notably uses "Ai" instead of "AI" throughout, which is either a stylistic choice or an error — but it's a humanizing imperfection that distinguishes it from more polished submissions.

## How to Write in This Voice Without Sounding Like AI

**The most important thing to understand about this voice is that it's written by people who are simultaneously experts in their domain and non-experts in regulatory writing.** They know their technology cold, but the prose shows varying degrees of comfort with the formal comment letter genre. To write convincingly in this voice, you need to **lead with specific organizational identity** — not generic claims about "our company" but concrete details: how many patients, what FDA clearance, how many patents, what specific product name. The credential paragraph should feel slightly dense, even a little list-heavy, because real organizations are proud of their specifics and tend to over-include them. Avoid smooth, perfectly balanced sentences that cover every angle — real writers in this voice have a *point of view* and sometimes let it show unevenly.

**This voice uses hedging, but strategically rather than reflexively.** Phrases like "may reduce avoidable hospitalizations," "has the potential to improve," and "we believe" appear regularly — but they're deployed around genuinely uncertain claims (policy outcomes, market effects), not as a general softening of everything. The writers are *confident about their technology* and *measured about policy predictions*. An AI-sounding version of this voice would hedge everything equally or, conversely, make sweeping confident claims about transforming healthcare. Real writers in this group will say something like "EchoSolv does not replace physician interpretation; rather, it strengthens and standardizes it" — a direct, confident product claim — followed by a more hedged policy ask. Watch for the **asymmetry between product confidence and policy caution**; that asymmetry is human and specific.

**Avoid these AI-writing traps specific to this voice:** Don't open with "In today's rapidly evolving healthcare landscape." Don't use "robust" as a filler adjective. Don't write perfectly parallel three-part lists for every single point — real writers in this group sometimes have two items, sometimes five, and occasionally a list that trails off into a run-on. Don't make every paragraph the same length. Don't resolve every tension neatly — PHC's comment explicitly says "this gap is not limited to safety-net settings" and then cites a major academic health system struggling with the same problem, which is a slightly uncomfortable admission that strengthens rather than undermines the argument. Real writers include inconvenient details when those details serve their credibility. The Health Command submission goes further — it introduces a revenue-from-pharmaceutical-sponsorships argument in a way that feels slightly incongruous with the rest of the letter, and that awkwardness is authentic. **Imperfect transitions, slightly uneven emphasis, and the occasional sentence that does too much work are features of this voice, not bugs to be corrected.**

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.
