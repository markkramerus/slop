# Writing Style Analysis: Industry-Medium-Org Voice

## Tone, Formality, and Opening Patterns

This voice occupies a specific professional register that sits between formal academic writing and corporate marketing — call it **institutional-technical professional**. It is measured, confident, and organizational rather than personal, but it never becomes warm or conversational. Comments almost always open by identifying the submitting organization and anchoring credibility immediately: "The University of Kansas Health System is pleased to provide feedback," "GeneDx appreciates the opportunity," "SANCIAN LLC submits the attached response...Drawing on over 15 years of experience." This is a consistent structural tic — establish who you are, establish why you have standing, then make your argument. The tone avoids emotional appeals almost entirely. There is no outrage, no urgency-for-urgency's-sake, and no personal storytelling. When values appear ("patient trust," "patient safety," "equity and access"), they are invoked briefly and functionally, as policy anchors rather than emotional appeals. Crucially, this voice is **confident but not aggressive** — it recommends, encourages, and supports rather than demands or condemns.

## Argument Structure and Language Characteristics

The argument structure is consistently **diagnostic-then-prescriptive**: identify a systemic problem with some specificity, then offer a concrete recommendation, often with regulatory citation. Scientix.ai does this most clearly — naming the problem ("compounding cost of integrating fragmented health IT systems"), then immediately citing specific regulatory hooks (45 C.F.R. Parts 160, 164; 42 C.F.R. § 422.101(c)). This pattern repeats across samples at varying levels of technical depth. The vocabulary is specialized but not academic — terms like "lifecycle governance," "post-deployment drift," "socio-technical system," "deterministic validation layers," and "human-in-the-loop" appear naturally, as working vocabulary rather than performed expertise. Sentences tend toward **moderate-to-high complexity** — compound structures with embedded clauses — but writers break them up with bullet points, numbered lists, and short declarative sentences for emphasis. Notice how InterSystems uses a clean three-part bulleted list after a longer framing paragraph. This rhythm — complex framing, then punchy specifics — is characteristic of the voice.

## Distinctive Features and What Makes It Feel Human

What distinguishes this voice from generic AI-sounding regulatory prose is a combination of **operational specificity and acknowledged limitation**. These writers name their own vantage point honestly and with some modesty: "Drawing on our experience building data infrastructure for regulated clinical environments," "Based on our experience executing IT, data, and analytics initiatives." They don't claim universal authority — they claim particular experience. There is also a recurring pattern of **naming the gap between theory and practice** as the central problem: the gap between documented workflows and how clinical staff actually operate, the disconnect between how healthcare leaders conceptualize AI and what it actually requires, governance approaches that focus only on pre-deployment validation being insufficient for post-deployment reality. This is a grounded, slightly skeptical intelligence that has seen implementation fail and wants to say so professionally. The SANCIAN comment does this most sharply: "HHS should treat AI not as technology to be adopted, but as care infrastructure to be governed" — a reframe that sounds like someone who has watched technology-first approaches fail repeatedly.

## How to Write in This Voice Without Sounding Like AI

To write in this voice convincingly, **lead with organizational identity and a specific operational claim, not a broad thesis**. Don't open with "AI represents a transformative opportunity" — that's generic. Open with who you are and what you've actually done: "X organization submits these comments drawing on our experience with Y specific thing." Then name one or two concrete problems with enough technical texture that they feel observed rather than generated — reference a specific regulatory citation, a workflow failure mode, or an implementation gap that has a name. **Avoid sweeping confident claims** ("AI will revolutionize healthcare") and instead use the hedged-but-purposeful language of experienced practitioners: "can reduce," "should consider," "we recommend," "evidence suggests." This voice uses hedging not from uncertainty but from professional discipline — it knows overclaiming undermines credibility. **Do not over-list.** AI writing defaults to exhaustive bullet points; this voice uses lists selectively, usually three to four items, after a paragraph that earns them. Finally, resist the urge to conclude with a grand vision statement. These comments end practically — "We stand ready to support HHS," "GeneDx welcomes continued engagement" — which is human and organizational, not inspirational. The voice sounds human because it sounds like it has a specific job to do and is doing it carefully, not because it is trying to impress anyone.

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.

### Example 2 (Source: HHS-ONC-2026-0001-0011)
> These comments are submitted in alignment with the HHS Artificial Intelligence Strategy and respond directly to the ten questions outlined in this RFI. The central observation is that effective and responsible adoption of AI in clinical care is constrained less by algorithmic performance than by workflow integration, lifecycle governance, and enforceable data stewardship across real-world clinical environments.
The attached submission treats clinical AI as a socio-technical system. Once deployed, AI systems reshape clinical workflows, redistribute accountability, and generate compounding learning from operational data, outcomes, and process metadata. Governance approaches that focus only on privacy or pre-deployment validation are insufficient to manage post-deployment drift, vendor updates, subcontractor access, and long-term learning accumulation.
Accordingly, the attached responses map each RFI question to relevant HHS StaffDiv and OpDiv equities, including CMS, ASTP/ONC, OCR, CIO/ONS, OGA, and acquisition functions. The submission highlights acquisition and contracting as the primary control plane for enforceable safeguards, and addresses interoperability, cybersecurity, international alignment, and patient trust as integrated components of clinical AI adoption.

### Example 3 (Source: HHS-ONC-2026-0001-0125)
> Scientix.ai Inc. submits the attached response to selected questions in this RFI, drawing on our experience building data infrastructure for regulated clinical environments. Our central observation is that the primary barriers to AI adoption in healthcare are infrastructural, not algorithmic — specifically, the compounding cost of integrating fragmented health IT systems, the persistent gap between documented workflows and how clinical staff actually operate, and the absence of deterministic validation layers that AI requires to earn clinician trust. We recommend that HHS modernize HIPAA data governance rules for AI-era requirements (45 C.F.R. We also identify the executive digital fluency gap — the disconnect between how healthcare leaders conceptualize AI and what it actually requires to succeed — as the most overlooked administrative hurdle to adoption. Our full response with supporting evidence is attached.
