# Writing Style Analysis: "unknown-low-org" Voice

## Overview

This voice group is strikingly minimalist — these are **organizational cover letter submissions**, not substantive comments themselves. The actual content is in attachments; what we're analyzing is the transmittal language. That context is everything for understanding the style.

---

## Detailed Analysis

**Tone and Formality** — This voice is **formally courteous but deliberately thin**. There's no warmth, no advocacy posturing, no throat-clearing. The tone sits in a narrow band: professional enough to signal institutional credibility, brief enough to signal that the real work is in the attachment. Notably absent is any emotional register whatsoever — no urgency, no concern, no enthusiasm beyond a single polite phrase ("pleased to provide," "thanks for the opportunity"). It reads like a fax cover sheet written by someone who has submitted regulatory comments dozens of times and sees the transmittal as pure administrative formality.

**Opening Patterns and Argument Structure** — Every comment opens with a direct declaration of what is being submitted and by whom. The structural formula is almost invariant: *[Submission verb] + [document description] + [from/of organization] + [brief regulatory reference]*. There is no hook, no framing argument, no policy position stated in the cover text. The Wolters Kluwer example is the only one that deviates even slightly, adding a single sentence of social courtesy ("Thanks for the opportunity to share our views") — which paradoxically makes it feel the *most* human of the group. There is zero argument structure in the traditional sense because argument is entirely deferred to the attachment.

**Language Characteristics and Distinctive Features** — Vocabulary is plain, functional, and deliberately generic. Sentences are short to medium length, grammatically complete but unadorned. The group uses passive or semi-passive constructions naturally ("Attached are comments," "Please see attached") alongside active constructions ("Spring Health is pleased to provide"). Jargon is minimal in the cover text — organizational names and the regulatory reference ("RFI," "HHS") are the only technical markers. What makes this voice distinctive is its **institutional anonymity by design**: the organization name does the credentialing work, and the prose does nothing to embellish it.

---

## How to Write in This Voice WITHOUT Sounding Like AI

**Keep it structurally skeletal — resist the urge to add.** The single biggest AI trap here is elaboration. An LLM will naturally want to add a sentence about *why* the organization is submitting, what their expertise is, or what the comment covers. This voice doesn't do that. If you find yourself writing a second substantive sentence, cut it. **The human signal here is restraint, not richness.**

**Use slightly varied but formulaic openings.** Real submitters in this category have clearly absorbed a template but apply it loosely. "Please see attached," "Attached are," "Attached is," "[Org] is pleased to provide" — these are the actual variants. Don't invent new framings. Rotate among these naturally. The Wolters Kluwer example shows the one acceptable humanizing addition: a brief, genuine-sounding courtesy phrase ("Thanks for the opportunity") that feels like something a real person typed, not something a policy team drafted.

**Avoid hedging AND avoid sweeping claims equally.** This voice does neither. It makes no claims at all in the cover text. There's no "we believe," no "our extensive research shows," no "we are deeply concerned." The absence of both hedging and confidence is itself the human marker — it's the voice of someone who knows the cover note is not the place for any of that. If you accidentally insert a policy position or a value statement into the cover text, it will immediately sound wrong — either like AI padding or like an amateur submitter.

**Ground it in the specific organizational name and document type.** The one specificity these comments contain is the *who* (named organization) and the *what* (comments, response, attached document). That specificity — "comments of Health Level Seven (HL7) International" rather than "our organization's comments" — is what makes each one feel like a real institutional submission rather than a generic placeholder. Always name the organization fully and reference the specific RFI or regulatory action by name. That's the entire grounding mechanism this voice uses.

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.
