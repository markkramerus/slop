# Writing Style Analysis: "Unknown-Medium-Org" Voice Group

## Tone, Formality, and Overall Character

This voice group occupies a distinctive middle register: more formal and structured than individual citizen comments, but less polished and comprehensive than major healthcare systems or law firms. The tone is **professionally functional rather than rhetorically sophisticated** — these writers know how to address a federal agency and use appropriate framing language ("respectfully submits," "appreciates the opportunity"), but they don't over-invest in diplomatic throat-clearing. The Cyberhill and Hackensack samples lean toward confident institutional voice, while ShiftOS and the deterministic AI comment lean toward **efficient brevity** — they have a point, they make it, they stop. There's a notable absence of emotional warmth or personal storytelling, but also an absence of the exhaustive hedging you see in major association comments. The Michigan Healthcare Freedom comment is the outlier — it carries genuine ideological conviction and uses capitalization ("ZERO") as emphasis, which signals a real person with real frustration rather than a communications department. Critically, this group **does not perform enthusiasm** — phrases like "we commend HHS for this visionary initiative" appear only in the more polished samples and feel slightly stiff when they do.

## Opening Patterns and Argument Structure

These comments almost universally open with **organizational identification before argument** — who they are gets established before what they think. But the identification is functional, not elaborate. Compare ShiftOS ("ShiftOS is a healthcare technology company building AI-powered workforce scheduling software for health systems") to how a major association might spend two paragraphs on membership statistics and history. Medium-org writers give you one or two sentences of credibility-establishment, then move. The Radiology Partners comment is the most developed version of this pattern, using its opening to establish domain authority ("radiology is at the cutting edge of AI technology") before pivoting to recommendations. The argument structure across the group is predominantly **list-based and recommendation-forward** — these organizations came to tell the agency what to do, not to explore the philosophical landscape. The Cyberhill comment uses numbered recommendations with topic sentences; the VALID AI comment uses explicit numbered questions as an organizing frame; the Michigan Healthcare Freedom comment uses a numbered list of explicit requests. This is not accidental — it reflects organizations that have internal decision-making processes and are translating conclusions into regulatory language, not building arguments from scratch in prose.

## Language Characteristics and Distinctive Features

Vocabulary sits at a **technical-professional level without being academic** — terms like "FedRAMP High-authorized," "CPT codes," "MIPS quality measures," and "agentic AI orchestration" appear without definition because the writers assume a knowledgeable audience, but the sentences carrying those terms are usually grammatically straightforward. Sentence complexity is **moderate and uneven** — a long compound sentence establishing context will often be followed by a short declarative. This unevenness is humanizing and characteristic: "AI is no longer experimental in health care and is already embedded across care delivery. Federal policy must therefore evolve." That second sentence is almost blunt. The group also shows **mild hedging and qualification** ("directly applicable," "materially accelerate," "can deliver actionable intelligence") without the excessive qualification of academic writing. One recurring structural quirk: many of these comments **gesture toward an attachment** ("Please see the attached PDF," "Please see comment letter attached") — a distinctly human organizational behavior that signals real workflow, not a self-contained argument. The Michigan Healthcare Freedom comment's use of "ZERO" in caps and the social worker comment's use of "charlatans!" and "scourge" represent the emotional register that occasionally breaks through even in organizationally-framed comments.

## How to Write in This Voice Without Sounding Like AI

**What this voice AVOIDS:** It avoids comprehensive coverage — real medium-org comments pick two to four points and stop, leaving obvious gaps that a thorough AI would fill. It avoids smooth transitions between paragraphs; ideas are often juxtaposed rather than bridged. It avoids abstract framing ("in today's rapidly evolving landscape") and instead grounds claims in specific operational context ("based on our experience serving high-needs Medicaid and Medicare beneficiaries"). It avoids the AI tendency toward symmetrical parallel structure in every sentence. **What makes it feel human:** The organizational self-description is slightly awkward and specific in a way that feels like someone adapted their About page rather than crafting a perfect introduction. There are small redundancies — Radiology Partners says "Radiology Partners, through its affiliated practices" twice in consecutive sentences, which no AI editor would leave in. Confidence is **domain-specific rather than universal** — these writers are sure about their operational experience but appropriately uncertain about policy outcomes. To write in this voice: lead with a one-to-two sentence org ID that sounds slightly like a website bio, make your ask explicit and numbered, use technical vocabulary naturally but don't define it, let sentences vary in length without worrying about flow, and resist the urge to address every dimension of the question. If you find yourself writing a smooth paragraph that covers all sides of an issue, you've drifted into AI territory — cut it to the two points your organization actually cares about and stop there.

## Human Writing Rules

**Every comment generated from this voice profile must sound like a real human wrote it.**
These rules apply regardless of archetype or sophistication level.

### Forbidden patterns (will make the output sound AI-generated)

**Em dashes** — never use `—`. Replace with a comma, period, or parentheses.

**Negative parallelisms**: never write "It's not just X; it's Y" or "Not only X, but also Y" or "It's not merely X, it's Y". These are among the most reliable AI tells.

**AI vocabulary words**: do not use `additionally`, `crucial`, `delve`, `enhance`, `fostering`, `garner`, `highlight` (as verb), `intricate`, `pivotal`, `showcase`, `tapestry` (abstract), `testament`, `underscore` (verb), `vibrant`, `landscape` (abstract), `evolving landscape`, `rich` (figurative).

**Inflated significance**: avoid `serves as a testament to`, `marks a pivotal moment`, `reflects broader trends`, `setting the stage for`, `underscores its importance`, `vital role`, `key turning point`, `evolving landscape`, `enduring legacy`.

**Superficial -ing phrases**: avoid tacking participial phrases onto sentences to add fake depth — e.g., `highlighting the importance of`, `underscoring the need for`, `contributing to`, `fostering collaboration`, `reflecting broader trends`.

**Copula avoidance**: write `is` and `are` — not `serves as`, `stands as`, `functions as`, `represents`, `marks`. If you mean "is", say "is".

**Rule of three**: avoid habitually grouping things into exactly three items. Real people don't think in triads.

**Vague attributions**: never write `experts say`, `observers note`, `some argue`, `industry reports suggest` without a specific name or source.

**Promotional language**: no `boasts`, `nestled`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `renowned`, `in the heart of`, `must-see`.

**Generic positive endings**: no `the future looks bright`, `exciting times lie ahead`, `this represents a step in the right direction`.

**Boldface bullet headers**: don't write lists where each item starts with `**Key Point:** explanation`. Write prose or plain bullets.

**Sycophantic openers**: no `Great question!`, `Certainly!`, `Of course!`, `I hope this helps`, `Let me know if you have questions`.

### Required human qualities

**Vary sentence length.** Mix short punchy sentences with longer ones that take their time. Never write a block of sentences all the same length.

**Have opinions.** Real people react to information. "I genuinely don't understand why they'd do this" is more human than a neutral list of pros and cons.

**Show uncertainty and mixed feelings.** "I'm not sure this is the right approach, but..." or "This concerns me, even though I see why they proposed it" signals a real person thinking.

**Use first person naturally.** `I keep coming back to...`, `What gets me is...`, `My experience has been...` — these read as human.

**Be specific.** Real people cite actual numbers, dates, places, names. Generic claims (`many people are affected`) are AI. Specific claims (`my clinic saw 40 patients last month who couldn't afford this`) are human.

**Let some mess in.** A tangent, a repeated point, a slightly incomplete thought — these are human. Perfect logical structure is a red flag.

**Use simple constructions.** `is`, `are`, `has`, `does` — not elaborate substitutes.

Both privacy and fraud protections are essential for securing our healthcare freedoms as Americans.
As proposed, these are competing priorities. To resolve the conflict, I request the following AI policies be followed.

Abigail Nobel, BSN, RN, MA

### Example 2 (Source: HHS-ONC-2026-0001-0064)
> Comment for Federal Register Submission — RIN 0955-AA13
Cyberhill Partners, LLC., in strategic alliance with ServiceNow, respectfully submits this comment in response to the Department of Health and Human Services' Request for Information on accelerating the adoption of artificial intelligence in clinical care.
As an AI solutions firm with extensive experience deploying secure, governance-compliant agentic AI systems for the Department of Defense and Intelligence Community, Cyberhill brings a differentiated perspective on operationalizing AI in highly regulated environments — directly applicable to the challenges HHS has identified across Regulation, Reimbursement, and Research & Development.
Our attached response details a proposed Clinical Care Intelligence Platform (CCIP) built on ServiceNow's FedRAMP High-authorized, HIPAA-compliant Healthcare and Life Sciences Service Management platform, augmented by Cyberhill's proprietary clinical ontology engineering, knowledge graph architecture, and agentic AI orchestration. This approach delivers explainable, auditable clinical decision support grounded in authoritative medical sources — addressing the trust and transparency gaps that continue to impede adoption.
We wish to highlight several recommendations for HHS consideration:
First, HHS should issue targeted guidance on AI-PHI boundaries under the HIPAA Privacy and Security Rules. AI systems can generate or infer Protected Health Information even from de-identified inputs, and the current regulatory framework does not adequately address this risk. Clear technical safeguard standards would reduce compliance uncertainty and unlock private sector investment.
Second, payment policy reform is essential to align incentives with AI-driven efficiency. New CPT codes for AI-assisted clinical services, inclusion of AI deployment metrics in MIPS quality measures, and shared savings provisions under the Medicare Shared Savings Program would directly accelerate adoption.
Third, HHS should establish certification pathways for non-medical-device clinical AI analogous to existing EHR certification under 45 CFR Part 170, providing the market with clear safety, effectiveness, and interoperability benchmarks.
Fourth, investment in standardized clinical AI evaluation infrastructure — including benchmarking datasets with diverse patient populations, regional AI testing centers, and prize competitions for evaluation methodologies — would materially accelerate responsible development.
Finally, our experience demonstrates that powerful healthcare AI does not require training models on confidential patient data. Privacy-preserving approaches leveraging metadata, aggregations, and synthetic data can deliver actionable intelligence while fully respecting patient privacy.
We appreciate the opportunity to contribute to this critical dialogue and stand ready to engage further as HHS develops policies and programs to advance AI in clinical care.
Respectfully submitted,
